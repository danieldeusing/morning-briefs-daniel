Gemma 4: four sizes, Apache 2.0 — and three months to the first independent board

Google's open model family comes in four variants, and the split is unusually clean: "Effective 2B (E2B), Effective 4B (E4B), 26B Mixture of Experts (MoE), and 31B Dense." The two small ones are built for devices and carry 128K context, the two large ones up to 256K. The license is Apache 2.0 — commercially usable with no special clauses, which is by no means the rule for "open" models (Meta's and Alibaba's licenses attach conditions). Downloadable on Hugging Face, Kaggle, and Ollama, with day-one support in vLLM, llama.cpp, and LiteRT-LM.

What matters here for coding

For an agent chain, what counts isn't parameter count but what the model can do out of the box: Google explicitly cites "native support for function calling, structured JSON output, and native system instructions." That's the difference between a model you have to coax into tool calls through prompt tricks, and one that handles them as its own format. That's exactly where most small open models fail in practice — not on the code, but on a mangled tool call.

blog.google · As of 2026-07-26
Dense vs. MoE: A dense model fires up its entire brain for every word — all 31 billion parameters compute every single time. A Mixture-of-Experts model is instead a firm full of specialists; only two or three of them stand up per word, and the rest keep sleeping. For Gemma 4's 26B MoE, that's roughly 3.8 billion active out of 26 billion parameters. You only have to compute the handful — but you have to keep all of them loaded, because you don't know in advance which specialist will be needed. The result: the MoE answers fast, but it doesn't save you any memory.

Rank 3 among open models — and what that really tells you

The real news of recent days isn't the model — it's its first outside evaluation. On Arena.ai's Text Arena board, Gemma-4-31B now sits at rank 3 among open models and rank 27 overall; the 26B MoE follows at rank 6 among the open ones. Against its predecessor, Gemma-3-27B, that's said to be a jump of roughly 87 points. The timing is notable: the family shipped on April 2, the technical report followed on July 2 — three months passed before the first independent measurement, during which only vendor numbers existed.

arena.ai (X) · As of 2026-07-26
Arena Elo: Not a test, but a vote. Two models answer the same question, a human sees both answers with no name tags and picks the better one — millions of times over, producing the same kind of score used to rank chess players. That measures something quite different from SWE-Bench: does the answer read well, not does the code run. A model can rank high in the Arena because it phrases things nicely, and still fail to patch a real repo. Take the rank as a signal of general usefulness — not as proof of coding skill.
Sourcing, honestly The primary source for the rank is a post from the Arena.ai account on X. It wasn't directly readable today — the fetch ended in HTTP 402, and the usual fxtwitter workaround didn't work either. The rank (3 among open models, 27 overall) is backed by two independent secondary mentions; the exact point values including "+87 points" come from a search-engine snippet of the same post and weren't verified against the original — so the rank is stated firmly here, but the point gap only with "roughly."

In practical terms, a rank of 3 at 31 billion parameters mainly means one thing: the "runs on my own machine" class has closed some of the gap to the cloud frontier again. By Google's own account, the Gemma family has been downloaded over 400 million times — not a quality metric, but it explains why tool integrations for Gemma tend to already exist by the time you need them. Try it Why try it: if you've been running Gemma 3 or Qwen locally as a second opinion, the 31B is the first candidate in months with a real jump — and Apache 2.0 means no licensing footnotes to worry about beforehand.

The numbers — and the hole in the middle

The Gemma 4 Technical Report (Google, submitted July 2, 2026) delivers the figures Google measured itself. Two matter for coding. LiveCodeBench v6: the 31B scores 80.0, the 26B MoE 77.1. Codeforces Elo: 2150 versus 1718 — a gap of over 400 points between the two siblings, showing the dense build is clearly ahead on competitive programming. Add the general numbers: AIME 2026 without tools 89.2, MMLU Pro 85.2.

arxiv.org — Gemma 4 Technical Report · As of 2026-07-26

More interesting than the codegen numbers for agent work is τ²-Bench: 86.4 on the Retail task, 75.0 on Airline. A notable pattern: on the lighter Retail scenario, the 31B edges out the 26B MoE (85.5), but on the harder Airline scenario it actually falls behind it (76.0). Bigger isn't automatically better here — anyone using either as a local tool-calling agent is better off comparing for themselves than reaching for the bigger number.

arxiv.org · As of 2026-07-26
τ²-Bench: A role-play test for agents. The model plays the service rep at a fictional company — process a return, rebook a flight — and has to call real tools, follow house rules, and talk to a simulated customer who doesn't reveal everything at once. Only the final database state is scored. So it doesn't measure code writing, but whether a multi-step task gets carried through to completion within the rules — the skill that decides whether an agent chain succeeds or turns into chaos.

Where Gemma 4 doesn't show up

And now the hole: Gemma 4 has no SWE-Bench Verified score. Not a low one — none at all. Three independent lookups confirm this: the official board at swebench.com, the raw-results repo SWE-bench/experiments on GitHub, and an aggregate last updated, by its own account, in July 2026, listing 104 models. Its verdict is unambiguous: "Neither Gemma 4 nor Gemma-4-31B appears anywhere in the leaderboard data or the full list of 104 evaluated models." For reference, the top of that board: Claude Fable 5 95.0%, Mythos Preview 93.9%, Opus 4.8 88.6%, Opus 4.7 87.6%, Gemini 3.1 Pro 80.6%.

llm-stats.com · github.com/SWE-bench · As of 2026-07-26
A number that's circulating wrong Search for "Gemma 4 SWE-Bench" and you'll still get served percentages anyway — usually 61.4%, occasionally 41.6%, spread across a handful of model-spec-sheet pages that look like documentation and aren't. These numbers trace back to no evaluation run at all. Don't carry them into a model choice. This has become its own pattern by now — aggregator sites fill in missing benchmark cells because an empty cell looks worse than a made-up one.
Skepticism, evenly applied The good Gemma numbers above are also vendor measurements. LiveCodeBench is built against training contamination — only tasks created after the training cutoff count — but that doesn't help against a harness optimized for the vendor's own run. And a board that doesn't say anything anymore: Aider Polyglot is stuck unchanged at November 20, 2025, over eight months old. Its top scores (gpt-5 high 88.0%, gpt-5 medium 86.7%, o3-pro high 84.9%) are numbers from the model cycle before last.

Kimi K3: weights drop tomorrow

This week's second open model comes out of Beijing. Kimi K3 from Moonshot AI is an MoE with 2.8 trillion parameters, 1M context, and a hybrid linear attention mechanism called Kimi Delta Attention. Since July 16, it's held rank 1 in the Frontend Code Arena with 1679 points, ahead of Claude Fable 5 (1631) and GPT-5.6 Sol (1618) — its predecessor sat at rank 18 on the same board. Full weights are announced for tomorrow, July 27, under a modified MIT license.

notebookcheck.net · As of 2026-07-26

Two caveats. "Open weights" at 2.8 trillion parameters doesn't mean "runs on your machine" — that's data-center hardware, open only in the sense of verifiable and self-hostable. And the Frontend Code Arena, like the Text Arena, is a vote: humans compare generated interfaces. Winning there says "the results are liked," not "the tests pass green." Still, going from rank 18 to rank 1 in one generation is a signal — and Cline has already moved ahead with support: v4.0.11 from July 24 lists Kimi K3 alongside Claude Opus 5.

Claude Code: Opus 5 is the default — and your default is asking again

Two entries from the official changelog that together shift your daily workflow. The fresh one first: 2.1.219 from July 24 — "Added Claude Opus 5 (claude-opus-5), now the default Opus model — 1M context, Fast Mode at $10/$50 per million tokens." Anyone who selects "Opus" has been getting a different model than before since Friday — with a price tag worth knowing before you let an agent run for an hour.

code.claude.com · As of 2026-07-26

The second is older but more important, and I think it flew under the radar for a lot of people. In 2.1.200 from July 3: "The 'default' permission mode was changed to 'Manual,' in the CLI, --help, VS Code, and JetBrains; --permission-mode manual and "defaultMode": "manual" are accepted alongside default." This isn't a renaming — it's a behavior change: what used to run through as the default now waits for your approval.

code.claude.com · As of 2026-07-26
What this means for you If you've noticed Claude Code asking more questions since early July: that's not a model quirk, it's the new default. Two honest sides to it. The upside: an agent that commits, pushes, and rewrites files without asking is expensive exactly when it's wrong — and the mistakes don't happen while writing code, they happen in the side effects. The cost: unattended runs now need a deliberate decision. If you have a pipeline built on silent pass-through, set the mode explicitly — in the project config, not in your head. Also from the same changelog: Claude in Chrome has been generally available since 2.1.198 (July 1).

SpaceX buys Cursor — and what that means for a tool

On June 16, SpaceX confirmed it's acquiring Anysphere — the company behind Cursor — for $60 billion in an all-stock deal; closing is expected in Q3 2026. Cursor was at roughly $2.6 billion in annualized revenue at the time of the announcement. This is by far the biggest consolidation the coding-tool market has ever seen — and it's coming from a direction nobody expected.

techcrunch.com · cnbc.com · As of 2026-07-26

My take, and I'll defend it: for you as a user, the purchase price is irrelevant, but the ownership structure isn't. An editor now belongs to a conglomerate whose core business has nothing to do with developer tools and whose priorities don't have to align with your workflow. The practical consequence is unspectacular but real: keep prompt and rule files portable, keep at least one second tool working, don't rely on anything critical that exists only in one editor. Worth noting: Cursor's own changelog has been quiet since the Router entry on July 22. This week's news comes from the owner, not the product.

MCP countdown: two days left

Short, since we covered it in depth yesterday: on Tuesday, July 28, the biggest MCP overhaul since launch arrives. The protocol drops session state and goes stateless — every request carries everything it needs, like an ordinary HTTP API. The price is spelled out in the announcement: "Servers using revision 2026-07-28 may not work with older clients, and vice versa." Compatibility only holds if both sides speak the same protocol era or one side translates. On top of that, Tasks moves out of the core into an official extension. The scale behind this: over 10,000 public MCP servers, roughly 97 million SDK downloads per month (that figure as of 2025).
