The two numbers, and why only one of them helps you

Secondary reporting says "roughly three hours until full resolution at 22:36 UTC." That checks out arithmetically for the ticket: 19:49 to 22:36 is two hours 47 minutes. But Anthropic's own monitoring note gives a much tighter window for the errors themselves: "From 12:45 PT / 19:45 UTC through to 1:26 PT / 21:26 UTC, we saw elevated rates of errors across Claude models." That's 101 minutes. The gap isn't a correction, it's a matter of definition — ticket lifespan versus measured error window. For reviewing your own failed runs, the second number is what counts: if your failed agent run fell between 16:45 and 18:26 local time, it was very likely not your prompt. After that, it was yours again.

What the primary source doesn't say Circulating summaries list claude.ai, the API, and Claude Code separately as affected. The log entry itself makes no such breakdown — it names "Claude models" broadly and doesn't call out any single model version. The three-way split is plausible, since all three surfaces hit the same inference backend. It isn't documented. Nor does the log name a cause: "an issue" is all it says — no post-mortem, no capacity or deploy explanation.
429 vs. 529: Both mean "not right now," but for different reasons. 429 is the bouncer who slows you down: you sent too many requests, wait and come back. 529 is the waiter telling you the whole restaurant is full — nothing to do with you personally. The distinction matters in practice because a lot of retry logic is tuned to the familiar codes and passes an outlier like 529 straight through as a hard failure instead of retrying with backoff. If your agent didn't wait gracefully last night but aborted mid-run instead, that's very likely why.

What to learn from this — before next time

Try it A 101-minute window is no big deal; a 101-minute window with no configured fallback route is, because during that time there's nothing to do but hit refresh. The cheapest way out is a second harness set up ahead of time: Cline is the most practical candidate today, because it routes models across multiple providers — v4.0.11 from Jul 24 added Opus 5 across providers and Kimi K3. Why try it: not for model quality, but because a stored second provider key turns an outage into a switch-over. Cursor's Router (Jul 22) also distributes across models, but within Cursor — against a provider outage, only a second provider helps, not a second model from the same vendor.

Codex builds the layer MCP leaves open

The Codex CLI got v0.146.0 yesterday, and today v0.147.0-alpha.2 is already out — two dated entries, which isn't a given for this project. The everyday improvements are quickly told: you can now "name new sessions with /new or /clear, pin important threads, and switch between" them, plus fixes for configured proxies and "enhanced stability for MCP connections and terminal responsiveness." The interesting line sits right next to it: "Agent Plugins manifests, workspace plugin publishing, and additional plugin" marketplaces.

github.com/openai/codex/releases · 0.146.0 from Jul 29, 2026, 0.147.0-alpha.2 from Jul 30, 2026, accessed Jul 30, 2026
Manifest vs. protocol: A protocol governs how two programs talk to each other — that's MCP, comparable to the electrical-outlet standard. A manifest is the packaging around it: a small file stating the tool's name, what it does, what permissions it needs, and where to get it — the label on the box. The outlet can be standardized while every manufacturer has its own box label and its own shelf in its own store. That's exactly the shelf OpenAI is building now.

Why this is more than a changelog entry

MCP has only just sorted out the wire itself: since the 2026-07-28 revision, every request carries its own protocol version, and client and server alike may "support multiple protocol versions simultaneously." What MCP deliberately doesn't govern is where a tool comes from: discovery, packaging, publishing, team sharing. Every vendor is now filling that gap on its own — OpenAI with manifests, workspace publishing, and its own marketplaces. My read: the lock-in has moved up a floor. At the protocol level you're free; at the shelf level you're not. Anyone building an internal tool today should keep the core as a dumb MCP server and wrap the vendor-specific packaging around it as a thin, disposable layer — not the other way around.

Cursor: iPad instead of substance

Cursor's freshest entry is "Cursor, now on iPad" from Jul 29. The counter-benchmark still spreads out

A benchmark hitting its ceiling doesn't mean the models are equally good — it means this benchmark no longer separates them. The broader Artificial Analysis Index still does: Opus 5 60.7%, Fable 5 59.9%, GPT-5.6 Sol 58.9%, Kimi K3 57.1%, Opus 4.8 55.7%, and a spread of 7.3 points across the top 10. The practical consequence for you: treat SWE-Bench Verified from now on as a pass-or-fail check, not a leaderboard. A difference of under two points there is no longer a basis for a decision, whether that's a subscription or a model swap in an agent chain. And yesterday's caveat still stands: these values are submitted, not independently remeasured.
