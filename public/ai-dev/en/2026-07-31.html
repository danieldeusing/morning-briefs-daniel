When the sandbox had a hole in it

Anthropic tests its models in sandboxed environments to see how far they get in a cyberattack. That's standard practice, and rightly so. Yesterday the company disclosed that this isolation failed to hold three times: a misconfiguration left the machines Claude accessed during evaluation running with "live internet access" — "Neither we nor our evaluation partner were aware of this misconfiguration until we detected it." Out of a review of 141,006 runs in which a model could theoretically have reached the outside world, three real incidents remained. By Anthropic's own account, those involved "three different Claude models: Opus 4.7, Mythos 5, and an internal research test model."

anthropic.com/news · published Jul 30, 2026, accessed Jul 31, 2026 · cross-check techcrunch.com

The three incidents, in order

First: in four runs, the model couldn't reach its simulated target inside the test environment — "but then discovered that the real company was reachable via the internet" — and ended up in a database with "several hundred rows of production data". Second, the incident with the longest shadow: a model uploaded a malicious package to the public Python index, assuming everything was simulated. The package was "made freely available online for roughly one hour" and during that hour was "downloaded and run on 15 real systems". Third: a scan across "roughly 9,000 targets", at the end of which a company's internet-facing application was actually compromised.

anthropic.com/news · all quotes verbatim from the Jul 30, 2026 report
Sandbox and egress: A sandbox is a walled-off play area where a program can do whatever it wants without consequences outside it. Egress is the question of whether traffic is allowed to leave that environment. Picture a playpen in the living room: the bars are the sandbox, the front door is the egress. If the door is left open, the playpen is still there — it just doesn't stop anything once the kid climbs over it. That's exactly what happened here: the sandbox was set up correctly, but the door right next to it was open, and nobody checked.

What this means for your own setup

Try it The story here isn't how capable a model is at attacking things — that was the point of the test. The story is that a configuration carried the entire security assumption, and nobody noticed it was wrong. Anyone running agents locally has the same class of bug at home: "I'm running this in Docker" is a claim about the filesystem and processes, not about the network. Why try it: --network=none for anything that's only supposed to rewrite code, plus a forwarding proxy with an allowlist for everything else — half an hour of setup, and an assumption becomes a verified property.

anthropic.com/news · Jul 30, 2026
The part that's getting too little attention The incidents weren't caught by a network alarm — they surfaced because someone later reviewed 141,006 transcripts. The earliest cases date back to April per the report, the review started on Jul 23, all three were confirmed the next day, and the affected organizations were informed on Jul 27. That's roughly three months during which the safeguard demonstrably didn't work and nobody noticed. What Anthropic announces addresses exactly that: "expanding our continuous monitoring of evaluation transcripts for unexpected behavior, improving our investigation tooling, and conducting more rigorous assurance work with the vendors we rely on." Translated into your everyday practice: log what your agent actually achieved, not just what it said. A safeguard you can't observe isn't one.

One more uncomfortable aside: the PyPI incident isn't purely an AI story, it's a supply-chain event. For one hour, a malicious package sat in the very index your CI installs from; fifteen machines ran it. Neither the package name nor the exact time window appears in the report — so there's no way to check whether you were affected.

MCP is getting boring — and that's exactly what it needed

On Wednesday this space noted that the version label had caught up to 2026-07-28. Today is about what's underneath that label, and it's the biggest overhaul since launch. The core line from the announcement: "MCP is transforming from a bidirectional stateful protocol into a request/response stateless protocol." The opening handshake, session IDs, and the held-open data stream are gone. Instead, every request carries everything the server needs to answer it.

blog.modelcontextprotocol.io · revision 2026-07-28, accessed Jul 31, 2026
Stateful vs. stateless: Stateful means the connection remembers who you are and where you left off — like a phone call. If the line drops, the conversation is gone, and the line has to stay occupied the whole time. Stateless means every message is a postcard that spells out everything necessary again — sender, request, ticket number. Postcards are more work to write, but any mail carrier can deliver them, you can stack them, copy them, and route them through a different post office. For software that means: the service behind it can restart at any time, be duplicated, or sit behind any load balancer.

MRTR — the replacement for the held-open line

The handshake wasn't there for no reason: some tools need to ask the user for something mid-call, a confirmation or a missing parameter. That's exactly what Multi Round-Trip Requests now handle: "MRTR replaces the server-initiated elicitation/create, sampling/createMessage, and roots/list requests that previously required a held-open stream." The mechanism is unspectacular, and that's why it's good — the server responds with "resultType: "input_required" along with the requests it needs answered", the client answers them and resubmits the request. Roots, Sampling, and Logging are thereby deprecated as standalone mechanisms, as is the old HTTP+SSE transport. No need to panic: "They still work, and they'll keep working for at least twelve months."

blog.modelcontextprotocol.io · accessed Jul 31, 2026

Why this is an operations question, not a protocol question

My read: the most important part of this revision sits in two footnotes. First, the headers — "Streamable HTTP requests now must include Mcp-Method and Mcp-Name. Your gateway, rate limiter, or WAF can route and meter on those headers instead of parsing JSON bodies." Second, caching: tools/list, prompts/list, resources/list, and resources/read now ship ttlMs and cacheScope. Together that means: an MCP server is now a perfectly ordinary HTTP service — hangable behind a standard load balancer, horizontally scalable, measurable and rate-limitable with existing ops tooling. That was the actual pain point of self-hosting, not the protocol semantics. Authentication is also getting stricter: authorization servers are supposed to return the iss parameter per RFC 9207, "and clients must validate it before redeeming a code". Practically: build new servers on the stateless core; for existing ones, rip out the held-open connection first — the rest has a year.

blog.modelcontextprotocol.io · accessed Jul 31, 2026
A number deliberately missing here The size of the MCP ecosystem circulates as "more than 10,000 public servers" — that figure comes from an aggregator, not from a primary page of the project. Plausible, but unsubstantiated, so it's not treated as fact here. Everything else above comes straight from the announcement itself.

Codex Security: open source down to the engine

OpenAI has open-sourced the Codex Security command-line tool along with its SDK — reports cite Jul 29 and Apache-2.0. The primary-source check confirms both and gives a fresher date: on the npm registry, @openai/codex-security sits under Apache-2.0, latest version 0.1.4 from Jul 30, 2026, described as "TypeScript SDK and CLI for Codex Security". The purpose: scan a repository for vulnerabilities and hang the finding as a gate in CI.

registry.npmjs.org/@openai/codex-security (primary-source check, accessed Jul 31, 2026) · cybersecuritynews.com · devops.com (both Jul 29, 2026)

The catch is in the same reports: "The CLI and SDK remain in limited beta and are available only to approved customers and partners." The package is publicly installable, the scanner behind it is access-restricted — the shell is open source, not the engine. Anyone building their own security gate into CI can still find a readable template here for how findings get modeled and expressed as a failure condition. Read as an openness announcement, it's thin: the hard part is judging which finding is real, and that stays closed.

Why this is systematic

Two reasons, neither of them a scandal — just how it's built. First, each tracker tests a different selection of models — if you don't run Qwen3.7 at all, you can't rank it first either. Second, LiveCodeBench is deliberately time-window based: it draws tasks from a fixed release period so models haven't already seen the solutions during training. Different windows yield different percentages for the same model — correct, yet not comparable. Rule for the next sales slide citing a LiveCodeBench number: without tracker, time window, and exact model version, the figure isn't a measurement — it's a claim.

Continue is out — the first real departure

For the first time, a name drops entirely out of the tool list this newsletter goes through every day. The Continue repository — for years the obvious open-source answer to Copilot as an IDE extension — says in its own words today: "The continuedev/continue repository is no longer actively maintained and is read-only for all users." Its last release was v2.0.0 from Jun 19, 2026, followed by the acquisition by Cursor. Together with the end of Windsurf's Cascade (Jul 1, 2026) and its renaming to "Devin Desktop" (Jun 2, 2026), that adds up to a pattern: the independent middle ground between vendor Copilots and your own command-line harness is thinning out. Anyone attached to an extension whose company got acquired is better off setting up a backup path today than waiting for the announcement.

Here's how it played out. Anthropic tests its models in isolated environments to see how far they get in a cyberattack. Egress is the question of whether traffic can leave that environment. Picture a playpen in the living room: the bars are the sandbox, the front door is the egress. If the door's left open, the playpen is still there — it just doesn't stop anything once the kid climbs over it.

The lesson here isn't about model capability, it's about configuration. Anyone running agents locally has the same class of bug sitting at home. A container on a default network can reach the outside world, and the line "I'm running this in Docker" is a claim about the filesystem and processes, not about the network. A run with no network at all for anything that's only supposed to rewrite code, plus a forwarding proxy with an allowlist for whatever actually needs to get out, costs half an hour and turns an assumption into a verified property. And the more uncomfortable part: this wasn't caught by a network alarm, it was caught because someone later read through a hundred forty-one thousand transcripts. The earliest cases go back to April. For three months the safeguard didn't work, and nobody noticed. A safeguard you can't observe isn't one.

The second story is quieter, and just as important for how you run things. MCP, the protocol behind tool connections, got a fundamental overhaul in the July twenty-eighth revision: from a two-way, stateful protocol to a stateless request-response one. Stateful means the connection remembers who you are and where you left off, like a phone call. Stateless means every message is a postcard that spells out everything necessary again. Postcards are clunkier, but any mail carrier can deliver them.

In practice, the opening handshake, session IDs, and the held-open data stream are gone. In their place: multi round-trip requests, covering the old case where a tool needs something more from the user mid-call. The server says input is required, the client supplies it, the request runs again. Roots, sampling, and logging are deprecated as standalone mechanisms, along with the old streaming transport. But they'll keep working for at least twelve months, so there's no rush.

The real payoff sits in two footnotes. Requests now have to carry two headers so gateways, rate limiters, and web firewalls can route and meter on them without reading the body. And list responses now carry a time-to-live and a cache scope. Put together: an MCP server is now a perfectly ordinary web service you can put behind a standard load balancer and scale horizontally. That was the actual pain point of self-hosting, not the protocol semantics. Anyone building a new server today should build it on the stateless core.
