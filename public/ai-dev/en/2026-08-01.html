Two boards, one new date, no new measurement

Anyone who wants to know today which model leads SWE-Bench Verified gets a different answer depending on the site — and both sites claim to be current. vals.ai carries "Updated: 7/31/2026" and has Claude Opus 5 leading at 97.0%, ahead of GPT-5.6 Sol (96.2%) and Claude Fable 5 (95.0%). llm-stats carries "Last updated August 1, 2026" — today's date — and has Claude Fable 5 leading at 95.0%, followed by Claude Mythos Preview (93.9%) and Claude Opus 4.8 (88.6%). Opus 5 doesn't appear there eight days after the model shipped, and neither does GPT-5.6 Sol. Both boards are dated today, and they contradict each other completely at the top.

The finding that resolves the contradiction

Lay the two lists over each other and something unexpected happens: where the same models appear on both boards, the values match to the decimal. Claude Fable 5 sits at 95.0% on both, Claude Opus 4.8 at 88.6% on both. So there's no measurement dispute here at all — the only difference is which models a board has actually run. vals.ai has Opus 5, GPT-5.6, and Kimi K3 in the field, llm-stats doesn't; llm-stats has Mythos Preview and Opus 4.7, which are missing from vals.ai's top 7. Two rosters, one yardstick, two "winners."

That's the practically important reframe: a #1 spot on one of these boards doesn't mean "best model" — it means "best model among the ones this board tested." And because no tracker discloses why a model is missing, the ranking itself is the least reliable piece of information on the page — the individual values hold up better than the order they're placed in.

vals.ai · llm-stats.com · comparison of overlapping entries, Aug 1, 2026
Board state vs. measurement state: The board state is the date printed at the top of the ranking. The measurement state is the day the numbers underneath were actually produced. It's the same difference as between the date on a restaurant menu and the day the fish was delivered: the menu can be reprinted fresh every morning without anyone having gone shopping. That's exactly what's happening here — vals.ai's seven values are digit-for-digit the same as on July 22; only the date above them moved. The practical takeaway: before quoting a ranking, don't look for the page's date — look for the newest model version in the field. If the model that shipped last week is missing, the board is stale, no matter what the date at the top says.

And what does the maker itself say?

Anthropic has published its own numbers for Opus 5 (released Jul 24, 2026, price unchanged at $5 / $25 per million tokens), and they paint a third picture: on CursorBench 3.2, the model "performs within 0.5% of Fable 5's peak score, but at half the cost per task," and on Frontier-Bench v0.1 it "more than doubles Opus 4.8's performance at a lower cost per task." The interesting part is the direction: the maker places Opus 5 just below Fable 5, while vals.ai measures it two full points above. That's not a contradiction — it's different yardsticks — but it shows how far the same pair of models can drift apart depending on what you throw at them. Anyone building a model choice on a single percentage is building on whichever number happened to be at hand.

What this means in practice Three rules, hard-earned this week. First: never cite a ranking, always cite the individual value plus the tracker plus the board state — "97.0% on SWE-Bench Verified per vals.ai, board state Jul 31" is a statement, "#1 on SWE-Bench" is not. Second: check whether the model you actually care about is even on the board. A missing entry isn't a bad score, but it isn't a good one either. Third: for your own decision, half a day with your own tasks beats any ranking — the use case that determines your model choice isn't in any of these datasets.

Open weights: a number nobody has re-measured

A good story has been circulating for weeks: Qwen 4 Coder 32B-A3B, released Jun 2, 2026, is supposedly "the first open-weight, Mac-runnable model to clear 80% on SWE-Verified" — the figure cited is 82%, Apache 2.0 license. If true, that would be the most relevant news of the quarter for anyone who prefers working locally. Only: the number lives in exactly one source, a review blog. It's on none of the boards discussed here, Qwen's own model page returned only an empty JavaScript shell with no content on retrieval, and search results turn up community requests for evidence of exactly this figure.

Which leaves it a sourced claim, not a measurement — and in this category the difference matters, because 82% would put the model within striking distance of Fable 5. Until a second, independent entry exists, the only defensible statement is: there is a new open coding model with 32 billion parameters under Apache 2.0 that runs on a Mac. That alone is worth an afternoon of trying — the percentage isn't.

llmcheck.net (sole source, ~Jun 6, 2026) · Qwen's own model page returned no content on retrieval · as of Aug 1, 2026
"32B-A3B" — what the second number means: 32B is 32 billion weights total, A3B means only about 3 billion of those are active per token. This is called mixture-of-experts — picture a large law firm where, for every question, only the two relevant lawyers stand up while the other forty-eight stay seated. You pay rent for the whole building (the model has to fit fully in memory), but salary only for the two who work (the compute per token stays small). In practice, a model like this needs plenty of RAM but runs considerably faster than its total size would suggest — exactly the combination that makes a Mac with lots of shared memory a viable platform.
