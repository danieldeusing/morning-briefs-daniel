Kimi K3 now sits in the Copilot menu

As of yesterday, GitHub is rolling Kimi K3 out into Copilot — "an open-weight model," run by GitHub itself on the infrastructure of Fireworks AI. Available for Pro, Pro+, Max, Business, and Enterprise; on the three individual plans it's on by default. The price: $3 per million input tokens, $15 per million output tokens, $0.30 for cached input.

What's notable isn't the model, it's the venue. Open weights used to be a side project: download it, quantize it, spin it up in Ollama, grumble about the speed. Anyone who wanted them in their daily workflow had to work for it. Now a model like that sits in the selection menu of the most-used editor assistant there is, on equal footing with the closed ones, billed on the same invoice. That's the real news: the detour is gone.

github.blog/changelog · announcement from Aug 6, 2026, retrieved directly Aug 7, 2026
Open weights: the "weights" are the numbers that came out of training — they are the model. If they're open, anyone can download them and run them on their own hardware. Like the difference between a recipe you get to keep and a dish you can only get at the restaurant: with the recipe, the restaurant can close and you keep cooking. What matters is what open doesn't mean: free. In Copilot you pay for running it at a provider just like any other model. The value is that, if you ever need to, you can move — to another provider or your own hardware — without switching models.

$3 / $15 isn't a bargain price. It's a statement.

That rate is exactly the one Claude Sonnet 5 rises to on Sep 1, once its launch pricing expires — $2 / $10 through Aug 31, then $3 / $15, per Anthropic's pricing page as of Aug 6. So Kimi K3 isn't priced below that, it's priced precisely on it. Pricing your open model at the established workhorse's list price instead of under it means you're not selling the price. You're selling the option: same bill, but you can take the weights with you.

GitHub writes that the model shows "frontier-level abilities on agentic coding with highly cost-effective pricing." No benchmark number comes with that — not a single one. Given what the next column has to say about benchmark numbers, that silence is almost refreshing.

Anyone working a client project on a business account and looking for the model in the menu: it isn't missing, it's switched off. The menu is getting shorter at the same time

On Jul 31, GitHub announced it would remove six models from Copilot altogether effective Sep 1, 2026: Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.5 and 4.6, Claude Sonnet 4.5 and 4.6, and Raptor Mini. One exception is stated explicitly in the announcement: "Claude Sonnet 4.6 will remain available to individual GitHub Copilot subscribers on annual plans so they retain a Sonnet offering."

Same week, then: one open model in, six older generations out. That's portfolio maintenance, not a conspiracy — but the selection says something. It hits almost exclusively the second-to-last generation, and the only fallback left hangs on the annual contract. Anyone on a monthly plan who's grown used to a particular older model has four weeks to adjust.

Worth trying Why an hour is worth it: Take a task you've already completed this week with your default model, and have Kimi K3 do it again in agent mode. You already have the comparison result, it only costs you tokens — and you'll learn more about its fit for your own codebase in one hour than from any leaderboard. What that's worth is the subject of the next column.

Two leaderboards, one contradiction

Update since yesterday On Aug 6, this brief reported that no independently maintained board recognized Claude Opus 5. That's no longer quite true — or rather, it holds for one board and not the other. llm-stats is dated Aug 7, 2026 today, lists 104 evaluated models, and still has Claude Fable 5 at the top with 95.0%. Opus 5: not listed. BenchLM is dated Aug 6, 2026 — and tops its chart with Claude Opus 5 at 96%.

And now the part that settles it: on the entries both boards carry, they match exactly — Fable 5 at 95, Opus 4.8 at 88.6, Opus 4.7 at 87.6. Not approximately. Digit for digit. Two separately run evaluations of the same task set don't land on the same decimal place three times in a row; the process is too shaky for that, the tool harness too different, the whole thing too expensive to even run twice. Either both draw on the same raw data, or one is copying the other.

That leads to an uncomfortable reading: BenchLM looks like the other board's list, with entries added on top whose numbers come from vendor announcements. That can't be proven. But the site also doesn't state any methodology for how the individual SWE-bench figures are arrived at — just general sentences about ranking weights and freshness metadata. And that's exactly the point: a leaderboard with no stated methodology isn't a measurement. It's a claim in table layout.

llm-stats.com · board dated Aug 7, 2026 per the site · benchlm.ai · board dated Aug 6, 2026 per the site, retrieved directly Aug 7, 2026 · Opus 5 vendor figure from Jul 24, 2026
Aggregator vs. testing lab: a leaderboard can come about in two ways. Either someone loads the model, actually has it run the tasks, and counts how many tests turn green afterward — that's a measurement. Or someone collects the numbers vendors state in their announcements and sorts them into a table — that's a press kit with a sort function. From the outside, both look identical: same percentages, same model names, same ranking design. Like a restaurant guide that either sends someone to eat there or just retypes the kitchens' own self-descriptions. The difference lives on the methodology page — and if there isn't one, you simply don't know.

Same pattern at LiveCodeBench

There, llm-stats, dated Aug 1, tops its chart with Gemini 3 Pro Preview at 91.7%, while BenchLM, dated Jul 26, has Qwen3.7 Max at 91.6%. Not a direct contradiction — six days separate the two snapshot dates. But the same situation again: two secondhand numbers, and the official board loads its table via JavaScript, so it can't be independently recounted. Both are presented here for what they are, then — dated secondary figures, not a measurement.

llm-stats.com (dated Aug 1, 2026) · benchlm.ai (dated Jul 26, 2026) — both captured via search summaries, not checked against the official table

The question worth asking a board

Not "who's on top," but "how did the number get into the row." Does the site state who ran the evaluation, when, with what harness, and where the logs live? Checking that costs thirty seconds and replaces a surprising amount of agonizing over decimal points.

Codex: login method decides

Effective Aug 31, 2026, GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.4-mini disappear from Codex — but only for users signing in with their ChatGPT account. Anyone running Codex via an API key keeps both, and they remain available via the API regardless. Recommended migration: gpt-5.6-terra for 5.4, gpt-5.6-luna for 5.4-mini — both significantly cheaper since Jul 30.

The split by login method is the interesting part. It isn't the model getting switched off, it's the convenient access to it. Anyone working off the subscription gets told what's current; anyone billing through the key decides for themselves. The same dividing line as with open weights on the left, just a notch milder — and the bill comes due on the day the provider rearranges its lineup.

Alongside that, a figure OpenAI states about itself: agentic work via Codex now accounts for 99.8% of weekly output tokens generated. There's no definition of what counts as "agentic" here, and it can't be verified. It's notable anyway — not as evidence, but as a directional signal: internally at OpenAI, chat has apparently become a marginal case.

Claude Code: /fast has meant Opus 5 since Jul 24

The fast-mode switch isn't news from this week — it's two weeks old. Opus 5 has been the default model in fast mode since version 2.1.219 on Jul 24, the same day the model itself launched. Opus 4.7 has been out of it ever since; /fast now only covers Opus 5 and 4.8.

What actually shipped this week is smaller: 2.1.221 (Aug 4) brings a focus view for VS Code, 2.1.222 removes ultraplan, 2.1.223 (Aug 6) adds a /teleport prompt when switching from cloud to local sessions plus marketplace controls. Useful day to day, but not a headline — and a good reminder to change trackers that "shows up in my list today" and "happened today" are two different things.

Two prices, no contradiction

Anthropic's pricing page lists Opus 5 in normal operation at $5 in / $25 out per million tokens — and alongside it, in a separate "Fast mode pricing" table, the same model (together with Opus 4.8) at $10 / $50. Both figures are accurate, then; they just apply to different operating modes: "significantly faster output for Claude Opus 5 and Claude Opus 4.8 at premium pricing." Anyone using /fast simply pays double — across the whole context window, explicitly including beyond 200k input tokens. Fast mode is a research preview, available only directly on the Claude API, not via AWS or the partner clouds, and not usable together with the Batch API.

In practice that means: fast mode is a cost decision, not a comfort setting. For a long agent run, /fast doubles the bill without it standing out mid-session.
