That's not a discount campaign, it's a purchase offer — and the price is public for the first time. Meta puts a price on what your source code is worth

On Aug 5, Meta released Muse Code: a command-line agent, in beta, installed via a script, for now macOS and Linux only. It's powered by Muse Spark 1.2 — not the 1.1 covered here on Aug 3; the version number is consistent across three separate reports. As a tool, that's unspectacular at first glance: one more terminal agent alongside Claude Code, Codex, Cline, and OpenHands. What's interesting is the price list next to it.

Contributor rate: you get the same model at a fraction of the price, and in exchange you let the provider use your inputs and the model's responses as training material. Like a photo lab that practically gives you the prints for free if it can use your pictures for its catalog. For code, "your pictures" concretely means: the task description, error messages, file paths, the snippets the agent read — and what the model made of them.

Twenty-one times over isn't a discount. It's a bid.

Do the math on the difference and the trade sits out in the open: Meta puts $1.15 per million input tokens and $4.05 per million output tokens on the table — not as a wire transfer, but as a discount. This is the first publicly posted unit price for developer work as training material that I've seen in a vendor's pricing table. Until now, the question "are you training on my data?" got answered with a checkbox in the settings. Here, it gets answered with a number.

And the number is more honest than the checkbox. Uncomfortable to read, but still the cleanest version: a provider that writes down the price is negotiating; one that collects consent in the sign-up dialog is not.

Rates per forbes.com (Aug 6, 2026) and implicator.ai (Aug 5, 2026), in agreement · difference figures are our own calculation from these rates
For client projects: settled With someone else's code, the Contributor rate isn't a trade-off to weigh, it's ruled out. What generates the discount — Meta gets to train on prompts and responses — is exactly what every standard NDA prohibits. And a terminal agent's prompt systematically contains more than "write me a function": file paths, architecture decisions, error messages straight from production. For your own projects and throwaway tooling, on the other hand, the rate is a serious offer.

It doesn't automatically beat $20 a month

Coverage is putting Muse Code up against the flat-rate plans: Anthropic's Pro with Claude Code at $20 a month, the Max tiers at $100 to $200; OpenAI's Codex via ChatGPT Plus at $20 or Pro at $100. The comparison limps in both directions. Billed per token, you pay less than any flat rate under light use — and more under heavy agent use, because a flat rate wins precisely when you max it out. Anyone who doesn't regularly bump against the subscription's usage limits saves nothing by switching, and loses the predictability.

The number that's missing

There's no published benchmark figure for Muse Spark 1.2 itself. What exists: the predecessor 1.1's figure on Terminal-Bench 2.1 (80.0), and a list of the models Meta reportedly tested 1.2 against — Grok 4.5, Claude Opus 5, GPT-5.6 Terra, Gemini 3.6 Flash, Kimi K3. No scores appear next to any of them. A launch with no scoreboard is rare, and it allows two readings: either the number isn't fit to show, or Meta has learned that a scoreboard of its own is mostly a target today. The second column of this brief shows why the second reading isn't the dumbest one.

Cline had the rate on the menu a day later

Per its release notes, Cline v4.1.6 from Aug 6 offers "meta/muse-spark-1.2-contributor on the Cline provider" — plus CLI v3.0.51 and SDK v0.0.71 the same day, both with the identical line. What's notable is the name's suffix: the selection menu explicitly names the data-donation variant. Within a single day, the cheap rate migrates from Meta's price list into a dropdown menu with no pricing table next to it. That's exactly how these decisions will get made from now on: not by reading the terms of service, but while scrolling through a model list.

The 86.1 is real. The comparison isn't.

Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max has been available via the API since Aug 3, and the launch chart claims a win on OSWorld-Verified: 86.1 against Anthropic's Fable 5 at 85.0, GPT-5.6 Sol Max at 83.2, and Gemini 3.1 Pro at 76.2. That chart comes entirely from Alibaba — including the rivals' figures.

Today's test: does any of that turn up outside the vendor's slide? Partly. BenchLM's board for OSWorld-Verified is dated Aug 7, 2026, and does list Qwen3.8 Max at the top with 86.1, followed by Claude Mythos 5 and Claude Fable 5, both at 85. GPT-5.6 Sol Max isn't on it. Neither is Gemini 3.1 Pro. So the figure exists in an outside table — but the comparison Alibaba is actually going for exists only in its own.

OSWorld-Verified: a test suite where the model doesn't write text, it operates an actual computer — opening windows, clicking, typing, saving a file, until the task is done. Like a driving test instead of a classroom lesson. The score is the share of tasks passed. And just like a driving test, the result hinges heavily on the surrounding conditions: which car, which route, how many attempts, how much time. That's exactly what the board itself warns about — comparisons are only valid "when the setup matches": same task set, same environment, same tool harness, same time and attempt budget.

The methodology flaw has a name: time limit

For Terminal-Bench, the second figure on Alibaba's chart, there's a concrete finding. The evaluator Vals.ai recomputed it and writes: "Alibaba's reported Terminal Bench results modify benchmark timeouts, whereas Vals preserved original timeouts." In plain terms: Alibaba gave the model more time per task than the test setup calls for. That's not fabrication — it may well even be documented. But it's a different test, and the results aren't comparable as a result. For an agent whose core promise is "keeps working independently for a long time," the time limit turns out to be about the most sensitive dial there is.

Alibaba states, in its own words, how the rivals' figures came about

And this is the part the company deserves credit for: by its own account, it tested the competition "using each vendor's own coding harness, Claude Code for Anthropic's models and Codex for GPT-5.6 Sol" and used "the highest published score across available configurations" for each rival. That sounds fair, and it half is. Measuring other models in their own harness is the decent approach. But "rival's highest published figure versus your own freshly optimized one" is a calculation where one side controls every dial and the other contributes a snapshot pulled from the archive.

What independent boards actually say

Compiled by latent.space on Aug 4, with Alibaba nowhere in the source chain: on the Frontend Code Arena, Qwen3.8-Max sits at rank 4 with 1,668 points, behind Claude Opus 5 Max (1,705) and Kimi K3 Max (1,676). On the Vals Index, rank 2 among open models, tied with Claude Opus 4.7 at 66.1. On SWE-bench, 87.3% — ahead of GPT-5.5 (82.6%), behind Claude Opus 4.8 (89.2%). On Terminal-Bench 2.1, 67.4.

That paints a different picture than the launch chart — and a good one: a very strong model in the top five, nowhere unchallenged in the lead. Read the vendor's slide and you see a changing of the guard; read the boards and you see a tight field. The second one is the more useful information.

The rule of thumb

A vendor's chart doesn't become a measurement just because someone copies it into a board. The check that worked today takes two minutes: don't look for the advertised figure on an outside board — look for the rivals from the same chart. If they're not there, it isn't a comparison. It's a lineup.

Editorial assessment, not a claim from the source

What verbosity costs

Qwen3.8-Max costs $2.00 per million input tokens, $6.00 per million output tokens, and $0.25 for cached input — cheaper than the previous Max ($2.50 / $7.50). At 2.4 trillion parameters, that's a remarkable price. Except the list price isn't worth much here.

Artificial Analysis gives the model an Intelligence Index of 58 against a median of 33 in the same price bracket — and notes in the same breath that, in its evaluation, it generated 150 million output tokens where the median sits at 66 million. So roughly double the text for the same amount of task. Anyone budgeting by list price is calculating past the actual bill for this model: $6 per million tokens, but roughly double the quantity of them.

Rates per latent.space (Aug 4, 2026), cross-checked against artificialanalysis.ai (retrieved Aug 8, 2026) · index and token figures from artificialanalysis.ai · the doubling conclusion is our own calculation
2.4T parameters, 95B active: "Sparse Mixture-of-Experts" means the model is made up of many sub-models, and only a few of them switch on per request. Like a large hospital — hundreds of specialists on staff, but only three handle your exam. That's how a model this big can still answer affordably. For you, that means: the figure "2.4 trillion" says something about metadata and the operator's storage needs, almost nothing about speed or price.

And two footnotes on "openness"

The open weights are still announced for the week of Aug 10, with no fixed day; until then, the model is purely API merchandise via Alibaba Cloud. Alongside that, a note that's getting lost in the enthusiasm: a commentator apparently found geographic bans for the US, EU, UK, and Korea in the terms of use. That hasn't been independently verified here — but anyone planning to rely on an "open" model for client work should read the license before committing to it.

As for the often-cited demo claiming the model carried a software project through 16 days autonomously, analyst Amit Jena's objection still stands: "Sixteen days of what? How many times did a human step in?" Until that's answered, it isn't a result — it's a commercial.

It's Saturday, August 8th. Today's real story isn't the product, it's the price list sitting next to it. On August 5th, Meta released a terminal agent called Muse Code — and for the first time, spelled out in public exactly what your source code is worth as training data.

The agent itself is nothing special on its own. It's in beta, installed via a script, for now only on macOS and Linux, and it's powered by a model called Muse Spark one point two — not one point one, that was the previous version. As a tool it lines up next to Claude Code, Codex, Cline, and OpenHands.

What's interesting are the two pricing tiers. The standard rate costs one dollar twenty-five cents per million input tokens and four dollars twenty-five cents per million output tokens. The second tier is called Contributor and costs ten cents and twenty cents respectively. Twenty-one times cheaper on the output side. The price for that: Meta gets to train future models on your inputs and the model's responses.

Do the math on the difference and the trade sits out in the open. Meta is putting one dollar fifteen cents per million input tokens and four dollars five cents per million output tokens on the table — not as a payment, but as a discount. It's uncomfortable to read, and still the cleanest version of this. Until now, the question of whether a company trains on your data got answered with a checkbox in the settings. Here, it gets answered with a number. A provider that writes down the price is negotiating. One that collects consent in a sign-up dialog is not.

For client projects, the matter is settled anyway, and settled against it. The very thing that generates the discount is exactly what every standard NDA prohibits. And a terminal agent's prompt systematically contains more than a task description: file paths, architecture decisions, error messages straight out of production. For your own projects and throwaway tooling, though, the rate is a serious offer. What's striking is how fast it landed in the toolchain: Cline has offered, since August 6th, of all things the data-donation variant in its provider menu, by name. These are decisions that will increasingly get made while scrolling through a model list, not while reading terms of service.

One number is missing entirely from Meta's side. There's no published benchmark figure for Muse Spark one point two. What's published is only the score of the previous version on Terminal-Bench — eighty point oh — and a list of comparison models with no scores attached. A launch with no scoreboard at all is rare.
