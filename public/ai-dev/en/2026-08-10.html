Anthropic does away with the permission prompt

On Friday, August 14, auto mode becomes the default in Claude Code for every Pro, Max, and Team account. Until now, the tool has asked before every file write, every shell call, every network access — going forward it runs straight through, and a classifier watches instead of you.

The justification comes down to a single number, and it's damning: users approve 97% of all permission prompts. Anyone who answers yes to 97 out of 100 questions has stopped reading them. The prompt stopped being a control a long time ago and became friction you click through — and safety you click through isn't safety.

techcrunch.com — reported Aug 9, 2026, 12:20 PDT; announcement dated Aug 9, switch effective Aug 14.
Classifier: A second, small model that doesn't write code, it only judges: is this one action dangerous, yes or no? Think of a bouncer who doesn't run the club, just stands at the door deciding who gets in. The upside: it never gets tired and reads the thousandth request as carefully as the first. The cost: it judges by patterns someone else set, and that someone hasn't published them.

The number everything hinges on

Anthropic backed the switch with its own test: 1,053 paying users, and auto mode caught 89% of harmful actions, while human review caught 13.6%. That's a factor of six and a half — and if it holds up, the prompt isn't just useless, it's actively harmful, because it creates a sense of safety with nothing behind it.

Two objections that don't appear in the writeup. First: this is Anthropic's own measurement, published by the company selling the replacement, and it's nowhere independently verified. Second, the definition is missing. What counts as a "harmful action" — an rm -rf in the wrong folder, a key left in plaintext, a network call to an unknown address? Without that list, the 89 isn't wrong, but it isn't checkable either. The head of Claude Code puts it this way: "The team and I use Auto mode exclusively, and have been for many months. I couldn't imagine going back to permission prompts!" An anecdote, not evidence.

techcrunch.com, Aug 9, 2026 — quote from Boris Cherny, figures from Anthropic's internal test

What this means for Friday

It's a default switch, not an abolition — the mode stays toggleable. Still, on Friday the behavior of your toolkit changes without you doing anything, and that's the point where it's worth taking a look.

The solid answer isn't "turn the prompts back on." If the prompt gets waved through 97% of the time, switching it back on gets you the ritual back, not the safety. The real boundary sits a level deeper, and it was already the topic here last week: a dedicated working tree per agent, a container, a branch you can throw away if it goes wrong. What the agent can't reach, nobody needs to approve. If you do exactly one thing before Friday, make it this: check whether your agent is running in a directory whose loss would actually hurt.

Editorial assessment based on the report linked above
The uncomfortable part The 13.6% is a verdict on the interface, not on people. A prompt that pops up for every cat of a file trains you within days to ignore it — and then you also miss the one that matters. Anthropic fixes that by removing the question. The other conceivable fix would have been to ask less often, but mean it.

The price gap widens to a hundredfold

Three coding models arrived within eleven days, and all three attack the same spot: price. On Jul 31, DeepSeek V4-Flash-0731; on Aug 3, Qwen3.8-Max; on Aug 5, Meta's Muse Spark 1.2 along with the terminal agent Muse Code. None of them claims to be the best model. All three claim to be good enough for most tasks — at a fraction of the cost.

Bottom to top, that's a 107-fold spread. Artificial Analysis converts the same range into running costs: around 3 cents per test run for V4-Flash versus 86 cents for Kimi K3, $1.86 for GPT-5.6 Sol, and $3.15 for Claude Fable 5 — the cheapest known model in the world, per the firm.

DeepSeek's cache hit rate deserves its own line: $0.003 per million, a 98% discount on input. That's exactly the line item where agent loops bleed money, because they feed the same project context back in a hundred times over. For the mechanical part of the work — reading test output, scanning logs, renaming across twenty files — that's a different order of magnitude from anything above it.

Not a rounding error — two real tiers: $0.20 is Meta's "contributor" tier, offered in exchange for letting Meta train on your prompts and data. I'm using the standard tier of $4.25 above.
Active parameters: V4-Flash has 284 billion parameters, but only 13 billion work on any given token; Qwen3.8-Max has 2.4 trillion total, around 95 billion active. Picture a hospital with 284 specialists, where only the 13 relevant ones ever walk into a patient's room. The breadth of knowledge is that of the whole hospital, but you pay for and compute only the small group. That's the entire trick behind the prices in the table — not better hardware, just less computation per word.

Meta buys its way in with the price tag

Muse Code is a terminal agent with persistent background runs, going head-to-head with Claude Code and Codex. Meta's own numbers are remarkably honest here: on Terminal-Bench 2.1, Muse Spark 1.2 scores 82.9% against 86.7% for Opus 5, and on Meta's internal coding test, Opus 5 wins 79.4 to 70.6. So Meta is saying it itself: we're worse. Just nearly six times cheaper on output.

That's a defensible bet, but not one for every purpose. A 3.8-point gap on a tool-use test means, in practice, more attempts, more dead ends, more manual cleanup. On mechanical work, price wins. On the difficult refactor, where the second attempt costs more than the first call, it doesn't.

For anything you want running locally or on your own hardware, that's the entry of the week — and the 27B variant is the one that stays realistic on a single card. And nobody is re-measuring

Now the part that ruined my weekend. Not one of the three new models has an independently reproduced SWE-Bench Verified score. The official leaderboard loads its table via JavaScript and returns nothing to a plain fetch; the underlying results repository, where the raw evaluations live, hasn't had a new entry in the Verified folder since June 2025 — fourteen months. Neither V4-Flash, nor Qwen3.8-Max, nor Claude Opus 5 shows up there.

What circulates instead: for V4-Flash, depending on the source, 70.3, 77.4, or 79.0 — figures that contradict each other and all trace back to model cards or summaries. For Qwen3.8-Max, 67.7 gets passed around, but that's SWE-Bench Pro, a different test with different tasks. So I'm not writing a single one of these numbers here as measured.

Checked Aug 10, 2026: swebench.com/#verified returns no table data on fetch (JS-rendered); github.com/SWE-bench/experiments, folder evaluation/verified/, has no entry newer than June 2025

The substitute is a vote

What's left is the frontend code arena, where Qwen3.8-Max has held rank 4 since Aug 3 with 1,668 points — behind Claude Opus 5 (Max) at 1,705 and Kimi K3 (Max) at 1,676, tied with Opus 5 (High) at 1,669. The gap from the top spot to rank 4 is 37 points out of roughly 1,700, just over two percent. Between rank 3 and 4 sits a single point.

@arena, post from ~Aug 3, 2026 — rank, score, and comparison figures taken verbatim from the announcement
Preference arena: Two models answer the same question, a human says which answer they prefer, and that builds a leaderboard. It's a blind tasting: you learn which wine people like — not whether it survives the evening. A task benchmark like SWE-Bench Verified instead asks something checkable: does the test come back green afterward? When the tasting replaces the exam, the industry starts measuring likability instead of getting the job done. That's exactly what just happened, and nobody announced it.

The Intelligence Index from Artificial Analysis stays at least somewhat useful, since the firm runs the models itself: V4-Flash scores 52 out of 101, Qwen3.8-Max 58, putting it at rank 9 of 185. That's an aggregate score across many tests, not a coding measure — it tells you both play in the same league, and nothing more.

Which leaves the advice that's been standing here for weeks and carries even more weight today: a solid comparison costs one afternoon. Two real tasks from your own project, both models in the same harness, results checked yourself. At $0.28 against $25 per million output tokens, that's no longer busywork — it's the only number that matters to you.

MCP: the candidate from Jul 28 sails through

The MCP specification's release candidate from Jul 28 brings a stateless protocol core, an extension framework, tasks, MCP Apps, and hardened authorization. Twelve days later, Cline already had the workaround for dynamic registration built in, and Cursor's changelog names this version too. Stateless here means: the server doesn't need to remember anything about you between two calls — which makes it runnable behind a load balancer, exactly the thing enterprise installs otherwise trip over.
