Cursor Now Belongs to SpaceX

As of yesterday, the acquisition announced in June is legally final. According to the regulatory filing, all of Cursor's common and preferred shares were converted into a total of 389,289,254 SpaceX Class A shares, implying a Cursor equity value of $60.0 billion. No cash changed hands — that's the whole point.

All-stock deal: Imagine trading your bike not for cash, but for shares in the bike shop. For Cursor's owners, that means their wealth is no longer tied to the editor as of today, but to SpaceX as a whole. And for SpaceX, it means the purchase didn't touch the cash register — the price gets paid in dilution.

Morgan Stanley analysts think Cursor could contribute around $2.5 billion in revenue to SpaceX in 2026 and about $13 billion in 2027. That's an estimate, not a reported figure — but it explains why the price doesn't look absurd: what got bought wasn't an editor, it was a subscription channel into practically every developer team.

What changes for you: nothing in the short term. The product kept shipping weekly right through the closing — on Aug 13, "builds" for cloud agents (pre-warmed work environments, up to three times faster startup) plus an AIUC-1 security certification; on Aug 12, Grok 4.6 in the model menu; on Aug 6, a dedicated router for auto mode.

Medium term, I see two open questions. First, the name: coverage of the closing mentions a planned rebrand, with no date attached. Second — and I think this is the more important one — model neutrality. Cursor's real value was always "we route you to whichever model is best." An owner with its own AI ambitions naturally has an interest in that router developing a favorite direction. Grok 4.6 was added two days before the closing; that proves nothing, but it's the kind of detail worth remembering.

Context Not a reason to panic, but a good excuse for a quick exercise: how much of your workflow lives in Cursor-specific formats — rule files, MCP integrations? If the answer is "everything," that's a concentration risk regardless of who owns the company.

Claude Code: The Default Has Shifted

It's a subordinate clause in the changelog, and it's practically the most consequential change of the week: subagent forking is on by default as of v2.1.232. A subagent of type fork inherits the full running conversation, including the prompt cache. On top of that, agent launches that don't come from a team member now run in the background in interactive sessions instead of blocking.

Fork & prompt cache: Until now, a subagent was the new person in the room — you had to explain everything to it again. A "fork" is instead a copy of yourself at the current moment: it knows everything you currently know. The prompt cache is the receipt proving the model has already read this context once — it gets carried over instead of you paying to re-read the whole text.

That's mostly good: less repetition, noticeably lower cost per subagent, no more briefing busywork. The catch sits right next to it. An inherited conversation also inherits the clutter — every dead end, every half-followed lead you chased before. And "in the background" means: you no longer automatically read along. Parallelism for free, attention as the price.

The second shifted default is auto mode — and unlike what the changelog trackers suggested, it's cleanly documented: Anthropic announced it on its own blog on Aug 7. It states, verbatim, that as of August 14, "new sessions on Pro, Max, and Team accounts" run in auto mode. A classifier lets harmless tool calls through and only holds back irreversible, destructive, or externally-facing ones. The justification given is that 97% of all permission prompts get confirmed anyway.

So the number checks out — it just answers the wrong question. The 97% is an argument about friction, not about safety. Safety comes from the classifier — and nobody has published a figure for its error rate.

Heads up Two defaults shifted at the same time — subagents inherit more, and permissions get asked for less often. Combined, that means more happens without you watching. Running /permissions once and deliberately setting your mode costs ten seconds.

The Leaderboard You Can No Longer Read

Let's start with what Anthropic itself writes about Opus 5 (Jul 24) — because what's missing there is the story. It cites FrontierBench v0.1 (43.3% at maximum effort against 18.7% for the prior generation, ahead of Fable 5 at 33.7% and GPT-5.6 Sol at 37.5%) and CursorBench 3.2, where Opus 5 lands "within 0.5% of Fable 5's top score" but at half the cost per task. No SWE-bench Verified figure appears anywhere. The vendor with, right now, the strongest coding model is skipping the best-known coding leaderboard.

Anyone who still wants the number has to rely on aggregators — and they disagree: on the same reference date (Aug 14), one provider has Opus 5 at 96%, the next at 97% at the top. I couldn't cross-check the primary source; swebench.com loads its table via JavaScript.

Same pattern on the harder SWE-bench Pro: 79.2% for Opus 5, rank three behind Mythos 5 (80.3%) and Fable 5 (80%) — per an aggregator whose site returned a rate limit today. The official Scale board, at time of fetch, still showed models from before Opus 5. None of these figures has two independent confirmations.

Google: One Platform, Half the Price

Since the 2.0 announcement at I/O (May 19), Antigravity is no longer an editor add-on but a platform: a desktop app, a standalone CLI, a managed agents API — meaning agents run by Google that you simply task — and an SDK for running it on your own infrastructure.

The figures circulating for the Gemini 3.1 Pro harness underneath: 80.6% SWE-bench Verified, 54.2% SWE-bench Pro, roughly 70.3–70.7% Terminal-Bench 2.1. I only have these numbers from summary write-ups with no day-level date attached — see the piece next door for why I'm not putting them in a chart.

The more interesting story is the cadence anyway: Gemini 3.7 Flash shipped on Aug 13, three weeks after 3.6 Flash, explicitly for coding and agent workloads — and at half the predecessor's price through year-end. Try Why: in an agent loop, it's not the smartest model that's expensive, it's the one pushing through the most tokens. That's exactly where a model in this class belongs, and a halved list price is an honest incentive to measure it for a week instead of guessing. Three weeks between two Flash generations is also a signal, though: don't bolt a toolchain permanently to either version.

Open Weights Are Catching Up Where It Counts

DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 (Jul 31, MIT license) is the most interesting release of the past two weeks, and not because of where its name sits on some leaderboard. The jump is in the agentic tests: DeepSWE 54.4 vs. 7.3 for its own preview predecessor, Terminal-Bench 2.1 at 82.7 vs. 61.8. Going from 7.3 to 54.4 within one generation isn't fine-tuning, that's different behavior. Artificial Analysis independently ranks the model 2nd out of 162 models measured. Try Why: $0.14 / $0.28 per million tokens with 1M context — as a workhorse in a loop that reads a lot and decides little, the price-performance ratio beats everything above it.

284B parameters, 13B active (MoE): A "mixture of experts" model is like a large specialist newsroom: everyone's in the building, but for any single question only a handful of people actually stand up. The model is huge, but computing each word only draws on a small slice of it — which is why it's fast and cheap. The catch: the whole building still has to be loaded.

So a sober addition on the "local" question: in 2026, open weights no longer mean "runs on your laptop" — 284B parameters is a server matter. You gain free choice of provider, not independence from the data center.

Qwen did both in the same week: the hosted service Qwen3.8-Max (Aug 3, 1M-token context, FrontierSWE up from 40.7 to 73.5) and, on Aug 8, open weights for Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B — 2.4T parameters, 95B active per token.

Inkling from Thinking Machines (Jul 15, open weights) is the quietest entry with the smartest detail: an effort dial between 0.2 and 0.99 determines how many thinking tokens the model is allowed to spend on a task. The company claims it matches Nvidia's Nemotron 3 Ultra's Terminal-Bench score using roughly a third of the tokens. Scores: 77.6% Verified, 54.3% Pro, 63.8% Terminal-Bench 2.1 — with the same caveats as next door.

MCP Goes Stateless

The spec release candidate from Jul 28 is the biggest overhaul since launch: a stateless core, plus MCP Apps (servers can ship their own UIs), a Tasks extension for long-running work, and OAuth/OIDC-style authentication.

Stateless: Until now, an MCP server had to remember who you are and where you're at in the conversation — like a waiter keeping every order in his head. Stateless means each request brings its own order slip along. Sounds more cumbersome, but it's the reason the whole thing can suddenly scale behind an ordinary load balancer.

In practice, that's the difference between "the MCP server runs on my machine" and "the MCP server runs as an ordinary service." The Tasks extension is the second real advance: tool calls that take minutes previously had to be forced into a request-response pattern.

On ecosystem size, 9,800+ servers are circulating across directories (as of Aug 2) — a metric with little explanatory power. It only gets interesting once someone counts how many of those speak the new spec.
