The Check Against Meta's Bar Charts

On Aug 13, this space complained that Meta had published only bar charts for Muse Spark 1.2 — Terminal-Bench, DeepSWE, an internal board, GDPval, all as image files, with not a single percentage in the page text and no methodology section. Update since Thu Someone has since filled exactly that gap, and the result is uncomfortable for Meta.

I pulled Vals AI's coding overview myself today — eight leaderboards, six of them updated on Aug 13, SWE-bench Verified on Aug 12, IOI on Aug 9. Muse Spark 1.2 doesn't appear at the top of any of the displayed leaderboards. Claude Opus 5 leads four of the eight boards (Code Migration, ProgramBench, SWE-bench Verified, IOI) and sits in second place on LiveCodeBench.

What this doesn't mean The overview page only shows the top spots for each board. "Not visible" therefore means not at the top — not not measured. I only have the specific ranking secondhand: an analysis by Labellerr, citing Vals AI tests, puts Muse Spark 1.2 at rank 14 of 50 models for general coding. I found no second independent confirmation of that ranking.

On top of that comes the trap you fall into when comparing the numbers currently circulating. For Muse Spark 1.2, the figure making the rounds is 82.9% on Terminal-Bench 2.1, a gain of 6.7 points over version 1.1. For Opus 5, one tracker cites 42.7% on Terminal-Bench 3.0. Set side by side, that looks like a landslide. It isn't one.

Benchmark versions 2.1 and 3.0: A benchmark is a test with a fixed set of tasks, and the tasks get swapped out from time to time — like a final exam that's called "Math Final" every year but asks different questions each time. Comparing a grade from one year's cohort against another is nonsense, even if both say "Math Final" at the top. Neither of my sources says which of the two Terminal-Bench versions is harder. The fact that the scores are forty points apart is itself evidence that two different tests were measured here.

On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, there's at least one figure on the same scale: Muse Spark 1.2 sits at 54 there, tied with Grok 4.5; Claude Opus 5 at 63 and rank 1 of 188. In the interest of full disclosure: I pulled these two figures from two different places, not from one shared table.

My take The Labellerr author's verdict nails it: benchmark claims that show up as an image with no methodology are marketing until someone independent re-measures them. I still think the architecture of Muse Code — subagents in their own git worktrees, an event log over everything — is the right idea. But as of today, the model underneath it isn't a top-tier model, and anyone taking the contributor tier at $0.10/$0.20 is paying with training rights to their own source code for a mid-pack model. Fine for a weekend project. Still no for client code.

Devin at Forty Billion

Cognition, the company behind the agent Devin, is reportedly negotiating a funding round that would value the company at around $40 billion, according to an Aug 12 Bloomberg report. In May it was $1 billion in fresh money at a $26 billion valuation. Three months, a good fifty percent more on the price tag.

According to the reporting, the figure is backed by an annual run rate of $1 billion — versus $492 million, which the company's CEO had confirmed three months earlier.

Annualized revenue run rate: Not a year's revenue, but the current month times twelve. Like earning three hundred euros in the December gift season and saying your shop does 3,600 euros a year. For a growing subscription business, that's a common and not unfair calculation — but it assumes the current month is representative, and it says nothing about costs.

For day-to-day work at the editor, that changes nothing for now. It's interesting as a market signal pointing in a direction this brief has been tracking for weeks: what's getting paid for right now isn't the model, it's the scaffolding around it. The bigger piece of evidence for that took effect last Friday: SpaceX completed its acquisition of Anysphere, the company behind Cursor — merger agreement dated Jun 16, effective Aug 14, 2026, paid entirely in SpaceX stock (389,289,254 Class A shares) at an implied equity value of $60 billion. That's what the SEC filing, Form 8-K, says.

The parallel only holds halfway As of Friday, Cursor is no longer an independent company — it's a wholly owned subsidiary of its buyer. That leaves only Cognition as an example of "an independent tool builder getting a rich valuation without training a top-tier model itself." What the Cursor price shows instead may be the sharper signal: a buyer put up $60 billion of its own equity for the editor, the scaffolding, and its user base — that is, for the layer that sits between the model and the developer.

My skepticism about Cognition stays two-sided. The valuation is reported, not confirmed — "in talks" isn't a closed deal. And the run rate doubling within a quarter comes with no public breakdown: how much of it is annual contracts, how much is a usage spike from a model switch? For buying a tool, though, something else counts: a vendor with this kind of capital cushion isn't going to vanish next year. For developer tools, that's a real argument — for Cursor, it now applies in a different form: its survival no longer hinges on its own funding rounds, but on its buyer's corporate strategy.

Where the Coding Leaderboards Stand

Yesterday, this space said SWE-bench Verified is done as a selection criterion. Today, for the first time in days, I can back that up with a cleanly dated table instead of two contradicting aggregators — and the table confirms exactly that finding.

SWE-bench Verified — Top Field per Vals AI, as of Aug 12, 2026

The top spot sits three points from a perfect score, and second place trails first by 0.6 points. At this range, a board no longer measures differences in capability — it measures noise and harness fine-tuning. As a reminder of the uncertainty here: a second tracker lists 96% instead of 97.00% for the same model, as of Aug 15. Both are secondary sources — swebench.com itself loads its table via JavaScript and, both yesterday and today, delivered no readable rows.

The useful news isn't in the rank, it's in the breadth. Opus 5 leads four of eight coding boards at Vals AI and sits in second on a fifth. Leading across eight different tests is much harder to explain away as training contamination or an environment tuned for a single test than a single top score is. That's the kind of claim a leaderboard can still support — "leads broadly," not "leads at 97.00 instead of 96.40."

And for completeness, the figure Anthropic itself cites and that no leaderboard verifies: on CursorBench 3.2, Opus 5 lands, at maximum effort, "within 0.5% of Fable 5's top score," at half the cost per task. That's a vendor figure on a vendor-adjacent test, and it's useful for exactly that: as a price-performance argument, not as proof of capability.

The Agent Decouples from the Model

The most practically useful news of the week sits in a version list. Ollama v0.32.11 from Aug 14 adds a connector for "DeepSeek harness and Muse Code," plus web search through the OpenAI interface. In plain terms: Meta's terminal agent can now point at a model running on your own machine.

Harness: The scaffolding around the model — what reads and writes files, runs commands, launches subagents, checks results. Like the car around the engine: the model supplies the power, the harness decides whether that becomes a delivery van or a race car. As long as both come from the same maker, you buy them together. Once they can be separated, you can drive the good harness with the model of your choice.

That's exactly what happened here, and it defuses the trade-off from Aug 13: what was interesting about Muse Code was never the model, it was the architecture — subagents in their own git worktrees, an event log over everything. Until now, that was tied to Meta's price list, and in the cheap tier, to Meta's training rights. As of Friday, that coupling is optional. Try Why: spend an afternoon running the harness with a local model on a real branch. Even if the local model is weaker, you'll be measuring what's actually new here — whether parallel subagents in separate worktrees improve how you work.

Around all that, Ollama had its busiest week in months. Muse Glimmer, Meta's open model, arrived on Aug 10, first via the MLX engine on Apple Silicon and, the same day, on all platforms. Nvidia's Nemotron 3.5 Lightning followed on Aug 11 as a 30B MoE, and on Aug 14, Qwen3.8 at the 27B size — the first release of this generation that's realistic on a single machine. On Aug 15, v0.32.14 shipped with WebP transcoding in llama-server.

Yesterday's sober footnote still holds: in 2026, open weights mostly mean "server-scale." But 27B and 30B MoE are exactly the class that runs on a well-equipped workstation. That class getting three new entries in a single week is the real movement here — not the next tenth of a point on SWE-bench.

Both details come from changelog summaries, not from a fetch of my own.

MCP remains at its Jul 28 state: a stateless protocol core, plus Extensions, Tasks, and MCP Apps; all four tier-1 SDKs support the release, with the Rust SDK in beta. No new signal this week — which, for a spec three weeks after release, is a good sign.
