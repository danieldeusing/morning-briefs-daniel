Continue Is Discontinued

Since early August, this brief has run a mandatory daily roundup through every coding tool, including the ones where nothing happens. Continue.dev has stood there since Aug 1 with the same sentence: unchanged since Jun 19. On Aug 11, I called it "the strangest case" — two releases one second apart, then 53 days of nothing, but no archive notice on the repository. That notice is there now.

I fetched the project page myself today. The notice reads, verbatim: "The continuedev/continue repository is no longer actively maintained and is read-only for all users." The Jun 19 release is described by the project's own text as the "Final 2.0.0 Release" — with polish to the VS Code extension, the CLI, and the JetBrains plugin, and with expanded telemetry and sign-in. This isn't a project that fell dormant. It's one that was properly wound down, without anyone saying so out loud. 35.5K stars still sit on the repository.

What I got wrong The observation was right — 53 days without a release is a warning sign for a tool this size. The conclusion was wrong: I treated the absence of an archive notice as counter-evidence ("the last-reported version stands until proven otherwise"). But an archive notice isn't a leading indicator — it's a cleanup step taken after the fact. Anyone waiting for it learns of the end two months late. As of today, this roundup applies a new rule: a tool with no release for over six weeks is carried as unresolved, not "unchanged."

In practice, that means three things. First: the installed extension keeps running — it's the maintenance that's discontinued, not the function. Second: there are no more security fixes coming, and a tool with write access to your source code and keys to third-party interfaces is the wrong place for "it still works." Third, and this is the urgent part: according to a retrospective on the shutdown, the service's cloud data was deleted after Jul 15, 2026. That isn't stated on any project page I could fetch — anyone who still had the hosted portion connected checks it themselves today, rather than relying on this secondary source.

The Layer Above the Model Gets Bought

Continue didn't die from a lack of interest — it died from an offer. According to the same retrospective, the team moved to Cursor in June, and Cursor has belonged to SpaceX since Aug 14. That closes the chain:

Continue Team→Cursor→SpaceX
Acqui-hire: One company buys another not for the product, but for the people. Like a soccer club that acquires a smaller club just to get its coaching staff — and then disbands the team. For users of the acquired product, that's the worst-case outcome: it doesn't die because it was bad, but because nobody's left to maintain it.

Cursor's own post about the acquisition justifies it purely in terms of compute: access to the "largest GPU fleet in the world," and with it the compute time to build stronger models that are also cheaper to run. Grok 4.6 in Cursor, published Wednesday, is named there as the first preview of the shared roadmap. The $60 billion valuation circulating in coverage, incidentally, does not appear in Cursor's own text — it comes from media reports and from the mandatory filing with the US securities regulator, which this brief reviewed on Aug 16.

That leaves an uncomfortable conclusion for how you pick tools. The usual reflex — "take the tool with the biggest community, it'll survive" — didn't hold for Continue: 35.5K stars didn't keep the project alive one day longer. And the second reflex, "take the one with the deepest pockets," now leads to tools whose survival no longer hinges on their own business, but on the corporate strategy of a buyer whose main business is rockets. Both criteria deliver less than they promise. The useful criterion is in the third column.

The Cheap Tier, Read Three Times

On Aug 12, this space carried an open contradiction: two prices were circulating for GPT-5.6 Luna, and I couldn't say which one held. Today it resolves, and the resolution is instructive.

GPT-5.6 Luna — via OpenRouter, labeled "50% off"$0.10$0.60
Gemini 3.6 Flash — promotional price through Dec 31, 2026$0.75$3.75
Gemini 3.6 Flash — from Jan 1, 2027, per Google$1.50$7.50

The second row is exactly half the first — for both values, down to the cent. That isn't a contradictory price, it's a middleman's discount on the same list price, and OpenRouter even labels it as such. The page names no end date.

Model router: A middleman through which you can reach many models from different vendors with a single API key. Like a flight-booking portal: you book the same flight, but not directly with the airline, and the portal layers its own promotional pricing on top of or below the fare. Convenient — but you shouldn't base your budgeting on the promotional price, since that belongs to the portal, not the vendor.
Sourcing note I saw the $0.20 / $1.20 list price directly on OpenAI's pricing page on Aug 12. Today, openai.com was blocked for fetching — so the figure comes from my last verified state plus two secondary sources. The widely repeated story that there was an 80% price cut on Jul 30 rests entirely on secondary reporting; I still haven't seen a dated OpenAI document for it as of today. So I'm citing the price, not the date of the cut.

The two Gemini rows are a different category, and the more interesting case. First, the price itself: Google's own pricing page lists Gemini 3.6 Flash at $0.75 / $3.75 per million tokens and states explicitly that this rate runs through Dec 31, 2026, reverting to $1.50 / $7.50 on Jan 1, 2027. So it's a temporary launch discount, not a permanent list price — anyone building an annual budget should plan for double that from January on. (Older reports, including the one cited here on Jul 21, still name $1.50 / $7.50; that's the rate that only applies again from 2027.) Google isn't selling Gemini 3.6 Flash on token price to begin with, but on consumption: the model is said to need 17% fewer output tokens than its predecessor on general tasks, and up to 65% fewer on the DeepSWE agentic test. The argument is sound at its core — what you pay is token price times tokens per task, and an agent that talks itself in circles ruins any cheap rate. But: both percentages are vendor and test-vendor claims, I have them via a citing trade source and not via Google's own page, and nobody independent has re-measured them. Until then, "up to 65%" is a marketing number with a true core.

Portability Is the Buying Criterion

If neither community size nor capital cushion keeps a tool alive, one question remains that you can ask when setting up: how much of what I'm building here survives the vendor? Two developments from recent weeks answer that halfway seriously for the first time.

MCP in its Jul 28 release has a stateless protocol core, plus Extensions, Tasks, and MCP Apps; the extension for enterprise-managed sign-in is marked stable and, per the spec page, is used by Anthropic, Microsoft, and Okta. Three weeks on, no addendum and no retraction — for a spec, that's a good sign.

Agent Plugins 1.0 has been generally available since Aug 12, in VS Code, the Copilot CLI, the SDK, and the Copilot app. The core idea is packaging: agent capabilities and MCP servers land in a single installable bundle that, per GitHub, is "managed independently of any single vendor."

Why these two go together MCP makes the connections swappable — which data sources and tools your agent can reach. Agent Plugins makes the setup portable — your house rules, workflows, and server list as one package. Only together do they mean a vendor switch is an afternoon, not a rebuild. Continue users are living through the version without that right now.

Try Why: take your actual setup — house rules, two or three MCP servers — and package it once as an Agent Plugin. The test isn't whether it works, it's how much you still have to rebuild by hand. That remainder is your vendor risk, and you measure it in an afternoon instead of on the day an archive notice shows up.
