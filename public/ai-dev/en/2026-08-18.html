Four Models, Two Points

Grok 4.6 entered Artificial Analysis's evaluation on Aug 12, and the result says more about the field than about the model. It lands at 61 points — tied with GPT-5.6 Sol, one point behind Claude Fable 5 (62), and two behind Claude Opus 5 (63). Four models from four different labs sit within two points of each other on a scale whose gradations nobody has calibrated.

Intelligence Index: not a single test, but a rollup of roughly a dozen exams — math, code, domain knowledge, tool use — collapsed into one number. Like a GPA: it compresses ten subjects into a single figure, and the difference between two very close averages tells you nothing about who'll turn out to be the better programmer. A number like that is useful for separating orders of magnitude — not for ranking next-door neighbors.
Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index — as of Aug 12, 2026 (V4-Flash: model page, Aug 18, 2026)

Things get interesting on price, and there the math clearly works out in Grok 4.6's favor. It costs $2 per million input tokens and $6 per million output tokens; Artificial Analysis prices its own test suite at $0.84 per task. Opus 5 sits at $5 / $25, Sol at $5 / $30 — for two points and zero points more, respectively. On the command-line test Terminal-Bench 2.1, which is more relevant to agentic work than any rollup score, Grok 4.6 scores 88.4%, putting it, per the evaluators, "in line with the leading models."

My read: at the top, the question "which model is strongest" has become a rounding error. The question you can still answer is "which model gets my task done at the lowest price per task" — and that's a calculation you can only run on your own source code, not on a leaderboard. Grok 4.6 is the first candidate in months where the price gap is large enough that running your own comparison is worth the effort.

Sol Grew After the Fact

When the GPT-5.6 lineup launched on Jul 9, the same evaluator wrote: "GPT-5.6 Sol (max): 59 points". Its model page for Sol, fetched today, reads: "GPT-5.6 Sol (max) scores 61 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index". Two points of growth, with the name unchanged and the price unchanged ($5 / $30).

There are two possible explanations for that, and both are uncomfortable for you. Either the model changed — OpenAI announced an improvement to Sol on Aug 6, citing "roughly 68% fewer responses with at least one factual error" in its own measurement, versus GPT-5.5 Instant. Or the leaderboard changed, because new sub-tests were added or old ones were reweighted. I can't resolve it: OpenAI's announcement explicitly refers to Sol in ChatGPT — the consumer product — while the index measures over the API.

What that means A model name isn't a version number. If you've hard-coded gpt-5.6-sol into a call, the identifier alone won't tell you whether you're talking to the same machine you were in July. Two practical consequences: first, every leaderboard score cited in your team wiki needs a retrieval date attached — without one, it isn't verifiable. Second, a small test suite of your own, run on your real source code once a month, is the only measuring instrument that actually belongs to you. Five to ten tasks are enough; you're not measuring intelligence, you're measuring drift.

The same day, OpenAI made GPT-5.6 Luna the default for free and Go accounts, adding unlimited text conversations and a "Think" button in the week starting Aug 10. For developers, only one part of that matters: the cheap tier is being pushed out broadly, and API prices were cut on Jul 30 by 80% for Luna and 20% for Terra. Anyone with a task that doesn't need top-tier intelligence — reformatting, commenting, generating test data — is currently paying pointlessly much for Opus- or Sol-class models.

That puts the margin of error in the same range as the signal being measured. Both firms say so themselves: Vals AI writes that there's "little room … to separate frontier models on this benchmark alone"; benchlm puts it as the top models sitting "within 1.0 points," which it reads as pointing to saturation.

Two footnotes belong alongside every citation of these numbers. First: swebench.com itself still loads its table via JavaScript and returns no readable rows on a plain fetch — so the figures above come from independent verifiers, not from the benchmark's own board. Second: no value exists for GPT-5.6 Sol on SWE-bench Verified that I could find. The only published scores for Sol are on SWE-bench Pro and DeepSWE. Anyone comparing Sol and Opus 5 "on SWE-bench" is comparing two different tests.

And the third, oldest footnote has stood in the same place for weeks: Aider's Polyglot leaderboard is still dated Nov 20, 2025, checked again today, topped by GPT-5 (high) at 88.0%. Nine months without an update means the list doesn't contain a single one of the four models in the left-hand column.

DeepSeek-V4-Flash: Two Prices, One Model

This brief covered the jump itself on Aug 15 — DeepSWE from 7.3 to 54.4, Terminal-Bench 2.1 at 82.7. What's new today is the fine print, and it matters more than it sounds.

First, where the jump came from: the architecture stayed unchanged — 284 billion parameters total, 13 billion active, a 1 million-token context. The entire gain came from post-training.

Post-training: The base model learns language and the world in a months-long, very expensive pass. After that comes a comparatively cheap second pass that teaches it how to behave — calling tools, planning steps, not giving up. Like a student driver who already knows the engine and the traffic rules and just needs practice hours: same brain, suddenly it drives. For you, that means capability jumps can arrive without new data centers, in weeks instead of quarters.

Second, the price — and here two sources diverge: Artificial Analysis lists the model at $0.44 / $1.32 per million tokens, the vendor itself at $0.14 / $0.28 on a cache miss and $0.0028 on a cache hit. That's not an error on either side, most likely a different operating mode — a more reasoning-heavy variant costs more. In practice: never budget with the list price from a comparison article, budget with the rate of the variant you're actually calling. Here, the difference is a factor of three to five.

Skepticism marker Every jump figure for this model is a vendor claim on an unpublished test rig — the source says so explicitly. None of it has been independently re-measured as of today. A ≈645% gain on a test that nobody but the vendor can run is a claim with a number's shape, not a result.

Cursor Now Wants Your Repository, Too

The one piece of hard news from the last 24 hours: Cursor introduced code hosting on Aug 17. A new "Codebase" tab creates and manages repositories, with sync to GitHub repositories and two-way handling of pull requests. Rolled out in early beta to all paid plans.

Editor→Agent→Host

This is the third step in a shift this brief has tracked for two weeks. First Cursor was the interface layered over other vendors' models, then the operator of its own cloud agents, now the place where the source code lives. Anyone with all three layers at the same vendor no longer has a tool — they have a platform. And that vendor has belonged to SpaceX since Aug 14.

My recommendation Two-way sync here isn't the convenience feature it's marketed as — it's your emergency exit, and you should treat it exactly that way. Concretely: GitHub (or whatever you're using today) stays the system of record, Origin is the copy. Not the other way around. The effort is the same either way; the difference only shows up on the day a vendor doubles its prices or gets sold. Yesterday, this space was still asking how much of your setup survives a vendor switch. For a repository, the answer is simply: all of it or none of it.

In the interest of accuracy, a correction to my own reporting: yesterday I described the Aug 13 cloud-agent environments, secondhand, as "up to three times faster." The changelog itself cites a ten-times-faster start and a three-times-faster time to first token — two different metrics that a summary had blended into one.

Tool Roundup

Try GitHub Copilot for JetBrains got two things on Aug 11 that belong together: memory across sessions and a connection to local Ollama models with your own key. Why try it: this is the first of the major vendors to let a locally running model into the IDE, instead of alongside it. For the share of work that doesn't need top-tier intelligence but does see your source code, that's exactly the right shape — and it costs nothing per call.

BYOK ("bring your own key"): The tool supplies the interface, you supply access to the model — your key, your bill, your choice of vendor. Like a restaurant with a corkage fee: the kitchen and service come from the house, you bring your own wine. With Ollama, the "key" is simply your own machine, meaning no bill at all.

Claude Code has run with auto mode as the default for Pro, Max, and Team accounts since Aug 14 — the agent keeps working without asking at every step, except for irreversible, destructive, or environment-leaving actions. My assessment from Aug 15 stands unchanged: the mode itself is good; the default is the real story.

Qwen launched a 2.4 trillion-parameter model, 3.8-Max, hosted-only, in early August (95 billion active, 1 million context, $2 / $6), then followed up on Aug 13/14 with Qwen3.8-27B, a small open-weight version under Apache 2.0. Both figures come from secondary sources, not from Qwen's own page — what's solid, at least, is that Ollama v0.32.12 added Qwen3.8-27B with MLX acceleration for Apple Silicon on Aug 14. That's the more interesting line for a workstation.

Ollama: no new release since v0.32.14 on Aug 15, checked myself today. Three quiet days after the busiest week of the summer.
