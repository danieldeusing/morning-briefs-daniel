Three Points, and Who Measured Them

The number at the center of today's story sits on Qwen's own model card: SWE-bench Pro 61.7 for Qwen3.8-27B. Next to it, OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol — the model Codex recommends by default — at 64.6. Two point nine between a model you can download for free and one you pay for by the token. If that holds up, it's the most important news of the month for anyone weighing how much vendor lock-in they still want to carry.

Open weights / Apache 2.0: "Weights" are the numbers baked into a model — the actual learned content. Open means: the file is publicly downloadable, you can run it on your own hardware, modify it, and build it into a product. Apache 2.0 is the most generous of the common licenses — no usage restrictions, no revenue cap, no "research only." Think of the difference between a rented car and one you own: with the rental, the owner decides where you're allowed to drive.

Except the comparison isn't a fair fight — and the difference isn't in the numbers, it's in who collected them. The 64.6 for GPT-5.6 Sol is confirmed by two independent aggregators in agreement, both as of Aug 18. The 61.7 for Qwen3.8-27B appears exclusively on the manufacturer's own model card. One writeup that reviewed the launch figures puts it bluntly: "Every launch score comes from Qwen … Independent replication is pending." All numbers from the vendor, independent replication still outstanding.

This brief derived a rule of thumb yesterday from two model generations of a different vendor: on agentic benchmarks, the vendor's own figure regularly sits about three points above the first independent remeasurement. Applied here — as an expectation, not a measurement — Qwen3.8-27B lands at roughly 58.7, and the gap to Sol grows from 2.9 to about six points. Not proof, but a more honest starting point than "three points."

SWE-bench Pro — top scores, as of Aug 18, 2026 (Qwen figure: vendor claim)

The second objection is bigger and doesn't need a rule of thumb at all. GPT-5.6 Sol isn't the top of this leaderboard. Above it sit Claude Mythos 5 at 80.3, Fable 5 at 80.0, and Opus 5 at 79.2. The gap from the open challenger to the actual best-verified entry is 18.6 points — more than six times the gap the headline suggests. Anyone who wants to write "open is catching up" needs to specify catching up to what: Qwen3.8-27B is in the neighborhood of OpenAI's flagship, not in the neighborhood of the top.

My take The direction is right, the magnitude isn't. That a 27-billion-parameter model shows up within shouting distance of a frontier flagship at all was unthinkable a year ago — that's the real story. But treat 61.7 as a claim with an expected value, not a measurement: figure on roughly 59, plan for an 18-point gap to the top, and wait for the first independent entry before you base an architecture decision on it. The test that actually decides this is your own repository anyway.

What 27 Billion Means on Your Own Machine

Regardless of whether the 61.7 holds up, the size class is the real reason this model matters to you. The model card lists 27 billion parameters and a native context window of 262,144 tokens, expandable to up to one million per the card. Other card figures, all from the same vendor: LiveCodeBench v6 90.3, Terminal Bench 2.1 (Terminus) 73.0, QwenSWEBench 79.0, averaged over three runs.

Why "27 billion" is really a hardware spec: Every parameter takes up memory. At the usual 4-bit compression, that's half a byte per parameter — 27 billion × 0.5 comes out to roughly 13.5 gigabytes for the weights alone, plus room for context. That fits comfortably on a 24 GB graphics card, tightly on 16 GB. For comparison: the trillion-parameter models in the same family need a data center. That's arithmetic, not a measurement — but it tells you in ten seconds whether the model will even start on your machine.

Try it Why it's worth it: Apache 2.0 means you can build it into a client project without asking permission; the size class means you can run it locally instead of shipping source code to a third-party API. Two properties no leaderboard captures, and ones that often matter more for contract work than four benchmark points.

One more caveat: Terminal Bench 2.1, of all things, is the benchmark where the vendor markup was most pronounced recently, and where the result depends heavily on the surrounding harness. I give the 73.0 the least weight of the bunch — not because Qwen is less credible than anyone else, but because this test isn't comparable without stating the harness.

SWE-bench Verified Is Done as a Differentiator

The evaluation I used today for SWE-bench Verified carries an update date of Aug 19, 2026, and reads like an obituary for its own purpose: the top spot is Claude Opus 5 at 97.00%, followed by DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 at 96.40 and Kimi K3 at 93.40. In total, seven of 86 evaluated models reach 95% or above. A test where seven contenders are splitting the top five percentage points no longer measures capability — it measures residual errors.

Verified vs. Pro: SWE-bench Verified is the human-reviewed subset of the original test — real GitHub bug reports checked to confirm the task is solvable and clearly described. Pro is the successor for when the first one gets too easy: bigger repositories, more files per change, fewer one-liners. Think of the difference between a driving test and advanced driver training — almost everyone passes the first, the second is where you actually separate people.

That's why the interesting action today is on Pro, where 15.7 points still separate first place (80.3) from the Codex flagship — and why both Qwen and the aggregators reach for Pro when making the comparisons that matter.

Two honesty notes on these figures. First: the same top model shows 96.0% instead of 97.00 on a second tracker — a full point of difference for the identical model, which both sides attribute to different harnesses and evaluation procedures, not a data error. It's the same effect this brief described for Terminal Bench yesterday: what gets measured is always the combination of model and environment. Second: the official SWE-bench site, which this newsletter treats as the primary source, again returned no readable data today — the table loads via JavaScript. I queried it myself, then fell back to the dated independent evaluation, as this newsletter's fallback ladder prescribes. So this figure comes from a dated tracker, not from the maintainer.

Codex: A Deadline in Eleven Days, and an Update That's More Than Cosmetic

First, the thing that belongs on your calendar. On Aug 31, 2026, GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.4-mini get removed from Codex. The model page names the successors directly: gpt-5.4 → gpt-5.6-terra, gpt-5.4-mini → gpt-5.6-luna. The default model for new sessions is already gpt-5.6-sol at medium reasoning depth. If you've hardcoded a model name anywhere — in a config file, a CI step, a script — you've got eleven days to switch it.

And then the piece that's actually interesting: Codex CLI 0.148.0 from Aug 18 brings four things. Conversations can now be exported in full as Markdown with /export. Sessions can be forked with codex exec fork, and archived and restored. Amazon Bedrock Runtime is now a built-in provider. And hooks can run commands asynchronously while calling MCP tools.

Try it Of these four, only the last one really counts in my view. A hook used to be a shell command that stepped in synchronously at a fixed point — useful for a linter, useless for anything that takes longer than a second. Made asynchronous and given access to MCP tools, it becomes its own small task running alongside the main one: a check against a ticket system, a document search — without pausing the agent. The second most useful item is session forking: build up a long context once, then play out three variants from it instead of starting over three times. The Bedrock integration is probably irrelevant to you, but it signals a direction — Codex is slowly becoming a tool you can also run other vendors' models through.

Cursor Now Holds a Goal — Update to Yesterday

Update since yesterday Yesterday this brief said Cursor hadn't shipped anything since "Origin" on Aug 17. That was already outdated at the time of writing: on Aug 19, the most substantial of the three August entries landed. Cloud agents can now "pick up work triggered by events, hold a goal until it's reached, and stay on track even across long sessions" — per the changelog.

"Holding a goal": Until now, an agent run was a task with a beginning and an end — you describe what needs doing, it works, it stops. A goal-holding agent instead gets a state it's supposed to maintain, and keeps working until that state holds. Think of the difference between "wipe down the kitchen once" and "the kitchen should be clean" — with the second, it comes back whenever someone's cooked. In practice, that means a directive like "keep this test suite green" turns into something an agent runs continuously instead of completing once.

The other two August entries: Origin (Aug 17, early beta, all paid plans) brings repository management, pull requests, GitHub sync, and integrations with Vercel, Depot, and Buildkite directly into the tool. And on Aug 13, cloud agents started launching into prebuilt environments instead of setting up each session from scratch — "three times faster to first token," per the vendor. My take on Origin stands from yesterday: GitHub is the original, Cursor is the copy. The goal-holding agent is the more interesting bet — it answers what an agent does when nobody's watching.

Copilot's "New" Usage-Based Billing Is Two and a Half Months Old

The claim that GitHub Copilot is switching to usage-based billing is making the rounds through the summaries again. I pulled up the announcement myself today: it's dated Apr 27, 2026, and has been in effect since Jun 1, 2026 — not news, but the status quo for the past two and a half months.

What's actually in effect: "Premium Request Units" have been replaced by GitHub AI Credits. Usage is billed by token — input, output, cached — at the API prices of whichever model you use. Subscription prices stayed the same and include a matching amount of credit: Pro $10, Pro+ $39, Business $19 per user, Enterprise $39.

The practical difference is bigger than it sounds: previously, every request cost the same regardless of which model ran behind it — now your usage depends on the model and the context length. If you're running Copilot across a team, break usage down by model, not by headcount.

From the same corner, but genuinely new: Agent Plugins 1.0 has been generally available since Aug 13 — a plugin built once runs in VS Code, the Copilot CLI, the SDK, and the app. And on Sep 1, several models get retired across all Copilot surfaces.
