Google Gives Up the Editor — and Wins Procurement

Yesterday's announcement reads small: Google is bundling Antigravity into its Gemini Enterprise subscriptions and releasing extensions for a handful of editors. I opened the announcement myself today — strategically, it's the most interesting story of the week. Antigravity is included in Gemini Enterprise Standard, Plus, and Standard Emerging Market. The Visual Studio Code extension is generally available; Visual Studio, JetBrains, and Zed are in preview. The standalone Antigravity 2.0 app and the Antigravity CLI remain alongside them.

Why "extension instead of dedicated IDE" is the point: Until now, Antigravity was its own editor — install it, migrate your settings, relearn your habits. An extension is an add-on for the editor you already have open. It's the difference between "move into a different house" and "I'll put a coffee maker in your kitchen." The second costs nothing in terms of habit — which is why it happens far more often.

The context is what makes the move legible. On Jun 18, 2026, Gemini CLI and the Gemini Code Assist extensions stopped serving requests from Free, Pro, and Ultra users — Google cleared out its own command-line tool and folded everything into Antigravity. Two months later, it has four editors, one app, one CLI, and a slot in a subscription enterprises already pay for. A cleanly executed two-step: clear your own field first, then occupy it properly.

Professionally, the governance layer interests me more than the agent itself. Verbatim from the announcement: administrators "maintain full governance with spend, security, observability and usage metrics consolidated in the Gemini Enterprise admin console." Enforceable controls include "workspace sandboxing, and browser and MCP server access" — that is, which tools the agent is even allowed to touch. And: "Enable comprehensive audit logging with a single toggle, capturing prompts, agent responses, and metadata for compliance reporting."

My take That's the sentence that describes the market: prompts and agent responses get logged with a single toggle. Anyone who thinks the competition between coding agents will be decided by benchmark points in 2026 is looking at the wrong column. It'll be decided by who's first to ship a tool that a compliance department will wave through — sandboxing, an access policy, an unbroken audit trail, a line item. Google's advantage here is one no startup can replicate: procurement has already signed the contract. For you as a one-person shop, this changes nothing; for the clients whose projects you sit inside, it changes everything. Expect "we already have something in Gemini Enterprise" to become the counterargument to your tool recommendation more often from here on.

Try it Anyone already working in VS Code or a JetBrains IDE gets the agent here without switching IDEs. VS Code is the mature path; the other three are explicitly preview and don't belong near client code.

What's Actually Open Today — and What Just Calls Itself That

Three names are circulating around Z.ai's (Zhipu's) GLM line, freely mixed up with each other. The difference is practical, not academic: only one of the three can you actually download today.

GLM-5.2 is the release that's genuinely open — the official model card lists 753 billion parameters, an MIT license, and roughly one million tokens of context; several matching evaluations put the active share per token at around 40 billion. Released mid-June. GLM-5.3 was announced on Aug 14, with the claim of being the strongest open-weights model for coding — but it's only available hosted. On the weights, it says verbatim: "The model weights are set to go open source in two weeks, once security reviews wrap up." Two weeks from Aug 14 lands around Aug 28. GLM-5.5 hasn't been announced as of today; what's circulating about it is third-party reporting and community rumor. I'm mentioning it only so you don't mistake it for a shipping product.

753 billion parameters, 40 billion active: Models like this are built as a "Mixture of Experts" — instead of one giant network, many small specialist departments, of which only a handful switch on per token. Like a law firm with 750 employees where 40 work on your case: you pay compute time for the 40, but memory for all 750. "Open weights" here doesn't mean "runs on your machine" — the file is several hundred gigabytes. On a single graphics card, the 27-billion class is still what's in play.

On the performance claim, I'm more cautious than the headlines. The figure making the rounds through the aggregators is that GLM-5.3 improves coding ability by 50% over GLM-5.2. I opened the article that claim is based on myself today — the number isn't in there. What is in there is more interesting: "The model shares the same base as its predecessor, GLM-5.2, and all gains come from extended post-training alone." Same base, the entire gain from extended post-training.

Post-training: A model comes together in two stages. In the first, it reads enormous amounts of text and learns language and code in the first place — the expensive, rare stage. In the second, it's taught how to behave: finish tasks, call tools, don't give up mid-refactor. Like a fully trained tradesperson getting onboarded into your business — no new craft, but far more useful within your workflow. "All gains from post-training" means: the foundation is unchanged, what improved was behavior.
My take Marketing a model as the "strongest model with open weights" while the weights aren't open isn't a detail — it's the whole story. The claim only becomes verifiable once the file exists. And the pattern repeats: announce during the news cycle, release two weeks later once nobody's watching anymore. Mark Aug 28 on your calendar. If the file shows up, the claim is real and we'll measure it against GLM-5.2. If it doesn't, it was an ad for a paid access tier.

What's already on the disk in the same class: Kimi K3 from Moonshot AI, open since Jul 27 — though under its own bespoke Kimi K3 License, not MIT (it includes a revenue-triggered clause above $20M/12mo MaaS revenue) — per matching secondary reports, 2.8 trillion parameters, 16 of 896 expert departments active per token, one million tokens of context. That's what GLM-5.3 will have to measure up against on Aug 28.

Claude Code: Two Releases in Two Days, One of Them Cost Money

The changelog lists two new versions: v2.1.238 from last night (around 8:33 PM, per a secondary tracker) and v2.1.237 from Aug 19. The list is unspectacular — and contains exactly one entry that cost real money if your setup was wrong.

From v2.1.237, verbatim: "Fixed prompt caching for sessions using an LLM gateway or custom base URL." Anyone not running Claude Code directly against the Anthropic API, but through a gateway or a custom base URL — the norm rather than the exception in a corporate setting — had broken prompt caching.

Prompt caching: With every request, you send along the entire context — system instructions, project rules, half the codebase. Caching means: the provider remembers that unchanged front portion and charges you a heavily discounted rate for it next time. Like a copy shop that leaves your original in the machine as long as you keep reordering. When that breaks, you pay full price every round for the same text — for long agentic sessions, that's not a rounding error, it's a multiple.

Also from v2.1.237: a built-in output style called "Concise" that "leads with results and skips preamble for short focused replies." Sounds cosmetic, but it's the entry I'd personally switch on first — preamble is pure token tax on short tasks.

From v2.1.238: keybindingFlavor for readline-style Ctrl-W; plugin marketplaces now support a headersHelper for generating HTTP headers; --defer-shutdown-max-min for self-hosted runners; and a fixed "memory growth in long interactive sessions" — the most practical entry for anyone who leaves sessions open for hours. One version before that, without an exact date in the changelog, added ANTHROPIC_DEFAULT_MODEL and notify_when_idle for cross-session messages — the latter being the missing piece for running multiple agents where one needs to notice that another has finished.

Try it The headersHelper is the quiet door-opener: a plugin marketplace can now sit behind a login, because the client generates the necessary HTTP headers itself. That makes an internal marketplace for proprietary extensions possible, instead of distributing everything publicly or by hand.

The August Throughline: Extensions Get Managed, Not Just Installed

Three stories this week look unrelated and point in the same direction. Antigravity lets administrators enforce MCP server access by policy. Claude Code lets plugin marketplaces generate authenticated HTTP headers. And the MCP extension Enterprise-Managed Authorization — already stable since Jun 18, 2026 and, per the project, already adopted by Anthropic, Microsoft, and Okta — officially became part of the new extensions framework with the final Jul 28, 2026 spec, which also introduces a stateless protocol core.

MCP: The Model Context Protocol is the power-outlet standard between AI agents and tools. Instead of building a separate connection to your ticketing system, your database, your wiki for every single model, you write one "MCP server" — and any agent that speaks the standard can use it. Like USB: one plug, many devices. Enterprise-Managed Authorization is the add-on that determines who's even allowed to plug in — the company grants and revokes access centrally, rather than the individual developer.

The sequence is worth noting: first, connecting tools got easy; now it's getting controllable. For solo developers, that means little — your MCP server keeps running. For anything you sell into an enterprise, it means a lot: an agent tool without central access control and without an audit trail isn't "lean" anymore, it's "not deployable." The authorization layer is no longer a version-2 task.

Confidence note: the spec details come from a search synthesis of the project blog, not from a direct page fetch of my own.
