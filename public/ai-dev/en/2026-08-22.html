Grok 4.6: Four Benchmarks You Can't Look Up Anywhere

xAI launched Grok 4.6 on Aug 12, 2026. The most solid frame of reference comes from a third party: on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, the model scores 61 points, tying it with GPT-5.6 Sol Max for third place — ahead of it sit Claude Opus 5 and Fable 5. Also: 500,000-token context, $2 per million input tokens and $6 per million output tokens. Above 200,000 tokens, the price doubles to $4 / $12.

More interesting than the ranking is what xAI itself reports for coding performance: CursorBench v3.2 69.9%, DeepSWE v1.1 65.9%, FrontierCode v1.1 Extended 61.3% — and Terminal-Bench v3.0 26%. Same model, four tests, a 44-percentage-point spread.

Why 69.9% and 26% aren't a contradiction: These tests measure two different jobs. An editor test like CursorBench hands the model a file and a clear task — it's the baker following a pre-written recipe. A terminal test puts it alone in front of a command line: explore the environment, call its own tools, notice its own mistakes, stay on track across many steps — that's the baker who has to shop, bake, and fix the oven with no recipe at all. That's exactly where every model breaks down today. For agentic work, the number that counts is the small one.

And here's where it gets uncomfortable: none of these four tests shows up on an independent, verifiable leaderboard I could open today. These are vendor figures, passed along through a trade article. On the two third-party SWE-Bench Verified evaluations I cross-checked today, Grok 4.6 doesn't appear ten days after launch — still listed is the predecessor, Grok 4.5 at 86.6%.

My take Launching a new model with four in-house benchmark names and then not showing up for months on the one board anyone can verify is no scheduling accident. It's the most convenient way to get measured: by yourself. I'm counting the 61 on the independent index as real news and the four coding figures as advertising until someone checks them.

Try it The price is the real argument here. $2 / $6 with a 500,000-token context makes Grok 4.6 a cheap workhorse for anything that needs context but little autonomy: reading through and summarizing an entire repository once, mapping legacy code, generating migration lists. For an agent left alone in a terminal for hours, I wouldn't deploy it at 26%. And factor in the doubling above 200,000 tokens — in practice that's a 200,000-token window with an expensive tail.

This Week's Paper: The Setup Beats the Model

On Aug 19, 2026, a test called Reconstruction appeared on arXiv. The task is devilishly well designed: a model gets only the reference list of a scientific paper — before that paper's publication — and has to reconstruct the actual research idea from it. 643 papers evaluated, six fields, seven flagship models. Safeguarded against memorization: citation cutoff date, anonymized source identifiers, frozen reference lists.

The solo result is sobering: 3 to 15 percent hit rate. The result with the setup is the news: 23 to 42 percent — per the paper, roughly 2.4x the best single model, and consistently across all six fields.

Cross-review and tournament selection: Instead of letting one model answer once, you let several answer — then have a different model, one that didn't write any of the answers, grade them. After that, the candidates face off in pairs like a chess tournament until one is left standing. Like a jury instead of a single grader, plus elimination rounds. The trick isn't more compute — it's that nobody grades their own work.

Important caveat, and I'm not smoothing it over: this is not a coding benchmark. Reconstructing a research idea isn't the same as building a feature, and the numbers don't transfer to SWE-Bench. What does transfer is the structure of the finding — and it lines up exactly with what's in the SWE-Bench column above. Where models differ by half a point but a review-and-selection step more than doubles the hit rate, the lever clearly no longer sits in model choice.

The other half of the honesty: even 42 percent means the majority still misses. The setup turns a hopeless task into a hard one — not a hard task into a solved one.

Try it You can build this into your own setup with no new tooling: for a hard refactor, don't generate one plan — generate three, then have a second model evaluate the three and pick one. Costs three times the tokens for the cheap part (the plan) and saves the expensive round (the wrong implementation).

Anthropic's Quarter: The End of the Subsidized Era

Anthropic has preliminarily reported over $11.5B in revenue for Q2 2026 — versus $787M in the same quarter a year earlier, roughly a fourteenfold jump, and up from $4.73B in Q1. For the first time, a positive adjusted operating margin is on the books. The annualized run rate most recently cited, back in May, was around $47B.

Adjusted operating income: What's left of the core business after operating costs are deducted — but before some uncomfortable line items are counted against it, typically employee stock compensation, interest, and taxes. Like saying "I earned more than I spent this month" while leaving out the rent. It's not a balance sheet, it's a metric with omissions — and this one comes from internal documents ahead of a possible IPO, not from an audited statement.

Why this belongs in a coding brief: Daniel's workflow hangs off one of these providers. Two readings, both valid. The optimistic one: a provider that no longer needs to cross-subsidize every token is a provider that won't triple its prices overnight and cut off your tool. The cautious one: a company gearing up for an IPO optimizes margins — and the cheapest levers in an interface business are generous cache discounts, cheap pricing tiers, and usage caps.

Context The number isn't the news, the direction is: the phase where flagship models sold below cost is winding down. Keep the second path warm — a local or open model in the same workflow that you can switch on if needed. The 96.40% for DeepSeek-V4-Pro in the middle column is today's best argument for not putting that off any longer.

Article 50: What You Have to Label — and What's Explicitly Exempt

The labeling requirements of Article 50 of the EU AI Act have applied since Aug 2, 2026. Anyone delivering artificially generated or altered audio, image, video, or text content must mark it in a machine-readable way. The penalty range for violations: up to €15M or 3% of global annual revenue, whichever is higher.

Machine-readable marking: Not a visible watermark for the eye, but a notation software can read — comparable to the EXIF data in a photo that carries the camera model and timestamp without showing up in the image itself. The goal is that a platform or a verification tool can later automatically determine: a machine made this.

For a one-person shop, the exemptions are the real news. Per the legal analysis, explicitly exempt are: source code, machine-to-machine communication, very short outputs like single words or captions, and assistive editing functions that don't substantially alter the input. In plain terms: whatever Claude Code, Cursor, or Copilot writes into your file doesn't fall under this labeling requirement. A spelling or rephrasing assistant normally doesn't either.

What is affected is the other half of your work: any client application that generates marketing copy, product images, synthetic voices, or entire articles. Two dates belong on your calendar. Dec 2, 2026 — by then, systems already on the market before the cutoff must also comply. Feb 2, 2027 — from then on, watermarks are required to be detectable across vendors.

In practice If you're shipping a generation feature for a client this fall, clarify in writing before implementation who's on the hook for the labeling: you as the system builder, or the client as the operator. That's a contract question, not a ticket. And Dec 2 also applies to whatever you already shipped live in the spring — that's exactly where it tends to get overlooked.
