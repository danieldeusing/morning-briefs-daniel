Twenty Attempts Later: The Number That Explains Your Agent Gut Feeling

If you've ever had an agent run the same task twice and gotten two different outcomes — here's the measurement behind that. Thinkingbox, posted as a preprint on arXiv since Aug 20, is a sandbox plus benchmark for agents in stateful business workflows: 507 policy-bound workflows from retail, hospitality, auto insurance, neobank IT, and consulting, each run in an isolated, MCP-compatible tool session with full execution traces.

What's graded isn't the answer but the resulting state: verification scripts accept valid paths but reject wrong, missing, and excess effects. The result across proprietary and open models: the strongest reaches 65.36% pass@1 — and 25.25% pass^20.

pass@1 vs. pass^20: pass@1 means one attempt per task, and you count how often it lands. pass^20 means the same task twenty times — and it only counts if all twenty runs go cleanly. Like an electrician who wires up a single outlet flawlessly: great. The question for your whole house is whether he can do that on all twenty. 65 versus 25 translates to: the model can do two out of three tasks. Only one in four can it do reliably.

The sentence that really sticks with me is further down in the abstract: many failed runs end cleanly and with valid state-changing actions. The agent doesn't crash, doesn't throw an error, reports "done" — and still leaves behind the wrong state. Exactly for that reason, the authors write, signals at the response or tool-call level aren't a usable proxy for completed work.

My reading, and I'll defend it: this is the number that should replace SWE-Bench in your head. Yesterday this newsletter covered two trackers arguing over a 1.2-point gap at the top of a saturated board. This gap is forty points wide — and it describes exactly the experience of an agent loop that works in the demo and doesn't in production. The practical consequence: for anything that touches state — a database, tickets, deployments — the relevant question isn't "can the model do the task," it's "what happens on the twelfth run at 5:30 in the morning." Check the state, not the success message.

"Ox Alpha": Ten Tasks Aren't a Benchmark

Since Aug 20, OpenRouter has been hosting an anonymous model under stealth/ox-alpha: 1,048,576 tokens of context, accepting text, image, and video input, free during preview. A number is circulating alongside it: 80% on DeepSWE, versus 65% for Claude Fable 5 and 52% for GPT-5.6 Sol.

The number comes from a test with ten tasks, in which the models also received an unequal number of attempts. That's not a measurement, that's an anecdote with a percent sign — and, right after the section above, almost instructive: ten tasks with one attempt each are the extreme case of pass@1, exactly the metric that, per Thinkingbox, says the least about usability. Update: Zhipu AI itself confirmed the identity on Aug 26 — "Ox Alpha" is GLM-5.3-Flash, a 320-billion-parameter MoE model (18B active) tested anonymously on OpenCode and OpenRouter ahead of its official release. The circulating 80/65/52% figures from the ten-task test remain unverified regardless — the identity is settled now, the benchmark isn't.

Try Still, a free preview with a million tokens of context is a cheap afternoon. Throw your own repository at it instead of taking a stranger's ten tasks on faith. Just don't build anything on top of it — stealth models vanish along with the preview window, and the price afterward is unknown.

Agent Lightning v1.0: RL for the Harness You Already Have

The second preprint of the week (arXiv, Aug 18) is the more practical half of today's brief. The starting point: modern agents live inside a harness that manages tools, context, and control flow — and that harness has become an essential part of the system, not an accessory. Agent Lightning v1.0 hangs training behind an LLM-endpoint proxy: the harness keeps directing the environment loop, and the trainer only ever sees request-response pairs.

"Harnessed agentic RL": Reinforcement learning (RL) means the model tries something, gets only a "succeeded / failed" signal back at the end, and shifts its weights so the successful paths happen more often — learning to ride a bike without an instructor, you either fall over or you don't. The harnessed part in front of it is the real twist: what gets trained isn't a bare chat model, but the model operating inside the exact tool loop it will later work in. Your prompts, your tools, your stop conditions are part of the training instead of being a foreign environment the finished model gets thrown into afterward.

The result: with 6,000 training examples and, by the authors' own account, modest compute, Qwen3.5-9B climbs on SWE-bench Verified from 41.8% to 56.4% — 14.6 points in absolute terms. The framework itself is roughly 3,500 lines of code, and the complete pipeline for the coding agent has been published as a reproducible workflow with training scripts.

The necessary skepticism: these are the authors' own numbers from their own paper, not independently remeasured, and 56.4% is far below what the top models post on the same board. The point isn't the absolute value either, but the gap — and who produces it: a nine-billion-parameter model that runs locally on a reasonable GPU. Try If you're already running a fixed agent loop, this is the first RL approach in a long while that doesn't require rebuilding the harness for training. The traces your loop produces every day are the dataset.

Gemini 3.7 Flash: Half Price, With an Expiration Date

Google released Gemini 3.7 Flash on Aug 13 — three weeks after 3.6 Flash. A point-one version after twenty-one days isn't a new generation, it's a touch-up, and it's explicitly aimed at coding and agents.

The price is the real lever, and it comes with a catch: $0.75 per million input tokens, $3.75 per million output tokens — roughly half what the predecessor cost at launch. But only through Dec 31, 2026. Starting Jan 1, 2027, the list price is $1.50 and $7.50. Anyone budgeting an agent loop on this model right now is budgeting with a price that expires in four months — better to run the numbers at double the value from the start.

Gemini 3.7 Flash vs. 3.6 Flash — as reported by Google, as of Aug 13, 2026, not independently remeasured

The reported jumps are solid: DeepSWE v1.1 from 49.0 to 65.3%, FrontierCode 1.1 Main from 34.4 to 43.6%, AutomationBench from 17.0 to 30.4%, plus 50 more Elo points in the WebDev Arena (1,538 → 1,588). Two caveats apply. First: these are vendor numbers, relayed through trade press — no independent follow-up measurement. Second, two summaries cite 49.0% and 48.6% respectively for the same prior DeepSWE score; that doesn't change the size of the jump, but it shows how quickly a decimal point slips when a number gets passed along.

In practice: this isn't a model for heavy research, it's the candidate for the many cheap steps in a loop — scanning files, summarizing diffs, routine edits. That's exactly what the price is built for, and that's exactly why the date matters more than the benchmark.

Cloudflare Builds Agents a Browser — and a Wallet

Covered retroactively, because it was missing here so far and because the pieces belong together: Cloudflare rolled out its agent infrastructure in a single week in early August. Kitesurf arrived on Aug 6 — a "stateless, highly scalable, and cost-effective" web browser that runs entirely on Workers and is explicitly built for AI agents. Cloudflare puts the difference from Chromium at 3-7x less CPU and memory on typical agent tasks like screenshots and HTML extraction.

But the decisive sentence in the announcement is a different one: "Your existing clients already work." Kitesurf speaks CDP — the Chrome DevTools Protocol, the remote-control interface that Puppeteer, Playwright, and chrome-remote-interface use to drive a browser. Swapping it in, in the best case, costs an endpoint URL, not a rebuild.

Two days earlier, on Aug 4, came Cloudflare Wallets: through it, agents can "autonomously purchase APIs and content, within clear guardrails," via the protocol x402.

x402: HTTP has had a status code, 402 Payment Required, since the nineties that was never seriously used — the traffic light was there, nobody ever drove through it. x402 fills exactly that slot: the server responds "this costs money," the client pays automatically and repeats the request. For an agent, that means it can pay for an API without a human ever entering a credit card. Who's behind x402 and who sits in the associated consortium, I could only see today through secondary sources — so no member list here.

My take: the browser is the smaller story. What's interesting is that the agent's hands become billed infrastructure — viewing pages, buying things, both metered and guardrailed. For a solo developer, that's convenient at first glance. But it also means your agent runs on someone else's bill, with limits you don't set. The guardrails are the part I'd read before letting the browser impress me.

Meta Reverses Course Back to Open Weights

Meta's third coding move in four months, and the notable part is the reversal. Aug 5 brought Muse Code — a terminal coding agent in beta, with persistent asynchronous background subagents — and Muse Spark 1.2. Aug 10 followed with open weights for Muse Glimmer, a multimodal 30B model; Spark 1.2 is expected to go open as well. That reverses the closed-weight line Meta launched the Muse series with back in April.

Muse Code is priced by usage at $1.25 / $4.25 per million tokens — noticeably above Gemini 3.7 Flash, and in the range where you think twice before letting an agent run unsupervised. Conceptually, the persistent background subagents are the interesting part: the same shift visible everywhere this week — away from "one session, one task," toward state that outlives the session.
