62.5 Against 53.4

Alibaba released Qwen3.8-Flash-Next as open weights on Aug 26. The number that matters this week has only now made it into coverage: on SWE-bench Pro, the model scores 62.5 — "ahead of Claude Opus 4.6 Max's 53.4 and Qwen3.7-Plus's 55.8," as DataCamp reproduces the comparison table. The second data point is more dramatic: on DeepSWE 1.1, it's 58.7 against 42.2 for the 27B sibling model and 16.5 for predecessor Qwen3.7-Plus.

SWE-bench Pro — Vendor Comparison (Qwen), as of Aug 26, 2026
SWE-bench Pro (and why 62.5 isn't worse than 96): All SWE-bench variants do the same thing: they take real, closed GitHub bug reports, hand the model the project, and check whether its patch makes the tests pass. Like a driving test, only pass or fail counts — the score comes from the share of solved cases. "Pro" is the harder exam: longer, multi-file tasks, chosen specifically so they weren't already in the training data. That's why scores there sit at 50 to 60, while the same models score above 90 on "Verified." Two different exams, two different scales — putting 62.5 and 96% side by side is like comparing a GPA to a driving-test score.

On the model itself: 125B weights total, of which around 6B are active per token (the mechanics behind that ran here yesterday), 262,144 tokens of native context, stretchable to 1M via YaRN. For local use, the same caveat still applies — all 125B weights have to sit fully in memory, regardless of how few of them actually do the math.

Skepticism, where it belongs. This is a vendor table: Alibaba chose the comparison partner, ran the test setup, and reported both numbers; no independent follow-up exists anywhere yet. And the jump on DeepSWE 1.1 from 16.5 to 58.7 within a single generation is the kind of curve where you ask about the test setup first, and about the model only after.

My take: the interesting part isn't the leaderboard position, it's who Alibaba chose as the yardstick. Claude Opus 4.6 isn't an arbitrary comparison — it's one of the six models GitHub is removing from Copilot on Tuesday. So the open weights are measuring themselves right now against the flagship class that the closed vendors are themselves retiring this week. That's not "open source has caught up." It's that the gap has shrunk to about one model generation — and a generation currently lasts roughly one quarter.

Last Day — The Shutdown List, Compact

Short, because this has run here twice already — but today is the day it actually matters. Two dates, two vendors, one weekend already behind us.

Tomorrow, Aug 31 — Codex. gpt-5.4 and gpt-5.4-mini disappear from Codex for anyone working via ChatGPT login; replacements are gpt-5.6-terra and gpt-5.6-luna. Verbatim: "will remain available on the OpenAI API and Codex sessions authenticated with an API key." If you've set up your own key, you won't notice anything tomorrow.

The day after, Sep 1 — Copilot. Six models across every Copilot surface: Gemini 3.1 Pro → Gemini 3.6 Flash, Claude Opus 4.5 and 4.6 → Opus 4.7/4.8/5, Claude Sonnet 4.5 → Sonnet 5, Sonnet 4.6 → Sonnet 5 (stays available to individual users on annual plans), Raptor Mini → MAI-Code-1-Flash. The date comes from GitHub's own changelog; several secondary sources still cite Aug 31 — that's wrong.

The Trap in the Replacement GitHub names MAI-Code-1-Flash as the successor for Raptor Mini — and then deprecates that very model itself nine days later: "we will deprecate MAI-Code-1-Flash across all GitHub Copilot experiences on September 10, 2026," with MAI-Code-1.1-Flash as the replacement. If you follow the intermediate step, you're moving twice. Switch straight to MAI-Code-1.1-Flash.

What's still worth doing today: a search through everything that carries a model name as a string — CI definitions, .env files, editor and agent configs, scripts with direct API calls. Look for: gpt-5.4, opus-4-5, opus-4-6, sonnet-4-5, sonnet-4-6, gemini-3.1-pro, raptor-mini.

GLM-5.3-Flash Gets Its Independent Number

This brief flagged an open bracket on Aug 27: Zhipu's GLM-5.3-Flash had been identified, but every performance figure came from the vendor. That bracket is now closed. Artificial Analysis puts the model at an Intelligence Index of 57 — "tying with Claude Opus 4.8 and surpassing DeepSeek's flagship model V4 Pro, which scored 53." Alongside that, a cost of $0.09 per task. AA data as of Aug 29.

Independent test bench: The difference between a vendor figure and an independent one is the same as between a carmaker's factory fuel-economy rating and the number a test institute measures on its own track. The manufacturer gets to choose tire pressure, route, and driving style — that's not lying, it's just optimizing everything it's allowed to choose. An institute like Artificial Analysis runs every model over the same course, with the same tasks, the same time limits, the same toolset. That's why the absolute numbers there are often lower than the vendor's — and why, for the first time, the ranking actually means something.

The vendor numbers, of course, stay vendor numbers: Zhipu cites, for the same model, Terminal-Bench 2.1: 84.3%, Toolathlon: 78.4%, CharXiv-R 89.4%, and MMVU 80.5%. Those stand here for what they are — a self-reported figure nobody has followed up on. On the architecture: 320B weights total, 18B active per token.

My take: the most telling number in the whole section is the last one — $0.09 per task. An index tie with Claude Opus 4.8 is a snapshot that will look different in six weeks. A price per task is a statement about structure: when the same work costs multiple times more or less between two vendors, it's the invoice — not the ranking — that decides for batch work, background agents, and anything that isn't waiting on an answer. The place to check that isn't a leaderboard, it's your own repository, two candidates, and two hours.

The Top — And Three Lists That Can't Agree

On SWE-bench Verified, Claude Opus 5 sits on top. Two independent trackers agree on the order but not the decimal: vals.ai writes "Claude Opus 5 leads at 97.00%," while BenchLM, as of 08/29, says "96%, followed by Claude Mythos 5 (95.5%) and Claude Fable 5 (95%)." The authority that could settle this stays silent — swebench.com loads its Verified table via JavaScript and again delivered not a single number today. Both figures are secondary-sourced, not primary-confirmed.

On Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index, it all lines up: Opus 5 at 63.0% ahead of Fable 5 (62.1%) and Grok 4.6 (60.9%), out of 183 listed models; on the Agentic Index, Opus 5 leads at 55.3. Combined with the figure from the neighboring section, that gives a continuous ladder — Opus 5 at 63, Grok 4.6 at 60.9, Opus 4.8 and GLM-5.3-Flash both at 57, DeepSeek V4 Pro at 53 — even though the two ends come from two different renderings of the same source.

LiveCodeBench is the counter-evidence to trusting any decimal. Three trackers, three different leaders, all dated in August: llm-stats has Claude Fable 5 at 89.78% (as of Aug 19), a second rendering names Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview at 91.7 as of Aug 25, and BenchLM puts Qwen3.7 Max on top at 91.6%. Here too, the official board loads its table via JavaScript and today showed nothing but "Loading leaderboard data…"

Contamination: If the exam questions were already in the textbook before the exam, the grade measures memory, not ability. That's exactly what happens to coding benchmarks: their tasks come from public code, and that code ends up in the next training run. LiveCodeBench fights this by continuously adding fresh tasks and slicing time windows — which is why it matters which window a tracker is measuring. Two lists with different windows can both be correct and still name different winners.

There's also one standalone figure I'm explicitly flagging as unverified: on Terminal-Bench 2.1, GPT-5.6 Sol is said to reach 89.5% at its highest reasoning level, and Claude Opus 5 89.1% at its maximum level — putting the default models of the two most widely used agents within half a point of each other. That comes from a single aggregator and couldn't be cross-checked anywhere today.

My take, and I'll defend it: the ranking is the information, the decimal isn't. Two trackers that differ by a point on Opus 5 are saying the same thing. Three trackers that crown three different leaders are saying: this board currently isn't a basis for a decision. If you need to replace a model in your tooling this week, don't base the choice on a percentage that three sources write differently.

Standing Thread: Three Open Families, One Number

Alongside Qwen and Zhipu, DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 from Jul 31 is still standing — 284B weights, 13B active per token, 1M context, weights under an MIT license on Hugging Face. Its own measurements: Terminal-Bench 2.1: 82.7%, Cybergym 76.7%, Toolathlon-Verified 70.3%, DSBench-FullStack 68.7%, DSBench-Hard 59.6% — all under its own test rig ("DeepSeek Harness, to be released"), so without outside verification.

This gets interesting side by side. On the benchmark of the same name, two open families report 82.7% and 84.3%, while the closed-model leader is supposedly around 89%. That looks like a five-to-seven-point gap — but all three numbers come from three different test rigs, and one of them rests on a single aggregator. The honest way to put it: same benchmark name, three different measurements — so more a direction than a distance.

None of these runs locally today. 125B, 284B, 320B weights all have to sit fully in memory, no matter how few experts do the math per token. On your own hardware, it's still the small releases that count.

MCP on the side: the protocol reports "close to half-a-billion downloads a month" for its first-party SDKs, with TypeScript and Python each past 1B total. Self-reported from the project blog — as an order of magnitude, it explains why MCP servers are now treated as ordinary infrastructure.
