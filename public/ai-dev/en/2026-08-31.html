OpenAI Cancels Cursor Effective November 12

OpenAI has told SpaceX it's ending the contract that supplies Cursor with OpenAI models. Verbatim, from the Aug 29 coverage: "The proposed shutoff date is November 12, 2026, which OpenAI said is the maximum notice its custom agreement allows." The reasoning is neither technical nor commercial: OpenAI "cannot be confident SpaceX will use its technology within its terms of service, citing experience with Elon Musk's companies violating contracts."

The timing is explained by the contract itself. SpaceX's acquisition of Cursor took effect on Aug 14; the special agreement allows termination only within a limited window after a change of ownership. That's why this news lands now and not back in June, when the acquisition was announced — and why the long notice period running to November isn't a courtesy, it's the maximum the clause allows.

Who actually supplies the intelligence in your editor: An AI editor is a restaurant, not a farm. Cursor writes the menu, sets the table, and takes the order — but the ingredients come from four different farms: Anthropic, Google, OpenAI, SpaceXAI. When one farm cancels its supply contract, dishes vanish from the menu overnight, even though nobody in the kitchen did anything wrong. That's exactly what's happening here: neither the model nor the editor got worse — a supply relationship simply ended.

What that means in practice. The scale involved is modest: OpenAI models reportedly serve roughly 5% of Cursor's traffic — the figure is attributed to Cursor CEO Michael Truell, but it appears in only a single secondary source and isn't primary-confirmed. That same source names three workarounds — your own OpenAI key in Cursor's settings, OpenAI's separate Codex extension, or an upstream gateway — and immediately qualifies them: they work for local chat and agent sessions, but not for tab completion, auto-routing, cloud agents, CLI, and SDK. Anthropic is said to have committed to providing more compute capacity for Claude models in Cursor; that too is single-sourced and, for now, belongs in the category "reported, not confirmed."

My take. The five percent is the least interesting number in this story. What's remarkable is the reasoning: a vendor is shutting off its own distribution channel because it doesn't trust the customer's owner. For you as a user, that means a model's availability in your tool hangs on a variable you can't observe and can't influence — one that doesn't announce itself by some number slowly getting worse. It flips on a Saturday afternoon, by press release.

Three Terminations, One Pattern

Line up the three events of this summer side by side, and a blueprint emerges. On Jun 18, Google discontinued the Gemini CLI for free, Pro, and Ultra users and pushed everyone to Antigravity CLI — "we can serve you best by pouring our energy into a single product built for today's multi-agent reality"; only Enterprise and Standard licenses keep the old path. Continue.dev, the open Copilot alternative, was acquired by Cursor in June and has been history since — which makes the ownership chain of that code today Continue → Cursor → SpaceX. And now a model vendor is canceling its own distribution channel because it doesn't like the buyer.

Owner changes → Supply contract becomes cancellable → Model disappears → your agent breaks

Not one step in that chain has anything to do with quality. Which is exactly why no benchmark helps against it: no percentage in the world predicts whether a model will still be in your tool four months from now.

AI gateway: A waystation between your code and the model providers. Instead of knocking directly on OpenAI's, Anthropic's, or Google's door, you talk to one address, and it forwards the request — to whom is decided in a config file. Like a travel plug adapter: the device stays the same, the outlet changes by country, and you only swap the attachment. The cost is one more place where something can break; the payoff is that switching vendors costs a line of configuration instead of a rebuild.

What makes a setup durable is unglamorous and checkable today: (1) Model names live in configuration, never in code — anyone with claude-opus-4-6 hardcoded in a loop will spend tomorrow morning searching files instead of settings. (2) Two vendors for the same task, actually tested at least once, not just entered somewhere. (3) Access runs through your own key or a gateway, not exclusively through the tool's subscription — that's exactly the difference that, at Cursor in November, decides between "keeps running" and "stalls." (4) A local fallback model for when everything jams at once; it doesn't have to be good, it has to run.

My take, and I'll defend it: choosing a tool has become less a matter of taste than a matter of supply chain. That said, I wouldn't switch tools over this — Cursor remains a fully capable editor with Anthropic, Google, and SpaceXAI models. But I'd stop treating the model picker as a product feature. It's a snapshot of someone else's contract status, and the three events of this summer show that it changes faster than the models inside it.

MiniMax on Bedrock: the 4 Million Figure Doesn't Exist

A number has been circulating through secondary sources for days: that MiniMax is available on AWS Bedrock with 4M tokens of context. It isn't in the primary source. AWS's own model card for MiniMax-M2.5 simply states "Context window: 196K tokens," launch date Feb 12, 2026, status active. AWS's machine learning blog lists the full family: "M2 (1M token context), M2.1 (196K tokens), M2.5 (196K tokens)," with 230B weights total and 10B active per token. So the only million belongs to the oldest model in the lineup — the gap between the rumored figure and the documented one is a factor of about twenty.

Context window: How much text the model can hold "in mind" at once — the task, source files, prior responses, all combined. Like a desk: whatever doesn't fit has to go in the cabinet, and the model can't see what's in the cabinet while it's answering. 196K tokens is roughly a mid-sized project directory, 1M is closer to an entire repository. For agents working on a task for hours at a stretch, this is the metric that makes or breaks the choice — which is why a factor of 20 here isn't a rounding error.

M2.5 still stays interesting for this section on its merits: AWS describes it as "trained specifically for agent-native execution … tool-calling, multi-step task decomposition, and long-horizon coding tasks" — explicitly built for software work. Try it If you're already on Bedrock, this gets you an agent-oriented model without a new vendor contract — just with a desk sized 196K, not a warehouse.

Two things here are more striking than the ranking itself. First, all five top entries are Anthropic models — which says more about how this list is populated than about the field. Second, Claude Opus 5 is missing from it entirely, even though two other trackers put it on top yesterday at 96% and 97% respectively. A second pull of the same benchmark on the same day gives exactly that order instead — Opus 5 at 96%, Mythos 5 at 95.5%, Fable 5 at 95%, followed by Kimi K3 (93.4) and GPT-5.6 Luna (93.0) — and describes the board as saturated, since the top five sit within four points of each other.

The official board could settle this. It still doesn't: swebench.com loads its Verified table via JavaScript and, on this run too, delivered not a single number. The same goes for livecodebench.github.io. For both leaderboards, today, there are only reproductions — no primary measurement.

My take. When two pulls of the same benchmark on the same day show not just different decimal places but a different set of models, the question isn't "which value is correct" anymore — it's "what was even measured here." An aggregator can simply not have added a model yet — that's not fraud, but it makes the list worthless as a ranking. In practice, that means these numbers support the claim "the top is tightly bunched," and nothing beyond that. If you need to replace a model this week, decide it in your own repository with two candidates and two hours — not on a board that can't agree with itself.
