Poolside Puts a 118-Billion-Parameter Model Out for the Taking

On Jul 21, Poolside released Laguna S 2.1 — an open-weight coding model. The key figures from the announcement post: a "118B total parameter Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model with 8B activated parameters per token," a context window of up to 1M tokens "in thinking and no-thinking modes," shipped under the OpenMDW-1.1 license.

Mixture-of-Experts (118B total, 8B active): Picture a large law firm with a hundred specialist attorneys. For your question, all hundred are in the building — but only two get called in. You pay for the knowledge of the whole firm, but the working hours of two. That's exactly how an MoE model computes: 118 billion weights have to sit in memory, but for each word it generates, only a one-eighth slice of 8 billion actually does the work. In practice, that means: the memory footprint is that of a giant model, the speed that of a small one. At your desk, the first number is the hurdle, not the second.

The second number worth talking about is the license. "Open weights" isn't the same as "open source" in the usual sense: you get the finished, trained file to download and run, not the training data and not the build recipe. Whether you're allowed to build a product on it is spelled out in the license terms — I didn't cross-check the exact text of OpenMDW-1.1 in this run; I'm only naming the label Poolside itself gives it. Anyone planning to build on it should read the license themselves first.

And now the benchmarks — with the handbrake on. Poolside cites four figures, all from its own measurements, none of them confirmed on an independent leaderboard:

Laguna S 2.1 — four benchmarks, all vendor-reported (Jul 21, 2026)

The bar chart isn't a ranking, it's a warning: one model, on one day, measured against four boards — and the spread runs from 78.5% down to 40.4%. Nearly a factor of two. Which of these numbers ends up in a headline isn't decided by the model, but by which board you pick.

My take. The interesting news here isn't the top score, it's that this model exists to download at all. A coding model of this size with open weights is the one architecture where the supply-chain problem from yesterday's brief — vendor cancels, model vanishes from the tool — simply doesn't apply: nobody can shut off what's sitting on your own drive with a press release. Try it But to be honest about the hurdle: 118 billion weights don't fit on a laptop. This is a candidate for a rented machine or a vendor who hosts it — not for an evening Ollama run.

$143 Million to Read the Code

On Aug 12, CodeRabbit announced a Series C of $143M at a $1.5B valuation, led by Atomico and Smash Capital, with BMW i Ventures, Datadog, and SineWave Ventures among the participants. The usage figures from the same announcement: the company "conducts more than 2 million code reviews each week and counts more than 17,000 customers," with revenue growth of "more than 5x year-over-year."

Automated code review: A program that reads your change before a human does, and writes comments on the affected lines — like an editor handing back a manuscript with notes in the margin. The difference from classic checking tools (linters, type checks): those look for known patterns in a file, a model reviewer tries to understand the intent of the change and will flag things even where the syntax is perfectly fine. It doesn't replace a human. It makes one faster — and with fifty changes a day, review speed is the whole problem.

The headline of the announcement states the purpose more plainly than any analysis could: the money exists to govern AI-generated code. And the math behind it is simple. If your team now submits three to five times as many lines because an agent writes them, the bottleneck doesn't shift a little, it shifts completely: from typing to checking. Two million reviews a week aren't proof of revenue, but they are proof of volume — somebody out there has a whole lot to check.

My take, and I'll defend it. The review layer is right now the most honest indicator of how much code agents actually produce. Vendor figures on coding-assistant usage are notoriously soft ("users," "requests," "accepted suggestions"). But someone who raises capital to control the output afterward has no incentive to overstate the volume — they have to service it. If you want to judge for yourself how serious the agent wave really is, this is a more sensible place to look than adoption slides.

OpenAI Builds a Keyboard That Watches Agents Think

OpenAI's first piece of hardware is neither a phone nor a speaker, but a $230 macropad built with keyboard maker Work Louder: the Codex Micro. It's described as a "$230 mini-keyboard" with "13 mechanical switches," plus a rotary dial, a small joystick, and a touch pad. Six of the keys are the actual idea — "six frosted 'agent keys' show live Codex thread status via RGB colors: white idle, blue thinking, green complete, amber input needed, red errors."

Reports cite mid-July as the launch window and a sellout within 24 hours; an exact launch date wasn't on any of the pages reachable today — several trade sites returned HTTP 403 — but the price and key count are confirmed across multiple independent reports.

You launch 5 agents → 4 running, 1 waiting on you → which one?

My take. It would be easy to dismiss this as a gimmick, and it isn't a buy recommendation either — $230 for six status lights is a stretch, and you get the same effect from a terminal status line for free. What's notable, though, is why the thing exists. It doesn't solve an input problem, it solves an attention problem: past three parallel agents, you lose track of which one is actually stuck. That OpenAI, of all companies, builds hardware for this first — and not for faster typing — says fairly precisely where the company thinks its own users' bottleneck sits. And the sellout suggests they're not entirely wrong.

Demonstrate Instead of Document — If It's Even True

The story that would touch Daniel's day-to-day most directly is also the worst-sourced one. Since Jul 21, the desktop app Claude Cowork is said to have a feature called "Record a Skill," available on Pro, Max, and Team accounts, not on the free tier. The method: "record your screen while you do a task, talk through it as you go, and Claude turns it into a skill it can run again" — which would remove the previous requirement to write a SKILL.md by hand.

Skill (in Claude): A stored work instruction the assistant pulls out on its own at the right moment — "this is how we do the monthly close here," "this is what a release process looks like for us." Until now, you write it out like a recipe. The new idea is the difference between a cookbook and standing next to someone and watching: you demonstrate it once while talking through it, and the system writes the recipe. The catch is the same as with watching — whatever you only do in your head and don't say out loud never makes it into the recipe.

And now the caveat I'm not going to smooth over. This story hangs on one source, and it's a news aggregator. Anthropic's own newsroom lists, for the past few weeks, "Improving our alignment and security efforts" (Aug 31), "Previewing the Model Hardware Standard" (Aug 27), and "Expanding our support for scientists" (Aug 27) — no post about Cowork skills. The documentation page where something like this should live redirects and ends in a 404. That doesn't mean the feature doesn't exist; product features often ship without a press release. It means: the date and the tier are unconfirmed here, and if you need them, check your own app instead of this brief.

Google Contradicts Google

Two pages, one vendor, opposite statements, both reachable today. The Google developer blog writes: "On June 18, 2026, Gemini CLI and Gemini Code Assist IDE extensions will stop serving requests for Google AI Pro and Ultra, as well as those using it free of charge using Gemini Code Assist for individuals." So free use ended two and a half months ago, with the Antigravity CLI as the replacement. The quota page at geminicli.com, meanwhile, still lists a free line unchanged: 1,000 requests per user per day via Google account sign-in. The page self-dates to that same Jun 18, 2026.

My take. I'm treating this as exactly what it is — an open contradiction in the vendor's own documentation, not a new free tier. Anyone reading the 1,000 figure today as a current offer is probably reading a page that never got updated. I wouldn't rely on either number; the shutdown announcement is the more specific and the more recent statement, and free quotas, in my experience, tend to get pulled back rather than quietly extended.
