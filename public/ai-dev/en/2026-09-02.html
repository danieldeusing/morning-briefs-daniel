320 Billion Parameters for 25 Cents — Until September 9

On Aug 26, 2026, according to its own release notes, Z.ai released GLM-5.3-Flash: natively multimodal, 320B parameters with 18B active, weights under an MIT license on HuggingFace. The context window is listed at 1M tokens — though that figure appears only in the secondary source. Having 18 of 320 billion active means Mixture-of-Experts: all the weights have to sit in memory, but only a slice of them computes for each generated word. This doesn't run on a laptop.

The real point is the price. List: $0.15 per million input tokens, $0.03 for cached input, $0.50 for output. Until Sep 9, 2026, 16:00 UTC, half of that applies — $0.075 / $0.015 / $0.25.

What "cached input" is: Billing is by text volume, split between what you send in and what comes out — output is a lot more expensive. "Cached input" is the discount for repetition: if you send the same 40 files with every agent step, the vendor doesn't have to reprocess that block each time — like a copy shop that already has the template loaded. Here, repeated context costs a fifth of fresh context. This is the line item that drives the bill in agentic work — not the clever answer at the end.

And now the benchmarks, with the handbrake on. For the jump from GLM-5.2 to 5.3-Flash, Z.ai cites: DeepSWE 63.4 versus 46.2, AutomationBench 48.8 versus 26.2, Toolathlon 78.4 versus 59.9, Terminal Bench 2.1 84.3 versus 81.0. The citing source explicitly notes that these figures are self-reported by the vendor and not independently verified — and the model doesn't show up on any independent leaderboard I could reach today.

My take. Try it Not for the benchmark figures, but for the MIT license plus a promo price with a hard end date: if you have an agentic workflow that burns through a lot of context, this week you can find out for a few dollars whether the model carries the grunt work — writing tests, renaming things, migrations. The license is the real insurance: when the price jumps on Sep 9, the weights are still there to download. Just not onto your laptop.

DeepSeek Leaves the Preview — and the Bargain Tier

DeepSeek V4 Pro has been generally available since Aug 12/13, 2026; the build "V4 Pro 0813" ends a preview that had been running since April. Technically: up to 1M tokens of context, up to 384,000 output tokens in a single response, thinking and non-thinking modes. The API now understands OpenAI's Responses format and ships with Codex connectivity — existing tools often just need to swap the base URL.

The real news is in the fine print: since Aug 16, peak-time output tokens cost $3.96 per million instead of a flat $0.87. More than four and a half times as much — and the end of the era in which DeepSeek was always the cheapest answer.

Two self-reported scorecards, side by side. DeepSeek cites Terminal Bench 2.1 at 87.9, DeepSWE at 62.7, and NL2Repo at 61.5 for V4 Pro. Z.ai cites 84.3 on the same Terminal Bench and 63.4 on DeepSWE for GLM-5.3-Flash. Both sets come from the vendors themselves, each with its own setup — comparable only in the way two self-reports ever are. Laid side by side, though: about a 3.6-point gap, against a 16x difference in output pricing in Z.ai's favor.

$0.87/M flat → since Aug 16 → $3.96/M at peak

My take. Time-zone-dependent pricing is more honest than it looks: compute really is scarcer during the day. For you in Brazil, it actually works out well — Chinese peak hours fall in the middle of your night. But if you let agents run unsupervised, you should know when they kick off.

Plus 25 Percent That's Actually Minus 17

As of Sep 14, 2026, Anthropic is raising Claude Code's weekly limits: +25% on the permanent base, for Pro, Max, Team, and seat-based Enterprise. Sounds like a gift. It isn't. Because since May 13, a temporary 50% surcharge has been running, extended multiple times — and it ends with the same announcement.

Base 100 → today 150 (with surcharge) → as of Sep 14: 125

Run the numbers once and the confusion stops: if you have 150 units today, you'll have 125 as of Sep 14 — roughly 17% less than now. At the same time, "25% more" isn't a lie, it just refers to a different baseline. Both numbers are correct. Only one describes what you'll notice on Sep 15.

My take, and I'll defend it. The move itself is defensible: a temporary surcharge, extended four times, is allowed to expire, and a raised base is more honest than an endless extension. What I'd criticize is the reference point in the communication. If you've gotten your users used to a quota for four and a half months, you have to quantify the change against that quota, not against a base nobody has experienced since May. In practice: if you've recently been hitting the weekly limit regularly, you'll hit it sooner starting Sep 14 — and that's a Monday, so it hits a full work week right away. A good reason to spend the next two weeks tracking which runs actually eat into the quota.

Self-Hosted Doesn't Mean Self-Contained

Since Aug 6, 2026, self-hosted environments for Claude Code have existed as an open beta — Team and Enterprise, off by default, in two operating modes (always-on and on-demand runners). Skim the announcement and you'll come away thinking "Claude Code now runs in-house for us." That's not quite right.

Runner vs. inference: An agentic coding tool does two things. First, thinking — that's the model, in Anthropic's data center. Second, acting — reading files, running tests, executing commands; that happens on a machine, the runner. "Self-hosted" only covers the second half: you bring the workshop into your own building, but the master craftsman stays on the phone. Prompts, responses, and tool results, per reports, still go to Anthropic for inference.

My take. It's still useful — just for a different problem. It solves access: the agent gets to work behind the firewall, on the internal network, against the database. It does not solve confidentiality toward the vendor. If you're touching this beta because of a data-protection requirement, get that boundary in writing before you promise it to a client.

A Third Stop Buying the Software

A number from McKinsey's "The State of AI: Global Survey 2026" has been circulating in the press since yesterday: 32% of organizations surveyed decided against buying at least one software product because they could build it themselves with coding agents. Among "high performers" — companies attributing at least 5% of their operating profit to AI, 6% of respondents per the survey — it's nearly 50%, versus 31% for everyone else.

First, the caveat. The figure comes from a secondary source; McKinsey's own page timed out, and I couldn't find a sample size or survey period anywhere. And the 32% doesn't mean a third of software spending disappears — only that at a third of the organizations, at least one purchase decision went differently.

My take, and it's the uncomfortable one. For an independent developer, this isn't market-research trivia — it's a description of your own demand curve. The client who used to buy a small tool — an import mask, a report generator, an interface between two systems — now tries building it themselves first. Exactly the class of job a solo developer delivers well. But the reverse is just as real: whatever a business unit cobbles together with agents in two afternoons is afterward unmaintained, untested, and owned by nobody. I'd bet the work migrates there — not "I'll build this for you," but "I'll make what you built production-ready."

Two Leaderboards, Two Winners

If you want to know today which model leads SWE-Bench Verified, you'll get a different answer depending on the board — and not just slightly different.

SWE-Bench Verified: A collection of real, already-solved bug reports from open-source projects. The model gets the report and the code directory and has to write a patch itself; success counts only if the test suite comes back green afterward. "Verified" means humans checked the tasks for solvability beforehand. Closer to real work than a quiz — but it's still an exam with a known pool of questions, and anyone who trains for it gets better at it without getting better at programming.

The order at the very top is worthless for a purchasing decision. What's still useful is the bottom edge — 60% is noticeably worse than 88%. Anything above that, you're better off deciding with your own code than with someone else's table.
