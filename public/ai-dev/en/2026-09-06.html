Astra, measured from the outside for the first time

What the test bench says when the maker isn't the one writing the table

Update since yesterday GPT-6 Astra launched on Thursday, 09/03 — staggered, first to participants in the Daybreak program, then "in the coming days" to ChatGPT Plus, Pro, Business, and Enterprise, plus the API and AWS. Yesterday's brief covered why access is so tightly controlled: Astra is the company's first model to cross the "critical" tier for cybersecurity. Today brings the other half — the first measurement that doesn't come from OpenAI.

An "index" in benchmarking: not a single test, but an average across several. Like a report-card grade: math, English, and gym all get folded into one number. Artificial Analysis runs two of these — a Coding Agent Index (Deep SWE, Terminal Bench, repo questions) and a broader Intelligence Index. The upside: individual outliers carry less weight. The downside: a single number no longer tells you what a model was actually good at.

On the Coding Agent Index, Astra — run inside OpenAI's own Codex harness — scores 67 points. That puts it roughly level with Claude Opus 5. The top spot belongs to Claude Fable 5.1 in Claude Code, at 70. And the most important footnote comes bundled right in: what's measured is never a model alone, but always model plus harness. The same model in a different harness produces a different number. Anyone reading "Model A beats Model B" out of tables like this is reading more into it than is there.

On the broader Intelligence Index, the result is even more sobering: 61 points — tied exactly with its predecessor, GPT-5.6 Sol. Claude Fable 5.1 sits five points above at 66, Gemini 3.8 Flash at 59. A generational jump that doesn't move the aggregate score is a very specific signal: the progress isn't spread across the board, it's concentrated in a handful of disciplines.

Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index — as of 09/03/2026

Two numbers get lost in the shuffle that interest me more than the index does: for coding, Astra needs roughly a third of the tokens its predecessor did for the same result, and Artificial Analysis measures the hallucination rate at maximum effort at 51% instead of 92%. Both are efficiency and reliability gains, not capability gains — and in the daily life of an agent making twenty tool calls in a row, that's exactly what counts.

The leap happens outside the editor

The one number where Astra really pulls ahead isn't a coding number. On an offline subset of OSWorld 2.0 — a test where the model operates a real desktop, clicking windows, finding menus, saving files — Astra scores 72.6% versus 65.7% for its predecessor, and does it in roughly 40 instead of 75 minutes per task, about 47% less time.

That's a different genre from "write me this function." It's the ability to operate programs that don't have a proper API. Reports say OpenAI demoed Astra controlling Excel, Blender, Power BI, and KiCad — though that list comes from search-result summaries, not a page I loaded myself; OpenAI's own announcement page returns HTTP 403 on request. Worth trying If you regularly fight a tool with no API — an old ERP front end, a CAD program, a reporting tool — this is the one area where Astra can do something this year that your Claude Code setup can't.

The price question: 57% cheaper or 75% more expensive?

Two sourced numbers that contradict each other — and why both can be right

OpenAI writes that Astra's most powerful configuration beats GPT-5.6 Sol's best setting at roughly 57% lower estimated API cost per task. Artificial Analysis measures the same model and, at maximum effort, gets 75% higher cost per task than its predecessor. That's not an error on either side. They're two different denominators.

different test→ different effort level→ different cost-per-task figure

OpenAI's figure rests on DeepSWE v1.1 and compares each model's best-placed configuration — there, Astra's higher token efficiency saves more than the higher price costs. Artificial Analysis calculates across its own index at maximum effort, where the model thinks more and the list price hits in full. And that price has gone up: 2.5x its predecessor's, from $4/$20 to $10/$50 per million input and output tokens.

Practical Don't adopt either number as your own. "Cost per task" isn't a property of the model — it's your workload times your effort level. If you're budgeting, the only reliable figure is the list price, and Astra's has gone up by a factor of 2.5. Everything below that you have to measure against your own task mix.

The competitor was already there — at the same list price

Update since Thu Anthropic already released Claude Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 on 09/01, three days before Astra. That makes the price comparison unusually clean: Fable 5.1 also costs $10 per million input and $50 per million output tokens — identical to Astra. Except at that price, Anthropic got cheaper: roughly 25% less than Fable 5 for typical workloads, often up to 45% for heavily agentic work.

Cache reads (prompt caching): In a long session, your tool keeps resending half the conversation history. The provider sets that recurring part aside instead of reprocessing it every time — like a waiter who remembers that table 4 always orders the same thing. Anthropic cut the price for this by 75%, to $0.25 per million tokens. In an agent run with many rounds, this is exactly the biggest line item.

On the measurement side: according to Anthropic, Fable 5.1 scores 55.8% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 — a test where the model works exclusively through a command line — 73.4% on CursorBench 3.2.0 (maximum effort), and 52.6% on Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1. The smaller Mythos 5.1 beats Fable 5.1 on Terminal-Bench at 60.9%, which explicitly breaks the "bigger model = better" ranking for terminal work. Astra's Terminal-Bench figure is circulating at 57.9% versus 37.3% for Sol; I only have this comparison from search results citing Artificial Analysis, not from a loaded table — treat it as a ballpark, not a measurement.

My take: if you work with Claude Code, there's no reason to switch this week. Astra doesn't lead in any of the independently available coding measurements, it costs the same per token, and where it genuinely leads — operating a computer — isn't where you work anyway. The flip side also holds: if you're already in the OpenAI ecosystem, Astra isn't a step back — it's the same value at a third of the tokens.

What's on SWE-Bench Verified — and what isn't

The test Daniel checks first for coding models has nothing to say about Astra: the model isn't listed on the Verified board. Making things worse, swebench.com loads its table via JavaScript — a fetch returns an empty shell with no numbers. What I have are two secondary sources (llm-stats.com and vals.ai) that agree within about a percentage point: Claude Opus 5 leads at 96–97%, Mythos 5 at roughly 95.5%, Fable 5 at roughly 95%, as of 09/02/2026.

Benchmark hygiene OpenAI's own numbers for Astra sound impressive — FrontierMath Tier 4 v2 97.6%, BenchCAD 95.9%, GPQA Diamond 96%, ARC-AGI-3 98.6%. All four are vendor-reported and haven't been independently re-measured by anyone so far. That's not an accusation — it's the normal state of affairs in week one. It just means: these numbers don't belong in the same column as a board entry, and a model missing from the independent board doesn't have a value there yet — not even a bad one.

OpenAI cuts off Cursor's model access on 11/12

OpenAI is pulling its models out of Cursor as of November 12, 2026. The trigger is SpaceX's acquisition of Cursor; as justification, OpenAI explicitly cites past experience with contract breaches by companies belonging to Elon Musk, writing that it's holding the cutoff "to the latest possible date" but won't ship any future models. According to Cursor CEO Michael Truell, roughly 5% of the platform's traffic is affected.

Sourcing note The circulating purchase price of roughly $60 billion and the 08/14/2026 closing date come from aggregated headlines (Forbes and others); the primary reports from CNBC and DevOps.com returned HTTP 403 on request. The date and traffic share are sourced; the purchase price is "reported."

Why this is more than a footnote: for the first time, model access is being cut not for safety or capacity reasons, but because the new owner isn't trusted contractually. That makes your tool's model selection no longer a technical property, but a business relationship someone else can terminate. 5% of traffic means most Cursor users won't notice anything. But if you deliberately run GPT models in Cursor, you have about ten weeks to rehearse the switch — and should take this incident as an argument for tools that keep the vendor swappable.

Circulating: /limit-reset in Claude Code

Since 09/02–09/03, users have been reporting a command, /limit-reset, that resets the five-hour session limit once a week. The interface reportedly shows "next reset available Sep 4 at 2pm" along with a note that the weekly limit stays untouched — usage already spent keeps counting against it. Worth trying Typing it costs nothing: if the command exists for you, you get your most important afternoon window back.

Unconfirmed Anthropic has not published this in official release notes. It surfaced via a Reddit post, written up in a blog — for some users the command doesn't exist at all, which looks like a staggered test. Treat it as a rumor with a scrap of evidence, not a feature: try it, sure, but don't build your weekly planning on it. The hard deadline remains 09/13–09/14, when the temporary limit increase expires.
