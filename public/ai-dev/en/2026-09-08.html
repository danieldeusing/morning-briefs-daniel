Four model launches in 72 hours, four different measuring tapes

Between September 1 and September 4, Anthropic, Google, Meta, and OpenAI each launched a new frontier model — and no two of them agreed to sit the same exam. Anthropic brings SWE-bench Pro to the table, Google brings HLE-Verified, Meta and OpenAI each bring a DeepSWE number, and as of today none of the four has a SWE-bench Verified score. So this week's story isn't who won — it's that the question can't currently be answered as long as everyone brings their own measuring tape, and that the official Verified process hasn't accepted a new submission since June 2025.

  • OpenAI Astra's pricing trap sits at 272,000 tokens. Beyond it, the entire request is billed at double the input price and 1.5x the output price — with a roughly 1.1 million-token window, the list-price zone is only a quarter of it.

The fourth column isn't a ranking. It's a list of four different exams, and the choice of exam is itself the message: every vendor shows the board it leads on.

Exactly one number is shared by two of the four — DeepSWE v1.1, where Meta reports 75.4 and OpenAI reports 74.1. That's a 1.3-point gap, and the temptation to turn it into "Meta beats OpenAI" is today's mistake. Meta's figure appears in only one secondary source; the independent tracker that carries the pricing and context figures doesn't list it. Both runs were conducted in-house, on each company's own harness. And Google claims that on that same DeepSWE, 3.8 Flash beats "most larger frontier models" — without citing a number for it. A claim with no value attached isn't a measurement.

Anthropic skips DeepSWE entirely and instead does well on third-party measurements: 81.2% on SWE-bench Pro (rank 1, board as of 09/04/2026), 90.52% on LiveCodeBench, 85.02% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 (rank 2, behind GPT-5.6 Sol at 85.77%). Better documented than anything else in this table — and still no answer to whether Fable 5.1 beats Astra, because Astra doesn't appear on any of these three boards.

SWE-bench Verified, Pro, DeepSWE: All three hand a model real GitHub bug reports and check whether its patch turns the test suite green. Think of them as report cards from three different schools: Verified is the old, small, human-vetted task set. Pro is its harder successor, with bigger, multi-file tasks. DeepSWE measures long tasks where the agent has to stay on track across many steps. An 81 on Pro and a 75 on DeepSWE relate to each other the way a B+ in Latin relates to a C in the long jump.
Why there's no chart today Putting four bars from four different exams side by side would claim exactly the comparability that doesn't exist — the bar heights would look like a statement. A bar chart returns here as soon as two of these models get measured on the same board with the same harness.

For reference only: Google's second, access-restricted variant, 3.8 Flash Cyber, is security territory and belongs in the software brief.

The official Verified repo has been stalled for over 14 months

Where the raw results should live — and what's actually there

swebench.com loads its Verified table via JavaScript; a plain fetch returns an empty shell, yesterday same as today. The fallback is the official results repository, github.com/SWE-bench/experiments, folder evaluation/verified — that's where the submitted raw runs live, one folder per submission, named by date. The most recent folder there is 20250629_deepswerl_r2eagent_tts. June 29, 2025. No new submission has been added in over 14 months.

That explains a lot. What circulates in search results as the "SWE-bench Verified leaderboard" doesn't draw on this repository at all — it draws on runs each aggregator conducts itself or sources from third parties. That's why they contradict each other so badly: this brief noted on 09/07 that the table at llm-stats.com leads with Claude Fable 5 at 95.0% and doesn't even have an Opus 5 entry, while leaderboard.steel.dev puts Opus 5 in the lead at 97.00%. Two boards that don't disagree about a value — they disagree about what counts as an entry.

Stated plainly, that means: none of the four new models has a fresh, independently submitted Verified score. Not Fable 5.1, not Gemini 3.8 Flash, not Muse Spark 1.3, not Astra. If anyone shows you a Verified number for one of these four today, they're showing you a third party's run — which can be entirely legitimate, but it isn't an entry in the official process and shouldn't be read as one.

Fittingly, the most usable comparison figure of the week didn't come from a board at all, but from an outfit that runs every model through its own harness: on 09/03, Artificial Analysis measured Astra at 67 points on its Coding Agent Index against 70 for Fable 5.1 in Claude Code (documented in this brief on 09/06). One harness, two models, the same scale. That's currently the best thing available — and the first place to look at the next model launch.

Copilot adds three of the four within four days

How fast a model launch reaches the tool today — and who it pushes out to make room

GitHub's changelog is the more honest timeline this week than any announcement page, because it records when a model actually reaches users. 09/01: "Claude Fable 5.1 is generally available in GitHub Copilot" — on launch day itself. 09/03: "Gemini 3.8 Flash is now available in GitHub Copilot." 09/04: "GPT-6 Astra is generally available in GitHub Copilot." Muse Spark 1.3 is missing from the list; Meta ships it through its own model API and "Muse Code" instead.

In between, a fourth entry appears on 09/03: "Upcoming deprecation of selected GitHub Copilot models." A secondary listing names 10/02/2026 as the next deprecation wave — Gemini 3.5/3.6 Flash give way to 3.8 Flash, Kimi K2.7 Code gives way to K3, Claude Opus 4.7 gives way to Opus 5; six more models, including Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 4.5/4.6 and Sonnet 4.5/4.6, reportedly already left on 09/01. The deprecation itself is documented via GitHub's own changelog; the model list only via a secondary source — I couldn't cross-check it against GitHub's documentation, since the obvious page for it returns a 404.

The point for your own workbench: model turnover is now maintenance work, and a quarter-year lifespan for a model name is the normal case. If you have hardcoded model strings in prompts, scripts, or CI configs, you get a few weeks' notice and then a silent fallback to a successor model — with different behavior on the exact same prompts. The least spectacular and most expensive side effect of this launch week.

Two pricing traps and one expiration date

What the list prices don't tell you

The expiration date. Gemini 3.8 Flash costs $0.75 for input and $3.75 for output per million tokens — the old 3.7 Flash price, held through 12/31/2026. On 01/01/2027 it doubles to $1.50/$7.50. That's not a discount that quietly gets extended — it's a dated transition. If you're building agent loops on Flash now and projecting operating costs, plan around the post-new-year number. Try it For throwaway runs, bulk migrations, and anything where throughput matters, it's still by far the cheapest of the four through year's end.

The threshold. Astra's trap sits at 272,000 input tokens: beyond it, the entire request is billed at double the input and cache price and 1.5x the output price, not just the excess portion. With a usable window of roughly 1.1 million tokens, that means the window is a good four times larger than the zone where the $10/$50 list price even applies. An agent that slowly fills up its context will eventually cross that line unnoticed.

The two prices. Muse Spark 1.3 is listed in the trackers at $0.10/$0.20 — that's the contributor rate. For standard/xhigh access, $1.25/$4.25 is cited elsewhere: a factor of 12 on input, a factor of 21 on output. That depends on the access tier, not the model.

Fable 5.1, by contrast, stays at $10/$50, same as Fable 5. Only cache reads got cheaper: $0.25 per million, which Anthropic says is 75% below the old rate. Anyone reusing long system prompts or large repository contexts across many calls — that is, anyone doing serious agent work — will notice that on the monthly bill more clearly than any benchmark shift.

Cursor lets agents run inside your own network

09/02/2026 — the most practical change of the week

From Cursor's changelog, verbatim: "Cursor supports self-hosted machines, which let you keep tool execution entirely in your own network." Since then, cloud agents can run their work on infrastructure you already operate — named options include AWS Lambda, Coder, Cloudflare, Daytona, Modal, Namespace, Vercel, and E2B. Individuals get "My Machines," organizations get "Team pools" with sleep and reconnect.

Execution machine: when a coding agent runs tests, installs dependencies, or launches a script, that doesn't happen inside the model — it happens on a real machine holding a copy of your code. Until now, that machine belonged to the vendor. Think of a contractor: previously he took your workpiece back to his own shop; now he brings his tools to you.

Why this matters more than most model news: for anything under NDA, involving customer data, or touching internal package registries, the cloud-agent option was simply unusable until now — not for lack of capability, but because the code would have left the building. Try it And the side effect is economic: anyone already paying a Lambda or Modal bill doesn't pay for agent compute time twice.

Devin: $900 million run rate, 89% self-written code

Two big numbers, both from the company itself

Cognition, the company behind Devin, reports more than $900 million in annualized revenue as of September 2026 — up from $492 million four months earlier. And internally, Devin now reportedly writes 89% of its own code, up from 13% in December 2025.

The revenue figure is the more solid of the two: it surfaces in the context of a funding round, where misstatements get expensive.

"89% of the code," on the other hand, is the industry's slipperiest metric. It says nothing about how much of it a human specified beforehand and reviewed afterward. Lines are the wrong unit to begin with: generated test scaffolding, migrations, and boilerplate count the same as the one hard spot where the architecture gets decided. It doesn't hold up as evidence for "agents replace developers" — but it does show that a vendor uses its own tool aggressively enough to state the number publicly.

Alongside that: Cognition has its own model, SWE-1.6, which works across providers, and Windsurf has operated as "Devin Desktop" since June 2026.
