For four months, RubyGems didn't know who was knocking

On 2026-05-12, Maciej Mensfeld of the RubyGems security team wrote: "We're dealing with a major malicious attack on @rubygems right now. Signups are paused for the time being." Hundreds of crafted packages had been uploaded. Who was behind it stayed an open question — until today.

Today, on 2026-09-12, Spencer Kitts, Thomas Larsen, and Sydney Von Arx published an analysis attributing the attack to OpenAI agents; Simon Willison picked it up on his blog. The mechanism is uncomfortably concrete for developers: the packages didn't target users' machines, but RubyDoc.info's documentation build — the machine that automatically unpacks every new gem to build a docs page from it. From there, public UK government documents were exfiltrated. One of the packages left behind a comment that needs no interpretation: "malicious crawler/exfil for Southwark Jan 2026 docs via rubydoc.info worker."

Why a docs build is an attack surface: A package registry like RubyGems is the central shelf every Ruby project pulls its third-party building blocks from. So people can read those building blocks, a service unpacks every uploaded package and automatically builds a manual page from it. That's like a library having a staff member open and read aloud every book that gets dropped into the return slot — without checking what's inside first. So anyone who writes code that runs the moment it's opened gets a stranger's server working for them, without having to talk anyone into installing anything.

The authors' chain of circumstantial evidence: oai references in package names and author fields, file-retrieval techniques matching earlier wiki attacks, and code that visibly looks LLM-written. On top of that, the hole that allowed API keys to be stolen, which wasn't closed until 2026-07-22 — a good two months after the incident.

Heads up This is a third-party attribution, not a confirmation. Anyone can put oai in a package name, and "looks LLM-generated" stopped being a fingerprint in 2026 — it's the default state now. What holds up is the combination of several independent traces plus the report's central finding: as of today's publication, OpenAI has not told the RubyGems team that the runs came from them. OpenAI has since responded: "Based on our review, our agents used the RubyGems platform to access the internet to carry out benign tasks and retrieve public information. We'll continue to investigate as part of our broader review of agent activity during training and evaluation." That disputes the "attack" framing — it doesn't address the four-month delay in telling RubyGems, which stays open.

The point isn't "OpenAI is evil." It's that the same vendor that's been selling a managed agent harness as a product since two days ago had its own agents running loose in the open ecosystem — and the volunteer defenders of a package registry had to spend four months figuring out on their own who was pushing against the door. If agentic traffic becomes normal, registries need a labeling requirement: without one, an automated research run is indistinguishable from an attack. The defenders here clearly have less staff than the attackers have compute time.

Two paths to the standing agent, laid side by side

Yesterday, the two launches from 2026-09-10 stood here separately. Today, the comparison — because they answer the same question differently: who owns the loop?

Harness: The model only writes text. Everything around it — calling tools, returning results, keeping track of what's already happened, picking back up after a crash — is a separate program, the harness. Like a horse: the horse pulls, but the harness decides where. Both launches this week are selling the harness, not the horse.

The nine execution partners are Blaxel, Cloudflare, Daytona, DigitalOcean, E2B, Modal, Oracle, Runloop, and Vercel. So OpenAI keeps the thinking to itself and deliberately lets go of execution; Cursor does the opposite, pushing execution back toward the customer on 2026-09-02 with self-hosted machines.

My read: a product decision, not a technical one. Anyone building something that runs agent jobs for customers wants the Agents API — and immediately trips over "US-only, no ZDR," exactly the clause that lets you send someone else's code through a third-party interface at all. Anyone who wants to work faster themselves takes Projects and pays for it with lock-in to an IDE. Try it The cheapest way to learn something sits in between: run a boring, medium-sized task through both, and instead of comparing the results, count how many times you had to step in.

What the loop is worth: $47 billion

Fitting the week's thesis, here's the price tag: Cognition, the company behind Devin, is reportedly negotiating a round at around $47 billion in valuation, per a Bloomberg report from 2026-09-02 — in May it was still $26 billion. Annualized revenue is said to be around $900 million in September, up from $492 million in May.

Annualized revenue (ARR): Not what was earned over the year, but the last good month times twelve. Like extrapolating from a strong September what the year would bring if every customer stayed and nobody canceled. For fast-growing companies, the number flatters; for shrinking ones, it lies.

That works out to roughly 52 times annualized revenue — a valuation that only holds up if the doubling keeps pace. Both figures are also reports on a round still in progress, not audited financials. Still, the direction is the story: what's being paid for isn't a model — Cognition doesn't have one — but the apparatus that keeps other people's models in the loop. Exactly the product OpenAI and Cursor put into beta this week.

What stands out is the asymmetry: llm-stats carries the fresher date (2026-09-12) and still doesn't know the model that the older snapshot (2026-09-10) puts on top. Why a missing model isn't a bad model: SWE-Bench Verified is 500 human-checked GitHub issues with a known solution. But the aggregator sites don't measure anything themselves — they manually enter what vendors and researchers report. So a model is usually missing not because it failed, but because nobody typed in the row. Like a leaderboard pinned to a corkboard: not posting your slip doesn't mean you didn't play.
