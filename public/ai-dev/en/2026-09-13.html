The Price of a Token Depends on What You Give Up

It's Sunday, and almost nothing is moving in the toolbox. So today's brief is the tally instead of the headline: the launch wave from September 1–3 is twelve days in, the price lists are settled, the access rules are known. And laid side by side, the four launches share one thing: none of them come down to capability, they come down to access. Anthropic makes rereading context cheap, Google glues an expiration date to its launch price, Meta sells the same reasoning engine for a tenth of the price — if you hand over your data for training — and OpenAI locks its new model's strongest capability behind a program. In 2026, the price of a token isn't a number anymore. It's a trade.

Twelve days later: the tally on the model wave

Between September 1 and September 3, four labs launched four models. Each one got its own day in this brief; today they stand side by side, because only now are all the price lists and access rules known — and because what looked like the story at launch isn't what shows up on the bill at the end of the month.

Anthropic's change is the only one that makes an existing setup cheaper immediately, without you changing anything: Fable 5.1's base price stays at $10 input and $50 output per million tokens, but cached input now costs just $0.25 instead of $1.00 — 75% less. Anthropic itself puts the effect at "around 25%" for typical workloads and "up to around 45%" for heavily agentic tasks.[1]

Cache reads: A language model has no memory between two requests — you resend the entire context so far at every single step. It's like having to re-explain the whole job site to a contractor before every task. Caching means the provider sets the unchanged start of your prompt aside and doesn't recompute it from scratch on the next call — it just rereads it. That rereading is what now costs a quarter of the price. And because an agentic session resends the same project context dozens of times, the lion's share of the bill in Claude Code, Cursor, or a homegrown harness sits at exactly this one line item.

A back-of-envelope number to make the scale concrete (our own math, not a vendor figure): rereading a total of 10 million cached tokens across a long agentic session now costs $2.50, down from $10.00. That's not a discount program — it's the difference between "letting agentic sessions run" and "rationing agentic sessions."

The second Anthropic story is structural: per the vendor, Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 are the same model with different safety tiers — Mythos available only through Trusted Access programs and currently only for US organizations.[1] So at Anthropic too, the model name no longer says anything about capability, only about access rights. You're no longer buying a model; you're buying a shell wrapped around one.

Meta sells the same reasoning engine for a tenth of the price — the price is your traffic

Muse Spark 1.3 has been out since Sep 2, and one number has been circulating in the trackers ever since: $0.10/$0.20 per million tokens. That sounds like a price break, but it's an incomplete figure. Meta runs two endpoints. The standard endpoint costs $1.25 input and $4.25 output, and your data stays private. The separate, opt-in "Contributor" endpoint runs around $0.10/$0.20 — and there, Meta trains on whatever you send it.[2]

That's not a price tier, it's a trade, and it should be written up as exactly that. For throwaway experiments on public code, the Contributor rate might be rational. For client code under a contract, it isn't — there, the 12x factor on input and 21x on output is simply the price of your client's source code ending up in the next training run.

For context on performance: Artificial Analysis lists the Max variant with an Intelligence Index score of 48 and calculates $1.60 per index task (xhigh: $1.37) — strikingly high given the token prices, which comes down to the model being verbose. Context window: 1 million tokens.[3] So even if you get the low list price, it still isn't the bill.

Astra is the first model OpenAI itself rates as critical

On Sep 3, OpenAI publicly launched GPT-6 Astra (API ID gpt-6-astra). One to two days earlier came the actually more interesting news: Astra is the first model from the company to hit the highest cyber tier in its own Preparedness Framework — "Critical."[4]

Preparedness Framework: OpenAI's internal risk scale. Before every launch, it's tested how far a model can go in areas like cyberattacks, biology, or autonomy — comparable to a building's fire-safety rating: past a certain tier, it can't just open its doors, it needs extra safeguards. "Critical" is the top tier, and Astra is the first OpenAI model to land there. What's being scored isn't the model's intent, but its capability — what someone could do with it, if they wanted to.

The numbers behind it are uncomfortably concrete: 100% on ExploitBench — a test that measures whether a model can turn a known vulnerability into a working exploit — and two zero-day flaws that Astra found in testing without human help. At the same time, OpenAI reports a markedly tougher defense against misuse attempts: 91.5% of cyber-related jailbreak attempts are refused, versus 59% for its predecessor, GPT-5.6 Sol.[4] Full cyber capability doesn't ship with the general launch — it goes first to a small test group and then rolls out through the Daybreak Blue program.

For day-to-day work, that means little — what you get through Copilot or the API is the locked-down version. What's interesting is the direction of travel: for the first time, the question isn't whether a coding model is good enough, but whether it's safe to ship in full.

Google's answer is called Fairwind — and the launch price has an expiration date

Gemini 3.8 Flash launched on Sep 2 at $0.75 input and $3.75 output per million tokens. Those numbers are a launch price and hold through 2026-12-31; after that they rise to $1.50 and $7.50 — a clean doubling.[5] Anyone budgeting a Flash-based pipeline today without entering the expiration date is planning off half the bill. That's the kind of detail that doesn't make the headlines and eats your margin in January.

Alongside it sits Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber: 86.2% on CyberGym, meaning autonomous vulnerability discovery, available exclusively to vetted government agencies, critical-infrastructure operators, and software maintainers through the new Fairwind program.[5][6] Same pattern as OpenAI, just two days earlier: one base model, two access shells — one public, one reserved for defenders.

The second point is cadence. This is the third Flash model in six weeks, and it shares its architecture with 3.7 Flash rather than introducing a new base model.[7] My read: this isn't a capability race anymore, it's capacity utilization. Anyone building their own data centers has a reason to roll out a new, cheaper version of the same design every few weeks — and the model number tells you more about Google's utilization plan than about a jump in capability.

The one bench number everyone's misquoting this week

Fable 5.1 tops a leaderboard at 81.2% — that part's true. But it's SWE-bench Pro, not SWE-bench Verified. On that board, Mythos 5 sits at 80.3% and Fable 5 at 80.0% behind it, among dozens of listed models.[8] Putting that number next to a Verified number compares two different exams.

Verified vs. Pro: Both are collections of real GitHub bug reports where the model has to write a working patch. "Verified" is the human-reviewed subset the industry has measured itself against for years — the standard entrance exam. "Pro" is the newer, harder selection with bigger, multi-file tasks — the master's exam. The same percentage means something entirely different on the two lists, and 81 on Pro is a different result than 81 on Verified.

The official Verified board still doesn't help with fact-checking: fetching swebench.com today still returns only the tab labels and no table rows — the page loads its data via JavaScript.[9] The fallback boards aren't referees either: Aider's leaderboard still carries "last updated November 20, 2025," a state ten months old that doesn't know any of this wave's models.[10] And for LiveCodeBench, the most recent dated state that could be found is 2026-08-25 — with Gemini 3 Pro Preview at 91.7%; nothing for September.

Which is why, again today, there's no bar chart in this brief. A chart with four numbers from four different exams would look like a result and would actually be a claim. The honest statement about this wave: none of the four new models has an independently verifiable Verified entry yet, and the vendor numbers are self-reported.

Open weights: Qwen updates the Max class

The most notable part of the wave is the part that's had the least written about it: on Sep 2, Alibaba released Qwen3.8-Max-0902, a coding/agent-tuned update to its already-open Max model — 2.4 trillion parameters — alongside the much smaller Qwen3.8-27B. Independent sources place the base weights (Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B) as having already gone open in mid-August (roughly Aug 12–14), so "first ever open" applies to the Max class as a whole, not to this September release. On top of that, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash landed on Sep 10 at $0.30/$1.20 per million tokens at the top tier.

Unverified The parameter count comes from search results, not a direct fetch from qwenlm.github.io or deepseek.com. It's presented here as a lead, not a confirmed number.

In practice, 2.4 trillion parameters is irrelevant for Daniel's hardware — that doesn't run on any machine sitting under a desk. What's relevant is the signal: once the Max class goes open, it erodes the closed vendors' pricing power in exactly the segment where they currently charge $10 and $50 per million tokens. The 27B model is the one that could actually run for you — but there's no verifiable number for its quality today.
