The Cache Is the Bill

What actually moved in AI coding this September isn't capability — it's the price of reading the same thing twice. Claude Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra carry the exact same price tag — $10 input, $50 output per million tokens. They differ in exactly one place: what a reused context costs. $0.25 at Anthropic, $1.00 at OpenAI, per million. That's the line that keeps recurring in a long agent session — and, of all days, today Claude Code fixes a bug that was quietly throwing that same cache away. Look further down and you find DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, open-weight, at $0.003. Three orders of magnitude on the same line. On an independent leaderboard, none of the three models appears.

Same sticker, quadruple the cache [1][2][3]

Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.1 on Sep 1, and the only pricing change from Fable 5 sits in a line that won't show up on any marketing slide: "Cache reads now cost $0.25 per million tokens, 75% less than Fable 5."[1] Input and output stay at $10 and $50 per million tokens.[1] OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, released Sep 10, carries, per the official developer pricing page, the exact same sticker — $10 input, $50 output — and charges $1.00 per million for cached input.[2] That's four times as much.

Prompt cache and cache-read: A language model has no memory between two calls. On every new turn in a session, the tool sends the entire prior history along again — your files, your rules, every previous reply. The provider is allowed to remember and reuse the part it already digested; that's the cache. Like a contractor who leaves his tools at your place overnight instead of packing them up every morning. The cache-read is the rate for that reused part — usually a tenth of the input price. That's exactly Astra's $1.00. Anthropic, at $0.25, is at a fortieth.

Why this is more than a footnote: in an agent session, the reused share grows with every turn. Turn one sends the context once, turn twenty sends it for the twentieth time — and from turn two on, almost all of it is cache. You pay the input line once; you pay the cache line again on every turn. That's why, over a long run across a large repository, the cache-read isn't the smallest line item — it's the one that repeats the most.

Where I'd hold back: nobody's output price has moved, and that's exactly where agentic coding burns the most — tokens written, not read. $50 per million output stands unchanged at both companies. The cache cut is a real win, but it's a discount on the cheapest line of the bill. Anyone who actually wants to cut costs trims output, not input. And one more caveat: a cache has to hit to save you anything. Change something near the start of your context — a rule, a file, a timestamp — and everything after it is invalidated, and you pay the full input price again.

And today Claude Code fixes that exact cache [4]

As of today, Claude Code sits at 2.1.273, one build above yesterday. The changelog entry that ties into the pricing story above reads, in substance: /login, /upgrade, and /extra-usage discarded earlier reasoning from the conversation — and thereby triggered a rewrite of the prompt cache.[4] So anyone who topped up their quota mid-session had, until today, been blowing up their own cache and then paying full input price again afterward, without noticing.

This is the kind of bug that shows up in no benchmark and still shows up on the bill. It's also a neat illustration of how tightly price and tool are now coupled: a rate is only as good as the harness that hits it.

The rest of this build is routine maintenance with two useful points: a notice when an MCP server drops mid-session and automatic reconnection gives up — previously the only sign was tools going missing — and the ability to spin off a session started with --remote-control from the Claude app as a background session.[4]

DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash: open weights, two orders of magnitude down [5][6][7]

On the same Sep 10 as Astra, DeepSeek released V4.1-Flash with open weights.[5] The architecture is the real news: a 552-billion-parameter MoE in a new causal encoder-decoder layout, where "only 8B active parameters compute for input and 16B for output."[5] Reading and writing run over paths of different width — and reading is the cheaper one.

MoE and active parameters: The model holds 552 billion weights in total but computes with only a small slice of them for each individual token. Like a library with five hundred shelves where only two get opened per question: the whole building needs the space, but only those two shelves cost time. That's how a model can be enormous and still run cheap — memory footprint scales with the total count, price scales with the active parameters.

The official API's prices turn that architectural trick into a number: $0.15 input and $0.60 output per million tokens off-peak, $0.30 and $1.20 during peak hours — peak hours being 01:00–04:00 and 06:00–10:00 UTC, Monday through Friday.[7] A cache hit costs $0.003 off-peak. Against Astra's standard rate, that's roughly a sixty-seventh of the input price and an eighty-third of the output price; on the cache-read line, even Anthropic's reduced price still sits about eighty times higher.

Reported alongside that is 74.2% on DeepSWE v1.1 — just above the 74.1% OpenAI reports for Astra — and a Codeforces rating of 3471.[6] I wouldn't take that lead seriously: 0.1 percentage points sits within the noise on any coding benchmark, both numbers come from the vendors themselves, and neither has been independently remeasured. What's notable isn't the ranking — it's that an open-weight model shows up in the same line at all, for a sixty-seventh of the price.

Open Which license the weights carry can't be confirmed today. The DeepSeek page didn't name a license text on retrieval; one secondary source mentions MIT, without a second confirmation. For anything headed into client code, that's the point to settle before downloading — not after.

Try this The test worth running isn't the benchmark, it's the off-peak bill: run a real agent task once on V4.1-Flash and once on your usual model, and put the two bills side by side. At this price gap, the open model doesn't need to be equally good to be worth it — it just needs to be good enough.

Correction: Cursor did move, after all [11]

Yesterday's mandatory round said "Cursor — nothing new today." That was wrong. The changelog, for Sep 10: "Today we're launching Projects in Cursor. Projects lets you take on larger bodies of work, such as a feature, a migration, or a full app."[11] A coordinator agent plans the work and hands out sub-tasks to sub-agents, with shared context across cloud and local machines. Added to that, since Sep 2, self-hosted machines that keep tool execution entirely inside your own network.[11]

In substance, this is the same shift as the pricing story above, just one level up: the bottleneck is no longer whether a model can solve a task, but who manages the context over days, without anyone having to leave a laptop open. A coordinator that delegates is exactly the workflow where the cache line explodes — every sub-agent gets context, every turn sends it again.

Task→Coordinator plans→n sub-agents→n × context per turn

What bothers me about it: the more of this runs in the vendor's cloud, the less visibility you have into what you're paying for. That makes the self-hosted machines the more interesting of the two announcements — it's the only one that hands control back instead of giving up more of it.
