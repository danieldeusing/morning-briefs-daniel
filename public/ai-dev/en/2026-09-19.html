Your Subscription Is Now a Down Payment

Nothing came out in this beat today — no model, no release, no announcement dated Sep 19. The right day, then, for what the daily drumbeat usually hides: a look at the price list. This summer, one vendor completely rebuilt its coding tool's billing, and two others simply shut their assistants down. Taken together, that adds up to a shift that slipped past every individual headline: the subscription is no longer permission to use a tool, it's a down payment on your own usage. And at the biggest provider, the one number that would make that usable isn't even on the page anymore.

Copilot Bills in Dollars — and Won't Name the Rate

Since Jun 1, 2026, GitHub Copilot no longer has fixed allowances. The former "Premium Request Units" are gone, replaced by a dollar balance: "Starting June 1, Premium Request Units (PRUs) will be replaced by GitHub AI Credits."[1] Its own community forum puts it even more tersely: "Effective June 1, 2026: usage-based billing is now in effect for all GitHub Copilot plans."[2]

Usage-based billing with credits: Until now, this worked like a phone plan with free minutes — 300 expensive requests a month, and you could keep count. Now it's a prepaid card: the card holds $10, every expensive request draws down a bit of it, and after that you keep paying per request. The difference isn't the price, it's predictability: with free minutes, you know in advance when you'll run out. With a prepaid card, you need to know what a call costs.

And that's exactly what nobody knows with Copilot. The published figures are stated purely in dollars:

PlanPriceAI credits included
Pro$10/month$10
Pro+$39/month$39
Business$19/seat$19
Enterprise$39/seat$39

What stands out is that the two columns match. The subscription price is the balance, cent for cent.[1] That makes the subscription not a plan anymore but a down payment with a minimum amount: you pay $10 and get to spend $10 in return. The usage right that a plan used to include on top of the price now lives entirely inside consumption.

What's missing is the one number that would actually make the model usable: what does a request cost? GitHub doesn't publish it, and the reasoning given is notable: "We can't disclose details, because that would give bad actors more to work with to get around our safeguards."[2] As an anti-abuse measure that's plausible — anyone who knows the exchange rate can calculate it and push right up against it. As a price disclosure, though, it's still a problem, and a lopsided one: the provider knows the rate, the customer doesn't. And a rate that's never published can also change without anyone ever announcing a price increase.

Reported, not confirmed A promotional bonus for Business and Enterprise expired on Sep 1; the reported drop is from 3,000 to 1,900 credits per Business seat and from 7,000 to 3,900 for Enterprise.[3] These raw credit figures don't appear on any GitHub page — they come exclusively from aggregators and are therefore not independently verifiable. I'm carrying them as reported, not confirmed. The direction is plausible and fits the pattern: same price, smaller balance — a price increase that isn't a price change.

The contrast sits at AWS. Kiro publishes both: the bundle and the marginal price. Pro costs $20 for 1,000 credits, with extra credits at $0.04 apiece.[4] That makes the whole ladder something you can actually calculate — $0.02 per credit bundled, $0.04 marginal, identical across every tier. Whether $0.02 is a lot is debatable; that you can work it out at all is the difference. My read, and it's an uncomfortable one for the market leader: for a usage-based tool, the number that matters isn't the monthly price, it's the marginal price — and Copilot simply doesn't provide it.

Twenty Dollars Is the Going Rate Now

Three of the four major paid entry tiers now cost the same. I checked each of the following four lines today directly on the vendor's own page, not on a comparison site:

ToolEntry priceWhat's included
GitHub Copilot Pro$10/month$10 AI Credits, metered after that[1]
Cursor Pro$20/month"expanded agent limits," frontier models, cloud agents[5]
Claude Pro$20/month ($17 billed annually)Claude Code included[6]
Kiro Pro$20/month1,000 credits, extra at $0.04/credit[4]

The temptation is to read Copilot as the cheap option. That's only true if you take $10 as half the price of the same thing — it's actually half the wallet. How much work $10 in balance carries depends on a rate that isn't published; how much work 1,000 Kiro credits carry depends on the same thing, except there you at least know the exchange rate in dollars. And the tier above splits wide apart: Kiro Pro+ costs $40, Cursor Pro+ $60, Cursor Ultra $200.[5][4]

Cursor's pricing page today also carries something nobody would have expected six months ago: every paid tier advertises "Grok Bot access," with tiered Grok limits from Pro up to Ultra.[5] That's the acquisition, made visible on the price list. SpaceX acquired Cursor's parent Anysphere on Aug 14 in an all-stock deal — roughly 389.3 million SpaceX Class A shares, corresponding to a $60B equity value.[7] That sounds like a hoax, and I treated it accordingly: Bloomberg and the company's own documentation (SEC Form 8-K) both cite the same share count. A rocket maker now owns one of the two most-used AI editors — visible in which model is now advertised in its entry-level plan.

Two Vendors Shut Down Their Assistants This Summer

Amazon is winding down Q Developer. New sign-ups have been blocked since May 15, 2026, and IDE plugins and paid subscriptions finally end on Apr 30, 2027.[8] Its successor, Kiro, was already available before that: generally available since Nov 17, 2025, roughly six months before the sign-up freeze — explicitly not as a feature update to Q Developer but as a ground-up rebuild.[19] Winding down Q Developer, then, isn't the starting gun for the successor — it's closing the door behind it. Google has cleared out the individual-user tiers of Gemini Code Assist: "Starting June 18, 2026, the Gemini Code Assist IDE extensions stopped serving requests for the Gemini Code Assist for Individuals, Google AI Pro, and Google AI Ultra tiers."[9] The GitHub app from the same family followed on Jul 17: "As of July 17, 2026, the consumer version is shut down, and all of the app's code review activity ends."[10] Users are redirected to Antigravity. Then there's Continue.dev, acqui-hired by Cursor in June and discontinued; its last release shipped Jun 19, and the repository is now read-only.[11]

End-of-support versus shutdown: Two very different things that sound alike. End-of-support means the thing keeps running but stops getting updates and support — like a car with no spare parts left, but it still drives. Shutdown means the server just stops answering. The extension is still installed in your IDE, the icon's there, the menu's there — and nothing happens. For individual users, Gemini Code Assist was the second case.

Three tools pulled from the field in a single quarter, all from well-funded vendors, none of them for lack of quality. That's the risk that actually hits a solo developer in practice — not that a model gets worse, but that the other end just stops answering. And the migration is never a version bump: Q Developer to Kiro means a different product, different configuration, different billing logic.

That raises a question about your own setup: does your work run through one vendor's proprietary extension, or through a swappable layer — an MCP server (the standard plug a model uses to call tools), an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, a local model? That's what determines whether a shutdown like this costs you an afternoon or a week — regardless of how good the tool happens to be right now.

No Benchmark Can Be Cleanly Cited Today

The table this brief usually leans on is SWE-Bench Verified on swebench.com. It loads its values via JavaScript, and on today's pull — same as the pass before — it returned only navigation and scaffolding, not a single number.[12] The Aider Polyglot board is reachable, but it's stamped "last updated November 20, 2025": ten months old, with gpt-5 (high) leading at 88.0% for $29.08.[13] That says nothing about today's state. LiveCodeBench, via Artificial Analysis, does show numbers — Gemini 3 Pro Preview (high) at 91.7%, Flash Preview at 90.8%, DeepSeek V3.2 Speciale at 89.6% — but the page carries no update date at all.[14] A number without a timestamp isn't a measurement, it's a claim.

That leaves exactly one dated snapshot, and it comes from an aggregator: llm-stats.com marks its SWE-Bench Verified page "last updated September 19, 2026" and lists, at the top, Claude Fable 5 at 95.0%, Claude Mythos Preview at 93.9%, Opus 4.8 at 88.6%, Opus 4.7 at 87.6%, and Sonnet 5 at 85.2%.[15] That's a citing secondary source I couldn't cross-check against the primary board — I'm reporting what it says, not that it was measured.

Benchmark saturation: If everyone in a class scores between 17 and 19 out of 20 on a test, the grade stops telling you anything about the students — it only tells you something about the test. Either it's too easy, or everyone trained specifically for it. That's exactly what happens to a benchmark once the top of the field bunches up tightly: the test stops discriminating long before the models stop actually being different.

That's the real finding of the day, independent of any single decimal point. When the top of the field sits between 85 and 95 percent and the first five places all come from one vendor, this board no longer distinguishes anything relevant to picking a tool. What's left are the last few percentage points — and that's usually exactly where the tasks whose grading you can argue about sit. That's why there's deliberately no bar chart in the brief today: a chart claims a measurement, and what I have is an unconfirmed snapshot.
