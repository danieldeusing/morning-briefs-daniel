260 Digits, 192 Sessions, $400,000

Eric Lu, an engineer at Cognition, used the in-house agent Devin to factor the number RSA-260 into its two prime factors. The report pins the timestamp down to the second: 2026-09-03 01:48:57 UTC. That makes it "a new record for the largest publicly solved problem in the RSA Factoring Challenge"; the previous record, RSA-250, had stood since February 2020.[1] The run began on Aug 13 at 0:11:58 Pacific — just under three weeks from the first line to the factors.

Factoring and the number field sieve: RSA relies on the fact that you can easily multiply two large prime numbers, but practically can't take the product back apart. Like mixing a color: blending blue and yellow into green takes two seconds; getting exactly that blue and that yellow back out of the finished green is the hard direction. The best known method for this is called the number field sieve — it collects millions of small computational relationships in a massive sieving phase, then solves a gigantic system of equations from them at the end. That sieving phase is exactly what ate up most of the compute time here.

The cost breakdown is the unusually honest part. 643 GPU-days went into polynomial selection, 3,813 into sieving, 467 into solving the linear system.[1] The author tallies it himself: "I estimate this factorization cost around 4,900 GPU-days, or 13.5 GPU-years, which comes out to roughly $400,000 at current market prices."[1] It ran on NVL72 racks with GB200, GB300, and B200 nodes across multiple clusters.

And then the numbers this brief is actually about: Devin contributed to 3,328 messages across 192 of 233 sessions used, averaging three sessions in parallel and peaking at 18 parallel sessions. Out of that came a "substantially modified" variant of CADO-NFS, which Lu describes as "the world's most capable GPU lattice sieve."[1] He states the division of labor himself, matter-of-factly: "Devin is a sufficiently powerful software engineer to solve a challenging problem at the intersection of computational number theory and GPU performance engineering. My role was primarily to set priorities, establish benchmarks, and recognize when work was going off-track."[1]

My read, and I think it matters more than the record itself: this isn't an autonomous agent that cracked a crypto problem. It's a human who kept up to 18 parallel threads of work in his head for three weeks — plus a tool capable enough at performance engineering to make that overhead worthwhile. The bottleneck has moved up a level: away from writing CUDA kernels, toward deciding which of 18 threads is currently going off track. Anyone trying to gauge what agents change in their own work will find more guidance in that ratio than in any benchmark percentage point.

The Price Tag Hidden Inside

The record itself is sport. What actually matters for decisions is a clause further down: "Hyperscalers or frontier AI labs could likely factor RSA-1024 numbers at a cost on the order of $30 million per number — and, with a bit more optimization, likely substantially less."[1] That makes 1024-bit RSA no longer a mathematical question but a budget question — and $30M falls within the range a state actor or a large corporation would spend on a sufficiently valuable target.

Context The same paragraph contains the reassurance: "RSA-2048 remains roughly a billion times harder than RSA-1024 and doesn't appear meaningfully affected by this work." Today's keys contain 2048-bit problems (~617 digits); 1024-bit (~309 digits) was already deprecated back in 2013.[1] If you're using 2048-bit, there's nothing to do today.

Except: "deprecated in 2013" doesn't mean "gone in 2013." 1024-bit keys still sit in legacy systems today — in old firmware signatures, in forgotten internal CAs, in devices nobody has touched in ten years. So the question isn't "is RSA broken" (no), but a mundane inventory question: does a 1024-bit key still exist somewhere in your own estate, and what depends on it? Until now, the answer was "theoretically vulnerable, but in practice nobody's computing it." As of Sep 3, there's a number attached.

Agent makes performance work cheap → Sieve software gets faster → Cost per broken key falls

It's not the math that moved, it's the cost of the implementation work in front of it. This pattern will repeat — anywhere a known method was impractical only because nobody wanted to spend months hand-optimizing GPU kernels.

The Ranking Nobody Measures

A tool ranking has been making the rounds for a week: LogRocket's "AI dev tool power rankings" from Sep 11 puts Claude Code at #1 ("↑ from 2 — now running Fable 5.1, 1,762 Elo, the highest WebDev Arena score of any model in any tool"), OpenCode drops to #2 ("lost rank due to Claude Code's growing quality lead, not a regression"), Cursor holds #3.[2]

That sounds like a standings table. It isn't one. A second round of research the same day turned up a completely different story from the same genre of text: Claude Code and Codex share #1, Devin climbs to #4 and reportedly absorbed Windsurf, while Cursor in turn absorbed Supermaven and Continue.dev. Both versions can't be true at once, and both read like SEO listicles. I'm explicitly not carrying the acquisition claims forward here — they're unsubstantiated.

The structural difference that matters: for models, there are entities that measure and whose methodology you can read up on — SWE-bench, LiveCodeBench, the arenas. For tools, there's nothing comparable: no panel, no board, no disclosed methodology. A "#1" claim for a coding tool is therefore not a measurement, it's a blog's opinion — an informed one, at best.

Elo (like 1,762) in an arena context: Elo is a number from chess. It doesn't measure how good someone is in absolute terms, only who beats whom: beat a stronger opponent and you earn a lot of points; beat a weaker one and you earn few. AI arenas work the same way, except humans compare two anonymous answers and pick the better one. The number is therefore always relative to the field — 1,762 means nothing on its own, only in relation to the other entries on the same board on the same day.

And that number isn't cleanly sourceable today. The arena board itself (arena.ai) renders its table via JavaScript and returned "Leaderboard Not Found" on retrieval.[4] The 1,762 figure comes from LogRocket; a second secondary source cites 1,765 points for the Max variant at its Sep 1 launch.[3] Two blogs, two slightly different numbers, no primary board behind either. I'm therefore carrying the value as "reported in the range of 1,760–1,765," not as a measurement — and the ranking as what it is: a well-informed opinion with numbers in front of it.

The Number Missing from Anthropic's Own Announcement

In September, Anthropic announced Claude Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 — technically identical, differing only in safety configuration: Fable generally available, Mythos restricted to vetted professionals in cybersecurity and life sciences.[5] The announcement cites hard numbers, specifically these:

What's not there: an SWE-bench figure. None, neither for Fable 5.1 nor for Mythos 5.1.[5] That's notable, because SWE-bench Verified was for years the number every coding-model announcement led with. When a vendor drops it and shows four other boards instead, that's a statement — not necessarily a bad one, since Terminal-Bench and OSWorld measure agentic work closer to reality. But it means: any claim that "Fable 5.1 leads SWE-bench" didn't come from Anthropic. The primary board swebench.com still only renders via JavaScript and returned no values today either[8]; the only figures circulating (Opus 5 at 96%, Mythos 5 at 95.5%, Fable 5 at 95%) come from an aggregator, don't include the 5.1 models at all, and are explicitly carried here as reported.[9] That's why there's no bar chart today: putting four values from four boards on four different scales side by side claims a comparability that isn't there.

The more tangible news for everyday use is the price list anyway. Fable 5.1 costs "an estimated 25% less than Fable 5 for typical workloads," input at $10 per million tokens, output at $50 — and cache reads drop 75% to $0.25 per million tokens.[5]

Cache reads: At every step, an agent resends the entire history so far — the project files, the rules, everything that came before. With prompt caching, the provider stores that unchanged preamble once and then reads it from cache instead of reprocessing it. Like a regular at a bakery who says "the usual" instead of listing the whole order every time. That preamble is by far the largest line item on the bill during long agentic runs — a quarter instead of a dollar per million tokens is therefore a price cut you'll see on your monthly bill, not on a benchmark.

Claude Code This Week: AGENTS.md and a Classifier

Four versions in three days, primary-sourced in the official changelog.[6] The entry that outlasts the day is v2.1.277 from Sep 18: "Added AGENTS.md support: in a project with no CLAUDE.md, Claude Code reads AGENTS.md instead; change it under 'Project instructions' in /config (not yet on Bedrock, Vertex or Foundry)."[6] Try this AGENTS.md is the vendor-neutral version of the same idea — a file in the project that tells the agent how things get built, tested, and formatted here. Anyone touching a repository with multiple tools no longer needs two parallel-maintained memory files. The precedence matters: CLAUDE.md still wins, AGENTS.md only kicks in when it's absent.

v2.1.278 from Sep 19 switches auto mode for API, Enterprise, Bedrock, Vertex, Foundry, and Gateway users to a server-side classifier — "at no additional classifier cost."[6] The classifier is the small model that decides up front whether a request needs a lot of thinking or a little; until now, that pre-decision ran alongside and got billed too. In between sits a fix for anyone working through a gateway: v2.1.276 fixes a regression from v2.1.275 that triggered 400 errors whenever ANTHROPIC_BASE_URL pointed at a proxy.[6] v2.1.275 itself brought an instant-send key (ctrl+enter or ctrl+x ctrl+s).[6]

Small correction The changelog does not say Fable 5.1 is now the default model in Claude Code. It only says Fable always appears in /model and "is only grayed out if your organization's settings disable it."[6] That Fable 5.1 runs at high thinking level by default in Claude Code, by contrast, is stated in Anthropic's model announcement.[5] Two different statements that are easy to merge into one.

Quick explainer on why this is hard in the first place. RSA relies on the fact that multiplying two large prime numbers together is easy, but taking the result back apart is practically impossible. Think of mixing paint: blending blue and yellow into green takes two seconds. Pulling the exact blue and yellow back out of the finished green is the hard direction. The best known method for that is called the number field sieve, and its sieving phase ate up the lion's share of the compute time here.

The author tallies up the cost himself, and it's refreshingly honest. Around forty-nine hundred GPU-days, or thirteen and a half GPU-years, which comes out to roughly four hundred thousand dollars at current market prices. It ran on modern Nvidia racks spread across multiple data centers.

But for this brief, a different breakdown is more interesting. Devin contributed to over thirty-three hundred messages, spread across a hundred ninety-two sessions, averaging three in parallel and peaking at eighteen at once. The human describes his own role this way: setting priorities, defining benchmarks, and noticing when the work goes off the rails. This isn't an autonomous agent cracking cryptography on its own. It's someone who held eighteen parallel threads of work in his head for three weeks straight. The bottleneck has moved up a level — away from writing code, toward deciding which thread just took a wrong turn.

And then there's a throwaway line in the report that matters more than the record itself. Hyperscalers or leading AI labs, the estimate goes, could break a one-thousand-twenty-four-bit key for roughly thirty million dollars apiece. That turns it from a math question into a budget question. The reassurance sits right next to it: two-thousand-forty-eight-bit keys remain roughly a billion times harder and aren't meaningfully affected by this work. If you're using today's keys, there's nothing to do. But that one-thousand-twenty-four-bit standard, deprecated back in twenty thirteen, still lingers in legacy systems today — in old firmware signatures, in forgotten internal certificate authorities. That's worth taking inventory over.

On to the second story, and here's where I get a little uncomfortable. A ranking of AI coding tools has been circulating for a week, putting Claude Code at number one, OpenCode at number two, and Cursor at number three. A second round of research the same day turned up a totally different version of the same story, with different rankings and different acquisitions. Both can't be right at once. The reason is structural: for models, there are measuring bodies with methodology you can actually read. For tools, there's nothing like that. A number-one ranking for a coding tool isn't a measurement — it's a blog's opinion.

Fittingly, there's a gap in Anthropic's own announcement for Fable five point one. It lists figures for Terminal-Bench, CursorBench, Humanity's Last Exam, and OSWorld. What's not there is an SWE-bench number. None at all. So anyone reading that this model leads SWE-bench didn't get that from the company. The practically more important number from that same announcement is the price anyway: reading from cache gets seventy-five percent cheaper, down to twenty-five cents per million tokens. On long agentic runs, that's exactly the biggest line item on the bill.

Two practical notes from the toolbox to close out. As of version two-point-one-point-two-seventy-seven, Claude Code also reads an AGENTS-dot-md file when no CLAUDE-dot-md exists. That's the vendor-neutral version of the same project memory, and it saves you from maintaining two files in parallel. And if you're working through a proxy and got stuck on version two-seventy-five: those four-hundred errors are a known regression, fixed in the very next release.
