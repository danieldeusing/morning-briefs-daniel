26 percent — Anthropic measures itself

Anthropic published a self-assessment of its own research work on Sep 17. Three numbers from it: Claude now leads 26% of in-house model research tasks — under 1% in February 2026. The share of work at "AI collaborates" level or higher sits at over 90%. And on the internal flagship platform, by Anthropic's own account, around 30,000 Claude agents run in parallel on research and development at any given time.[1] Bloomberg picked up the numbers the same day.[2]

Autonomy ladder: Anthropic sorts every task onto a ladder — from "human does everything," through "AI suggests," "AI collaborates," up to "AI leads, human reviews." Think of a kitchen: the assistant hands you ingredients, then cooks alongside you, then runs the station alone and calls you over only if something goes wrong. The 26% is the share of stations the assistant now runs alone.

Now the skepticism, and it matters more than the number. Anthropic defines the rungs, sorts its own tasks onto them, and sells the product whose performance comes out of that sorting. That's not a benchmark — it's self-grading against a house rulebook. Bloomberg's report confirms the claim was made, not that the categorization is accurate. There's no published task list, no second party independently sorting the same tasks, no audit. "Leads" could mean a three-day rebuild or a refactor pass across forty files.

Even so, I think this report is the most useful thing to appear in this category in a week — just not because of the 26%. The useful number is the 90. It says that at Anthropic, it isn't the ceiling that ran away — it's the floor that rose: almost nothing there gets done without AI anymore. The 26% is a headline; the over-90% is a way of working.

What you can take from this costs half an hour: take your last work week and sort every major task yourself onto these four rungs. If most of your work still sits at "AI suggests" — autocomplete, a chat window on the side — then the gap to 90% isn't about the model. It's the same models Anthropic uses. The gap is in the harness and in how finely you break tasks down. That's the one conclusion from this report that doesn't depend on whether the 26% was measured cleanly.

Two leaderboards, two different firsts

Yesterday this beat noted that Claude Opus 5 has no entry at all on llm-stats' SWE-Bench Verified board. That still holds: there, Claude Fable 5 leads at 0.950, followed by Claude Mythos Preview at 0.939 and Claude Opus 4.8 at 0.886.[1] A second aggregator, benchlm.ai, says something different for the same period: "As of September 21, 2026, Claude Opus 5 leads the SWE-bench Verified leaderboard at 96%," followed by Claude Mythos 5 at 95.5% and Claude Fable 5 at 95%.[2]

Both claim to reflect the same table. They agree on neither the leader nor the model names. The official board on swebench.com loads its numbers via JavaScript and returns nothing on a plain fetch — which is exactly why these aggregators exist in the first place, and exactly why neither can be checked against the original.

My conclusion isn't "one of them is lying." It's more specific: when a table's two secondhand copies already disagree in the first row, the ranking has stopped being information. What's left is the range — the top sits at 95 to 96%. And that range is the real news: at 95%, this benchmark is done. The remaining four to five percentage points are, to a significant degree, broken or ambiguous tasks, and whoever sits on top there is decided by details of the scoring harness, not model quality. Picking a coding assistant in 2026 by SWE-Bench Verified is about as informative as picking a car by a top speed no road allows.

One number from the same board stays interesting: the best open-weight model, DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Max, sits at 0.806 tied for 8th/9th (the board doesn't rank ties stably).[1] On a saturated scale, the gap to the top says less than the fact that a downloadable model is on that board at all.

A 27B model in 5.9 gigabytes

PrismML released Ternary Bonsai 2 27B on Sep 17: a compressed version of Alibaba's Qwen3.8 27B, under Apache 2.0, multimodal, with a 262K context window. Memory footprint drops from around 54 GB in FP16 to 5.9 GB — "more than 9x smaller" — at an effective 1.76 bits per weight. The vendor claims 98.2% of the original's aggregate benchmark performance is retained.[1]

Ternary quantization: A model consists of billions of numbers ("weights"), normally 16 bits each. Ternary means each weight can only take on three values — minus one, zero, plus one — plus a shared scale factor per group of weights. Like a map that, instead of an exact elevation at every point, only stores "downhill / flat / uphill" plus a note on where the steep grades are. Much less data, and you can still find the route.

The vendor's comparison table is unusually candid, breaking out six categories individually — Bonsai 2 versus the uncompressed original: math 96.57 vs. 97.06. Instruction following 82.66 vs. 81.25. Coding 81.58 vs. 82.17. Agentic and tool-calling 77.57 vs. 79.74. Knowledge and reasoning 83.95 vs. 86.66. Vision understanding 78.59 vs. 81.64.[1] So the loss isn't spread evenly: pure coding retains 99.3%, vision understanding and reasoning around 96%.

Two things here deserve a raised eyebrow. First, the compressed model beats the original on instruction following — 82.66 vs. 81.25. A smaller model can't outdo the thing it was made from; if it still comes out ahead, this test's measurement noise is bigger than the measured difference. That discounts every percentage in this table in both directions. Second, a secondary source additionally reports a Terminal-Bench 2.1 score of 52.8 vs. 69.7 for the original — a drop to roughly three-quarters.[2] This line does not appear in PrismML's own table (I checked it again on Sep 22). I'm citing this figure as a single secondary source's claim, not a measurement — but it points exactly in the direction the vendor's own table already hints at: agentic work is the category that suffers most.

Worth trying Still, for Daniel's setup, this is the most interesting release in weeks. 5.9 GB means it runs on an 8 GB graphics card and on any 16 GB Mac — where a 27B model otherwise doesn't fit even at Q4 quantization, which needs around 16 GB. The catch no product page mentions: the weights shrink, but the context cache doesn't. If you actually fill the 262K context, you need several gigabytes on top of that again — the advertised context window and the advertised memory footprint never hold at the same time. As a model for local tool-calling and short-context code questions: yes, worth trying. As a replacement for the agent that plows through an entire repository: less so, going by its own agentic-work row.

Three tools, the same quiet handover

Laid side by side, this week's tool movements add up to a pattern. GitHub Copilot's weekly release on Sep 18 introduced, among other things, tiered automatic model selection — you no longer pick a model, you pick a stance: "Efficiency," "Balanced," or "Intelligence." Also: code review now auto-resolves addressed comments on follow-up passes, suggests commit messages, and the CLI got reliability improvements for MCP, worktrees, and sandboxing.[1] Bolt launched a third agent tier called "Forge" on Sep 14, running on open weights — GLM 5.3, GLM 5.3 Flash, Kimi K3, DeepSeek v4 Pro — as a research preview through Oct 14, alongside a new $9 "Bolt Lite" tier.[2]

The pattern: with Copilot, the vendor picks the model based on the budget stance you commit to. With Bolt, the tier picks the model — open weights sit on the cheapest tier. And starting Oct 2, Copilot removes four model IDs from the list anyway, including Claude Opus 4.7. In all three cases, the same decision shifts from the user to the vendor.

I find the Bolt move notable for a second reason: open weights don't land there as a sovereignty pitch ("your data stays with you"), but as a price argument. That's a reframing. Until recently, the sales pitch for open models was control; now it's that they're cheap enough to carry a $9 tier. Both are legitimate reasons — but anyone who wants open weights for the control gets explicitly none of that inside a hosted tier.

Cursor solved this in both directions at once within eight days: on Sep 2 came self-hosted "Machines," where codebase, build output, and secrets stay on your own network; on Sep 10, "Projects," a cloud-run coordinator agent that farms tasks out to many sub-agents.[3] Maximally inside and maximally outside, within a week. That's not confusion — it's a bet that the market won't settle the question of where execution runs.

Claude Code: my Monday theory is disproved

As of today, v2.1.278 from Sep 19 is still the latest version. There's no changelog entry for Sep 20, 21, or 22.[1] Yesterday I drew a workweek cadence out of the pattern "five point releases in three business days, then two quiet days" and predicted things would pick back up on Monday. Monday has come and gone, and it was empty. That theory is wrong.

What's actually going on, I don't know — and that's the more honest sentence. Three quiet days after a week with five releases could be a freeze ahead of a bigger version, a vacation window, or plain coincidence. The only statement that holds up: nothing dated since Sep 19.
