Gemini 4 Carbon: now open to all, at a launch price

Google unveiled Gemini 4 Carbon about two weeks ago, at first only for a small group of cyber defenders. Since Nov 10 the model has been, in Simon Willison's words, "available to everyone else".[1] Willison calls it "Google's best ever Gemini" and released version 0.36 of his llm-gemini tool for it the same evening.[1]

  • Price today: $2 / $10 per million input/output tokens.[1]
  • Price from Dec 1: $4 / $20, double.[1]
  • Thinking levels: Low, Medium and High. The default is Medium.[1]
  • For comparison: Fable 5.2 costs $8 / $40.[2] So Carbon is four times cheaper per token today, and still two times cheaper from December.
Single source: The general release and the prices appear only at Willison so far. We found no Google primary source; the Google blog page returned only navigation. Several aggregators still speak of limited access. Willison is a very reliable source, but check price and access in your Google console before you commit to anything.

My take: This is the most interesting news of the week for your budget, not Fable 5.2. A model that sits just ahead of Opus 5.5 on the general index and costs a quarter of Fable belongs in every comparison test. The announced price jump is a classic launch discount. Google buys itself three weeks of attention. That is legitimate, but do not plan around today's price. For any decision that extends past December, count on $4 / $20.

Try it Before Dec 1, run one real task from your backlog once with Carbon (High) and once with Fable 5.2 (Medium). Note cost, duration and whether the result fits without rework. Why: Only this direct comparison shows whether the lower price makes up for the weaker quality.

The new table: Fable on top, Carbon ahead of Opus

Artificial Analysis measures models independently of the vendors. In the Intelligence Index the order today is:[1]

  • Fable 5.2 (Max): 65
  • Fable 5.2 (Xhigh and High): 62 each
  • Gemini 4 Carbon (High): 60
  • Opus 5.5 (Max): 59
  • Gemini 4 Carbon (Medium): 58
  • Opus 5.5 (Xhigh) and GPT-6 Astra (Max): 57 each
Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index — independent measurement, retrieved Nov 11, 2026 (the page gives no date)
Intelligence Index: A composite score from many tests: math, knowledge, logic, and a little code. It is like the average grade on a report card. It tells you who is good overall, but not who is the best programmer in the class.

Three caveats before you crown a winner:

  • Not a coding index: The page shows no separate coding score today. So we are comparing general intelligence, not programming skill.
  • No date: The page says only "Updated", without a day. So we give our retrieval date.
  • Effort matters: Carbon on High beats Opus 5.5 on Max. But Max costs Opus many thinking tokens. A comparison at equal levels would be fairer.

We did not retrieve SWE-bench Verified, Aider, LiveCodeBench or LMArena today. So we cannot cite an independent coding number for any of the new models.

My take: The gap between Fable 5.2 and Carbon is five points; the price gap is a factor of four. Yesterday's issue noted that Fable 5.2 uses very many tokens per task. Together, these two facts make Carbon a serious candidate for most everyday work. Fable stays for the truly hard task.

Prompting Fable 5.2 right: "The codebase is yours"

Anthropic has published a docs page "Prompting Claude Fable 5.2", and Willison quotes it on Nov 10.[1] One tip from it is directly useful for Claude Code. If you want the model to improve the structure as it works, tell it the code belongs to it. The suggested sentence: "This codebase is yours: what it's for and how it's built are your call, and you'll keep building on it."[1] According to the guide, the model is then more likely to merge duplicate code.[1]

The flip side shows in Willison's own test from Nov 9. At the Max level, Fable 5.2 used its entire budget of 128,000 tokens on thinking and delivered no image at all.[1]

Token budget: You give the model a cap on how many tokens it may use for an answer. Thinking and answering share this pot. It is like a student who spends the whole exam time thinking and then hands in a blank sheet.

My take: The "it's yours" sentence is a double-edged tool. In your own project that is exactly what you want: fewer duplicates, cleaner structure. In someone else's code, say at a client with clear rules, you want the opposite. There the model should only solve the task and not rebuild. So put the sentence deliberately in the CLAUDE.md of your own repos, not globally.

Try it Add the sentence to the CLAUDE.md of one of your own projects and give a refactoring task. Compare the diff with a run without the sentence. Why: It costs one line and shows right away whether the behavior changes.

Claude Code: quiet, but one hook detail is worth a look

There has been no new version since yesterday. The current one is 2.1.322 from Nov 10, with Fast Mode on Vertex AI in the regions global, us and eu.[1] We described the security fixes from 2.1.321 yesterday. If you have not updated yet: claude update.

One detail from 2.1.317 on Nov 3 got lost and suits unattended agents well: "Added onFailure: "block" for Agent SDK hook callbacks, so a callback that fails blocks the action instead of letting it run".[1]

Fail-open and fail-closed: A hook is a guard that checks before an action whether it is allowed. If the guard crashes, there are two options. Fail-open lets the action through anyway. Fail-closed stops it. It is like a store register: if the card reader fails, do you let customers leave without paying, or not?

My take: For a security hook, say a guard against secrets in a commit, fail-closed is the only sensible setting. Until now, an error in the hook could silently defeat the check. Together with permissions.denyInsteadOfPrompting from 2.1.319, you now get agents that refuse when unsure instead of hanging or waving things through.[1]

Try it Review your Agent SDK hooks that are meant to protect something, and set onFailure: "block" there. Why: A guard that gives up on its own error does not protect exactly when it matters.

Meta Muse Ember: the cheap helper

Meta unveiled Muse Ember on Nov 10, its smallest model.[1]

  • Price: $0.10 input and $0.50 output per million tokens, up to 200,000 tokens of prompt length.[1]
  • Above that: $0.30 / $1.50.[1]
  • Context: up to one million input tokens.[1]
  • Context for comparison: According to Willison, the same price as GPT-6 Luna and Claude Haiku 5.5.[1]

My take: According to our research, Meta gives no coding numbers. A model like this is still interesting for agent work, as a cheap sub-agent for searching, summarizing and reading logs. But Haiku 5.5 costs the same and is already built into Claude Code. Without an independent coding comparison, I see no reason to switch.

Open weights: Ripple-65B Preview

Ricursive Intelligence released Ripple-65B Preview on Nov 4, under Apache 2.0. The vendor cites 80.5 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and 79.1 on SWE-bench Pro v2.0 Hard.[1] These are vendor claims, and we know them only from a summary, not from the primary source. We found no independent measurement.

My take: Numbers around 80 from a 65B model would be a leap that deserves skepticism. Benchmark gaming and training contamination are the first things I would check at new labs with no track record. Wait for a measurement from Artificial Analysis or Vals.ai. With 65 billion parameters, the model needs more than 32 GB of RAM anyway; it does not fit on a regular Mac Mini.

Also: Latent Space reports on Nov 6 "Inkling 2 Small" from Thinking Machines, trained in 68 hours on 12 trillion tokens.[2] We know only the headline.
