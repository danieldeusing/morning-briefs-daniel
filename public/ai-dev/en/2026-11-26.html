FrontierCode 2.0: Six models on one board

For three weeks we have lacked a measurement for Fable 5.2, GPT-6.2 and Gemini 4 Neon that does not come from the vendor. Today there is one. Cognition, the company behind Devin, runs the FrontierCode 2.0 benchmark and shows six current top models from its chart there. "Each model is shown at its best reasoning effort," writes Cognition.[1]

FrontierCode 2.0: A test with real programming tasks from open-source projects, including security patches and performance optimizations.[1] It scores not only whether the code works, but also whether the model scopes the task sensibly and fits it cleanly into the existing project. Like a driving test: it is not only about whether you arrive, but also how you drive.
FrontierCode 2.0 (Cognition), each model at its best reasoning level, retrieved Nov 26, 2026

The order: Fable 5.2 at 50.2%, Opus 5.5 at 47.3%, Gemini 4 Neon at 46.2%, GPT-6.2 Astra at 44.2%, GPT-6.2 Sol at 43.8% and Gemini 4 Carbon at 42.6%.[1] The values appear in Cognition's chart. The page does not output a table of numbers. Four of the six values match other sources: Fable 5.2 with benchlm.ai, Neon with Google's own announcement, Astra and Sol with reports on the GPT-6.2 launch.[2][3]

One detail speaks for the measurement: Anthropic reports lower values for its own models than Cognition does. For Fable 5.2, Anthropic gives "49.1%" at Xhigh, for Opus 5.5 "45.5%".[4] A vendor that scores itself lower than a third party is probably not flattering anything here. Gaps of one to two points between two runs are noise. The order at the top stays the same in both measurements, though.

Caveat Cognition is not a neutral lab. With Devin, the company sells its own coding agent. The page is dated Oct 27, 2026. The entries for GPT-6.2 and Neon were added later; the page does not say exactly when. And the page gives no number of tasks.[1]

The real finding: More thinking does not always help

Reasoning effort: The setting for how long a model thinks before it answers. A higher level costs more tokens and therefore more money. Like a student with more time in an exam: usually it helps. Sometimes, though, he then writes things nobody asked for.

That is exactly what Cognition measures. The page says: "models can score lower at higher reasoning effort". And more precisely: "Pass rates on most criteria increase with model effort level, but pass rates on scope criteria decrease".[1] The failure rate on scope rises from 3.8% at the lowest level to 4.9% at Max.[1] So the model thinks more and then builds more than the task requires.

For Fable 5.2, benchlm.ai shows the costs, based on Cognition's data: "XHigh scores highest, at 50.2% for $17.73 instead of $55.87." Max reaches 48.2%.[2] So Max costs about three times as much per task and delivers two points less.

My take: this is the most useful benchmark number of the week, and not because of the ranking. If you set your agent to the highest level out of habit, you pay more and more often get changes nobody ordered. You may know this from daily work: the agent tidies up three files "on the side". I would not read too much into the ranking itself. Between 2nd and 5th place there are 3.5 points, and the harness here is Cognition's own, not yours.

Try it Run one real task from your repo once at the second-highest level and once at the highest. Compare the diff, not just the test result. Why: you see right away whether the highest level invents scope for you and whether the premium is worth it.

Deadlines at the turn of the month: Carbon gets more expensive than Neon

Google's own pricing page now confirms what yesterday came only from reports. Gemini 4 Carbon costs "$2.00 through November 30, 2026" per million input tokens and "$4.00 starting December 1, 2026". Output rises from $10 to $20, context caching from $0.10 to $0.20.[1]

Context caching: If you often send the model the same long text, for example your repo's context, the provider stores it temporarily. From the second time on, you pay only a small fraction for it. Like a baker who has already prepared the dough: the next loaf is faster and costs less.

My take: from Dec 1, Carbon is more expensive than Neon and scores worse on FrontierCode. If you use Carbon via API for coding today, there is hardly a reason not to test Neon first. Caveat: FrontierCode is a benchmark. If Carbon does something better in your project, only your own test will show it. Fable 5.2 costs almost three times as much per token as Neon. Whether the four points of difference are worth it depends on how expensive a wrong diff is for you.

It's Thursday, November twenty-sixth. A quiet day: there's no new announcement today from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google or the major coding tools. But there is, finally, a measurement that doesn't come from the vendor itself.

Cognition, the company behind Devin, shows six current top models from its chart on its FrontierCode two point oh benchmark. Fable five point two leads with fifty point two percent. Behind it come Opus five point five at forty-seven point three, Gemini four Neon at forty-six point two, GPT six point two Astra at forty-four point two, Sol at forty-three point eight, and Gemini four Carbon at forty-two point six. Interestingly, Anthropic itself reports somewhat lower numbers for its own models. Cognition does sell its own coding agent, though, so it's not a neutral lab.

The most important finding is a different one. More thinking time doesn't always help. According to Cognition, models at the highest reasoning level sometimes score worse, because they build more than the task calls for. Fable five point two hits fifty point two percent at the second-highest level, for just under eighteen dollars per task. At the highest level it's only forty-eight point two percent, for almost fifty-six dollars. The tip: try a real task at both levels and compare the diff.

There are two deadlines at the turn of the month. On December first, Google doubles the price of Gemini four Carbon, to four dollars per million input tokens and twenty dollars for output. That makes Carbon more expensive than the newer Neon, which costs three and fifteen dollars and scores better on FrontierCode. If you use Carbon for coding, test Neon first. And in Claude Code, managed plugin marketplaces will update themselves by default starting November thirtieth at the earliest, unless you turn that off.

On the tools side: Claude Code stays on the version from the twenty-fifth. OpenHands shipped version one point four six with a feedback button. Codex has published three alpha versions without notes, and a bigger release seems to be on the way. Cursor has been quiet since November sixth, and GLM five point four still has no open weights.
