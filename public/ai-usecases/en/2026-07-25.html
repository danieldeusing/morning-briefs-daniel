The vendors are buying themselves installers [1][2][3]

Four checks since May, one pattern — the most recent is a good three weeks old. On July 2, 2026, Microsoft launched Frontier Company — $2.5B and roughly 6,000 engineers, consultants, and industry experts whose sole job is getting Microsoft's existing AI tools running at customers; first partners: London Stock Exchange Group, Unilever, Land O'Lakes, Accenture.[1] The other three checks are considerably older — the wave began in May: Anthropic announced a $1.5B joint venture on May 4, 2026, on May 11 a deployment firm in the OpenAI orbit launched with over $4B and, via the Tomoro acquisition, bought in 150 engineers at once, and Amazon reportedly poured $1B into a parallel effort as of late June.[2] That fits with Presence from Jul 22, which OpenAI explicitly does not offer as self-service (details in yesterday's brief).[3]

Forward Deployed Engineer: Not a product you download, but a person who shows up at the customer's site and wires the AI into their systems — like a kitchen fitter who doesn't just deliver the cabinets but installs them. Job postings for exactly this role rose more than 800% between January and September 2025.[2]

Why the money is flowing there is explained by the same survey: 71% of executives at companies with $1B+ in annual revenue name organizational maturity as the main brake on AI performance — only 11% cite the technology itself; 85% report their data is fragmented or only loosely integrated.[2]

Model access cheap→ Integration expensive→ Vendors buy staff→ Mid-market stays unserved

Sample size and survey period aren't disclosed on the citing page, and the population is limited to large enterprises. Directional signal, not proof.
My take This is the most honest price tag of the year: the market values deployment work more than model access — and buys it exclusively for customers large enough to warrant a 6,000-person team. For an 80-person business, that solves exactly nothing. That's not a niche — that's the market twiceD stands in.

What measured agent impact looks like: 19 municipalities [4][5][6]

Date honesty upfront: this isn't news from today. Pilot phase March–May 2026, BMDS wrap-up Jun 15, 2026, Städtetag write-up Jul 8, 2026.[4][5] I'm bringing it forward anyway: it's the only material this summer that puts numbers on before-and-after by task type — while the vendor billions next door don't come with a single outcome figure.

The Federal Digital Ministry's Agentic AI Hub brought together 19 municipalities and nine AI startups in 20 pilot projects; nearly 600 participants had applied. As of the cutoff date, 70% of the solutions were in operational use, 80% processed real municipal data, 65% had been rolled out in under four weeks, and satisfaction stood at 95%.[4]

Article 15 GDPR access request: anyone can find out what data a government office or company holds on them — in full, within one month. In practice, that means searching every system, file, and inbox for one person and redacting anyone else's data. Like hunting an apartment for a single slip of paper — in every folder, every drawer, every box.

The impact figures are unusually concrete: for complex GDPR access requests, turnaround time dropped by more than 90% — from several weeks to a few hours, or per BMDS up to 28 hours of pure working time per case. For "long-term care assistance" applications, total turnaround time fell 45%, agency processing time fell 31%, and follow-up work on meeting minutes fell by about 78% (from 45 to 10 minutes). Seven municipalities modeled nearly 600 administrative processes in twelve weeks (Flensburg led with 264), at system costs of roughly one euro per case.[4][5]

Agentic AI Hub — measured time reduction by task type (pilot phase 03–05/2026; the GDPR figure is a floor value)
Methodology flag This is a funding program's self-evaluation, not an independent audit: baseline figures come from the agencies' own self-reporting, sample sizes are small (the security question rests on 15 of 18 evaluable cases), and programs are reluctant to report failures. The program's size also shifted: 18 pilots, 17 municipalities, 10 startups at kickoff, versus 20/19/9 in the wrap-up.[5] Munich's prototype (with Celonis, pre-screening immigration files) ran only on synthetic data — nothing is proven there.[6]
My take Two takeaways. First: half the solutions are considered transferable to other municipalities, and the BMDS is building a dynamic procurement system (a "digital department store") for purchases below the tender thresholds, by early 2027.[4] A timeline you can actually build a proposal around. Second: the best-measured case in the wrap-up — the Article 15 access request — isn't a public-administration-specific problem. Every German company past a certain size has the same problem with the same one-month deadline. That becomes Idea 1 below.

Two commissioned studies, one shared blind spot [7][8]

Avalara/Censuswide surveyed 1,505 CFOs and senior finance leaders (US, UK, India, Australia) from Jun 15–22, 2026, published Jul 21. The picture: 92% feel career pressure to show AI returns — but only 7% put governance ahead of speed. 44% are only "somewhat confident" they could explain their agents' actions to auditors or regulators, 23% say that after a serious agent failure accountability would be unclear or would fall on no one, 76% have no one internally who truly understands their own agents.[7]

Forrester (commissioned by FPT, Jul 8, 2026, n=397) measures the same thing from the operations side: only 26% are rated "advanced" at production operations, 41% cite integration complexity and 38% cite data silos as the biggest hurdle, 51% spend at least 5% of their IT budget on AI — and when it comes to proof, it gets thin: 35% collect no quantified metrics at all, 10% measure results "in no form."[8]

Methodology flag Both are commissioned studies with a sales angle: Avalara sells compliance automation ("your agents won't pass an audit"), FPT sells AI implementation ("only 26% are advanced"). Both are panel surveys based on self-reporting, and Germany isn't in either sample. Direction, yes; proof, no.
My take Despite the conflicts of interest, both say the same thing as the municipal wrap-up next door: whoever measures gets ahead; whoever doesn't measure is deploying blind. The municipalities could say "minus 45% turnaround" because they'd timed one task type beforehand. The first consulting product here isn't the model — it's the stopwatch before the pilot starts.

Gartner: $234B in software revenue hangs in the balance [9]

Since Jul 1, 2026, Gartner has been putting a number on what agentic AI costs software vendors: $234B in spending on enterprise applications is "at risk" by 2030 — roughly 20% of enterprise SaaS spending. Managing VP George Brocklehurst gets to the heart of it: "This breaks the link between user growth and revenue growth for a lot of vendors."[9]

Seat license: Software is paid for per user account — 50 employees, 50 licenses, like movie tickets: one per head. If an agent does the work of five people through an API, five seats sit empty — but the revenue from a full house is still expected.
Why this matters In 2027, customer budget negotiations won't be "licenses vs. projects" anymore — they'll be "which license does the agent make redundant." No one can answer that today — license-management tools count logins, and agents don't generate any. That becomes Idea 2 below.

EU AI Act: eight days until Aug 2 [10]

What actually happens on Aug 2, 2026 is smaller than originally planned after the "Digital Omnibus," but it's not nothing: the obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models and the AI Office's enforcement powers remain unchanged and take effect. What got postponed are the tough high-risk obligations: Annex III systems to Dec 2, 2027 (16 months later), product-embedded systems under Annex I to Aug 2, 2028.[10]

Project ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Article 15 access agent for the Mittelstand — on-prem

ELI5 & value: If a customer, applicant, or former employee asks "What data do you have on me?", a German company must respond in full within one month: search every system, compile everything, redact anyone else's personal data, draft the response letter. Today, that means a human digging through inboxes, file servers, ERP, and ticketing systems. The value isn't a guess — it's the best-measured number from the municipal program: over 90% shorter turnaround, up to 28 hours of work saved per case, at system costs of roughly one euro.[4] Plus avoided risk — missing the deadline is a finable offense.
Prototype (V0): (1) A real, completed access response from the pilot customer as a test case — including the question of where exactly the search was run. (2) Connectors for exactly three sources (mail archive, file server, one line-of-business system). (3) Person search and hit list via a locally running open model (Ollama/vLLM), so no personal data leaves the building. (4) Redaction proposal and response letter — always with human sign-off. (5) A complete log of which source was searched when; that log doubles as the evidence trail for regulators. Out of scope: erasure requests, data portability, multilingual responses.
Market & gap: The category is crowded: OneTrust (from ~$10,000/year), TrustArc (from ~$15,000/year), DataGrail (2,500+ prebuilt connectors), plus Ketch, MineOS, Securiti, Transcend.[13] In Germany, DataGuard (Munich) is the best-known "privacy-as-a-service" vendor.[14] What they share: their connectors target cloud/SaaS landscapes, and the product is at its core the workflow management of the request. The real work in the Mittelstand lives elsewhere — in the Exchange mailbox, on the file server that grew organically, in the ERP, exactly where no SaaS connector reaches. The gap: searching and redacting in the messy in-house estate, on-prem, for a company that isn't signing a $10,000 subscription for twelve requests a year.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High — with one clear warning. In favor: tight scope, one legal framework instead of industry know-how, project-based work (a realistic V0 per customer is 4–6 weeks solo) with low ongoing load — an access response is a triggered run, not a 24/7 channel. Runs entirely locally, so no GPU hosting risk. Against it, honestly: this is the most sensitive system in the house, a tool that by definition searches all personal data. It shouldn't be offered without a co-signing data-protection lawyer and a clean permissions model; the liability question belongs before the first line of code. Scale via templates per source system, one contractor per parallel customer. bmds.bund.de · expertinsights.com · legiscope.com

2 · SaaS license X-ray: which seats survive the agent?

ELI5 & value: Companies pay for software per user account. When an agent does the work through an API, the user disappears — but the bill stays, because no one knows which license is still needed. A "license X-ray" is the limited-scope stocktake for exactly that: what's still being used, which tasks are foreseeably moving to agents, which vendor is shifting to usage-based pricing? Value: saved subscription costs and a negotiating position before renewal. Gartner's $234B in at-risk software revenue is, from the customer side, exactly this savings potential.[9]
Prototype (V0): (1) Pull actual spend from accounting and credit-card statements — that's also where shadow IT shows up. (2) Usage signals per app from the existing identity provider (Entra ID/Google Workspace), deliberately limited to what's easy to pull. (3) Cross-check against the workflows the customer wants to automate anyway: which task in which tool goes away? (4) The result is one document: a cancel/downgrade list, a renewal calendar with negotiating leverage, two or three apps an agent will replace next year. Out of scope: ongoing monitoring, a dedicated platform, procurement handling.
Market & gap: SaaS management is real, but not huge — $4.58B (2025) → $9.37B (2030), 15.4% CAGR; market leaders are IBM, ServiceNow, and Flexera, plus specialists like Zluri, Torii, Productiv.[15] By its own positioning, Zylo targets organizations with 500+ employees and $5M in SaaS spend — the German Mittelstand falls below that.[16] The sharpest description of the gap comes from the market leader itself: Flexera writes that classic license optimization no longer works this way, because "your SaaS management tools were built around identity and login events. The new unit doesn't generate either one" — agents run on API keys and are invisible to login-based detection.[17] The gap: everyone inventories the present, no one answers the future question of "which seat goes away" — and below the Zylo threshold, nobody serves it at all. There, a two-week human review is the better-fit product, not an annual subscription.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. Pure advisory work: no runtime responsibility, no maintenance, no on-call duty — the project ends when the document is delivered. Effort 1.5–2.5 weeks per customer, about half the data-gathering part scriptable and reusable. The honest downside: no recurring revenue, and the method is copyable. Only sustainable as an annual repeat in the renewal cycle, or as a door-opener — whoever has seen a Mittelstand company's license list knows, before any competitor, which process is next in line for Idea 1. marketsandmarkets.com · flexera.com · ciodive.com

Why is the money flowing exactly there? Still, my take is clear. Right now the market is valuing deployment work more highly than model access itself — and it's only selling that work to customers large enough to justify a six-thousand-person team. For an eighty-person company, that solves exactly nothing.
