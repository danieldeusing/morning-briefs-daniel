Insurers are buying AI seats, not measured impact

The insurance sector is right now the best case study in applied AI — because in a single week, both halves of the story became visible. On top: AXA has been rolling out Microsoft 365 Copilot worldwide since July 20, and Legal & General signed for 10,000 licenses in late June. Two insurers, 10,000 licenses, zero metrics of their own [1][2]

On July 21, 2026, Insurance Business reported that AXA had begun a worldwide rollout of Microsoft 365 Copilot to its workforce — embedded into Teams, Outlook, and Word.[1] Four weeks earlier, on June 29, 2026, Legal & General signed a three-year contract with Microsoft: Copilot for 10,000 employees plus expanded Azure usage, staged — first the digital unit, then customer service and underwriting.[2]

Underwriting: the step where an insurer decides whether to accept a risk and at what price. Think of a bouncer with a calculator: not everyone gets in, and whoever does pays a different cover charge depending on risk. The job is mostly reading paperwork and checking it against rules — which is why it's the favorite use case for document AI.
What's missing here AXA hasn't published a single metric of its own for this rollout — no time savings, no error rate, no user count. The only number in the article belongs to a competitor: Aviva reportedly cut life-insurance review times "by about half"[1] — with a specialized tool, which says nothing about AXA's office assistant. The roughly 140,000 employees on AXA's earlier in-house build "Secure GPT" also aren't a confirmed Copilot user count. For Legal & General, secondary sources cite both June 16 and June 29 as the contract date; no primary announcement was reachable.[2]
My take The most expensive misunderstanding of the year is equating two entirely different things. The horizontal assistant (Copilot in Outlook) spreads small time gains thin across the whole workforce — which is exactly why they never show up in the group results. The specialized tool (Aviva's review assistant) touches one process whose turnaround time you can clock before and after; that's where the 50% comes from. Whoever justifies the first purchase with the second result gets an uncomfortable question from finance in twelve months. That's exactly the business opportunity here: the rollout is sold, the proof isn't (idea 1).

The gap between the boardroom and the desk [3]

On July 21, 2026, FinTech Global reported figures from the "2026 State of P&C Insurance Technology" report — 750 professionals surveyed across property and casualty insurance. The core finding is a perception gap: 91% of executives believe they're steering their portfolio in real time, but only 27% of underwriters agree. Similarly on rules: 93% of executives think underwriting guidelines are applied consistently, while 88% of employees say decisions regularly deviate — due to missing data and disconnected systems. And 89% admit to using unsanctioned AI tools.[3]

Methodology flag The report comes from Federato — a vendor that sells exactly the solution it's suggesting is needed (a portfolio-aware underwriting platform). The headline finding, "companies that fully embed AI in their workflow are 3.6 times more likely to achieve real portfolio control," is therefore marketing with data attached: self-reported, no disclosed sampling method, read via the trade press rather than the original.[3] What's solid is the gap between two respondent groups in the same questionnaire — no vendor invents that contradiction on purpose.
Licenses bought centrally→ Front-line process unchanged→ Employees reach for their own tools→ Leadership sees adoption, not reality
My take The 89% shadow AI figure is, for me, the number of the week, regardless of who paid for the survey: demand at the desk level is already there, it's just being served around IT rather than through it. A rollout that lands at the top but not the bottom doesn't produce refusal, it produces parallel operation — with customer data sitting in accounts nobody knows about. So the right question in a mid-market conversation isn't "are you using AI yet?" but "which tools are your people using that you didn't buy?"

Do 54.5% or 41% of German companies use AI? [4][5]

Both numbers are right — they just don't measure the same thing. The ifo Institute reported on June 5, 2026: 54.5% of companies in Germany use AI in their business processes, up from 40.9% a year earlier.[4] Bitkom put the figure at 41% among companies with 20+ employees on March 11, 2026, up from 17% a year before.[5] A thirteen-point gap on the same question — the explanation is in the methodology, not a contradiction.

Methodology flag Field timing: Bitkom surveyed in calendar weeks 2–6, i.e. January/February; ifo surveyed in May — three to four months later on a steeply rising curve. Panel: Bitkom phone-surveyed 604 companies with 20+ employees; ifo uses its business-cycle survey panel without stating a sample size or lower cutoff on the page cited. Question wording: "using AI in business processes" is broader than active operational use. Both are self-reported — no one verifies whether the marketing department's ChatGPT access really counts. What both independently show: roughly a doubling within twelve months.
ifo Institute, May 2026 — share of companies using AI in business processes, in percent (retail is shown only rounded there, so it's omitted from the chart)

Two details matter more for customer conversations than the headline. The size reversal: large companies lead (67.2%), but small firms (51.2%) use AI more often than mid-sized ones (47.2%) — plausible, since a ten-person shop just signs up for a subscription, while the 200-person company first has to clear works council, data protection, and IT security. And construction: from 7.1% three years ago to 39.8% — the industry with the worst digitization reputation has more than quintupled.[4]

My take The real finding is buried in a third ifo number: 22.5% of companies use exclusively free AI applications.[4] Paired with the 89% shadow AI figure on the left, that's a consistent picture — a significant share of reported "adoption" isn't a project at all, it's a privately set-up free account with company data sitting inside it. And at the same time, the best sales pitch out there: demand is proven, execution isn't.

Article 50: the operating manual arrived twelve days before the exam [6][7]

On July 20, 2026, the EU Commission published its final guidelines on the transparency obligations under Article 50 of the AI Act — meant to ensure compliance "in a consistent, effective, proportionate and uniform manner." The same page carries a code of practice on transparency for AI-generated content.[6] The obligations apply from August 2, 2026, six days from now; for legacy generative systems, the machine-readable marking requirement has a grace period until December 2, 2026.[7] The substance and deadlines were covered in full in yesterday's brief.

Code of practice: not a law, but a fill-in-the-blanks guide written together with industry — the regulation says "mark it machine-readably," the code says what that looks like in practice. Like an industry standard attached to a law: you don't have to follow it, but if you do, you have less to explain.
Source flag The often-cited fine range for Article 50 violations — up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover — could not be verified against the official regulation text (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), only against several matching compliance write-ups.[7] That's fine for a budget decision; for contract language, check Article 99 of the Official Journal directly.

Brazil: AI in the clinic means admin work, not diagnosis [8]

The twelfth TIC Saúde survey (CGI.br/Cetic.br, published 2026-05-12) measured for 2025: 18% of Brazilian health facilities used AI — 31% among those with more than 50 beds, and 29% in diagnostic and therapeutic support services. Among AI-using facilities, 76% work with generative models.[8]

The survey's own headline is also its caveat: use "is still concentrated in operational tasks" — scheduling, documentation, billing. That's the realistic sequence, and it applies just as much to Germany: where a mistake lands directly on a patient, AI arrives last; where it only reshuffles an appointment, it arrives first.

Methodology — praise, for once 3,270 facility leaders surveyed, fielded February through November 2025, run by Brazil's internet registry — no vendor survey, no sales interest. The most solid adoption number in this brief. The cost: fieldwork ended eight months ago, so the number is more of a floor than a snapshot.

Project ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Impact proof for already-purchased AI licenses

ELI5 & value: Thousands of companies have bought AI licenses for their workforce — AXA worldwide, Legal & General for 10,000 people, and per Bitkom, 41% of German mid-market companies with 20+ employees.[1][2][5] At the next budget meeting comes "and what did it get us?", and the answer is usually "people like it." An impact proof is a repeatable measurement method: pick three to five process types (drafting a quote, answering a complaint, writing minutes), measure turnaround time and rework before the rollout, then measure again after. Value: the customer either walks away with a documented budget or saves half the licenses. Either way it's money, deliverable in weeks.
Prototype (V0): (1) Two workshop days: pick process types where a before-number already exists (ticketing system, ERP timestamps, CRM status). (2) A measurement plan with a control group — one department with the license, one without, same process type. (3) Pull usage data from existing telemetry rather than building new: Microsoft's Copilot adoption report provides active users, actions per week, and the Power/Habitual/Novice segments, benchmarked against the top 25% of similar companies.[11] (4) Overlay the process metric against the usage metric — that's the value contribution. (5) One page per process type: before/after, cost per license, a recommendation to "expand, reallocate, or cancel." Out of scope: model training, prompt libraries, change-management programs.
Market & gap: Measuring usage is solved, and Microsoft owns it: the Copilot Adoption Report counts active users, actions, and feature distribution, and defines a "power user" as someone with 15+ actions per week in at least 9 of 12 weeks — requiring a Viva Insights analyst role plus Power BI.[11] That's exactly the gap: the tool answers "how much is being clicked," never "what got cheaper as a result." Demand is quantified — per Bitkom, only 52% of AI-using companies can even identify a measurable contribution to business success.[5] Nearly half can't. The big consultancies sell transformation programs here; for a 150-person company with 40 licenses, that's three sizes too big.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. Pure consulting work with a clear start and end, no ongoing infrastructure, no on-call burden. Telemetry comes from the customer's existing contract, the output is a document. Realistically 2–3 weeks per customer solo, then zero maintenance load; scales via a reusable measurement template plus a contractor for data prep. Honest downside: a one-time engagement with no recurring revenue — a door-opener, not a mainstay. And you have to be willing to say "cancel 15 licenses"; that's exactly what makes the recommendation credible. learn.microsoft.com · bitkom.org · insurancebusinessmag.com

2 · Pre-screening assistant for small insurers, brokers, and underwriting agencies

ELI5 & value: Before an insurer accepts a risk, someone reads a stack of paperwork — questionnaire, business description, prior-claims list, expert reports — and checks it against underwriting rules. Most applications are clearly acceptable or clearly outside policy; only a fraction is genuinely a judgment call. A pre-screening assistant sorts the stack in advance, pulls out the decision-relevant fields, and flags contradictions — a human still decides. Value: the most solid number in this whole industry comes from exactly this process type — Aviva cut life-insurance review time "by about half."[1] For an underwriting agency with eight case handlers, that's half a headcount without a new hire.
Prototype (V0): (1) One line of business, one application type, 200 real historical cases with known outcomes as a test set. (2) Extraction via an existing document model, not built from scratch. (3) Underwriting rules kept beside the model — not hidden in a prompt, but as a table the business side edits itself. (4) Output is a draft for a decision with a source reference to the page in the document, never an acceptance or rejection. (5) Measure against the 200 historical cases: how often the pre-sort is right, how often it misses something. Out of scope: pricing, policy administration integration, automatic issuance.
Market & gap: This category is crowded and well-funded — Sixfold and Federato for case triage at P&C insurers, Guidewire UnderwritingCenter as the end-to-end standard, Majesco as a rules engine, Earnix for pricing, Indico, Ocrolus, and SortSpoke at the document layer.[12] Market-size figures are floating around but only work as a rough direction: $14.99B (2025) to $246.3B (2035) is a projection aggregated across two third-party sources from a vendor blog — the range itself shows how loosely defined the category is.[12] More important is the target: all of them aim at carriers with billion-dollar premium books and dedicated program teams. The gap: German underwriting agencies, broker pools, and specialty insurers with 20–200 employees, who won't adopt a US platform or take on an integration project — and where the Federato numbers above (91% vs. 27%, 89% shadow AI) describe what's actually happening.[3] German contract law, German documents, EU hosting: twiceD's home turf.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium — deliberately not "high." In favor: tight scope, purchased heavy components, one customer is enough as a reference, and the process type comes with the before-number built in. Against it, honestly: a live system inside a regulated process — traceability for every recommendation, a version log of the rules, someone to call when the pre-sort gets worse. Realistically 6–8 weeks for V0, then half a day per week per customer; around three parallel installs, a dedicated contractor becomes necessary. Viable — but only with a support contract, and only as long as the assistant pre-sorts rather than decides. getperspective.ai · fintech.global · insurancebusinessmag.com

It's Monday, July twenty-seventh. Insurance is today's best case study in applied AI, because in the same week, both halves of the story showed up at once: the buying and the reality.

First, the buying. On July twenty-first, the trade outlet Insurance Business reported that AXA had begun rolling out Microsoft 365 Copilot worldwide to its workforce, embedded in Teams, Outlook, and Word. Four weeks earlier, on June twenty-ninth, the British insurer Legal and General had signed a three-year contract with Microsoft: Copilot for ten thousand employees, plus expanded Azure usage, rolled out in waves.

And now here's what's missing from both announcements: a number. AXA hasn't published a single metric of its own for this rollout. No time savings, no error rate, not even a user count. The only figure in the article belongs to a competitor: Aviva reportedly cut review times in life insurance by about half. That number belongs to Aviva, and it belongs to a specialized underwriting tool, not an office assistant. To me, that's the most expensive misunderstanding of the year: the broad assistant spreads small time savings thinly across the whole workforce, which is exactly why they never show up in the company's bottom line. The specialized tool touches a single process whose turnaround time you can clock before and after. Whoever justifies the first purchase with the second result is going to get an uncomfortable question from finance in twelve months.

The other half of the story comes from a survey of seven hundred fifty professionals in property and casualty insurance, also reported by FinTech Global on July twenty-first. Ninety-one percent of executives believe they have real-time control over their portfolio. Among underwriters, only twenty-seven percent agree. Ninety-three percent of executives think underwriting guidelines are applied consistently; eighty-eight percent of employees say decisions regularly deviate. And eighty-nine percent admit to using AI tools that nobody signed off on.

A methodology note belongs right here: the survey comes from Federato, a vendor that sells exactly the solution the report points toward. That's marketing with data attached. What is solid, though, is the gap between two groups of respondents in the very same questionnaire — a vendor has little reason to invent that particular contradiction on purpose.
