The Workforce Asks for a Brake — and the Company Co-Signs

On pacingthefrontier.com sits a sentence framed as a request: "We request that the U.S. government support an international effort to develop the technical and governance tools needed to deliberately pace the frontier of automated AI development." The letter's reasoning is unusually direct: "There is a real risk that capability development rapidly accelerates beyond our ability to understand or control the resulting systems."[1] As of my check on 07/30/2026, the site lists exactly 1,293 signatories — employees of OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and Meta AI.[1]

Among the names are not the doubters on the sidelines, but the people at the curve itself: Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei along with co-founders Jared Kaplan and Jack Clark, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, Meta's chief scientist Shengjia Zhao, and Google's head of AI safety, Anca Dragan.[3] Within hours, two employers followed suit: OpenAI stated that acceleration could get high enough "that the world will need to pace the rate of AI advancement," and said it hoped to contribute to work led by the US government.[2] Anthropic wrote that it supports the petition — signed by its CEO, several co-founders, and senior staff — and pointed to its own research on recursive self-improvement.[4]

Recursive self-improvement: An AI system that doesn't just solve tasks, but takes over developing the next system — whose successor then gets built even faster in turn. Like a toolmaker who builds better machine tools, which he uses to build even better machine tools: the first few rounds are easy to follow, but past some point nobody understands the machine that comes out anymore. Those are exactly the rounds the letter means by "automated AI development."
Sourcing note The count is a live number: signing is ongoing. On 07/28, media reported 1,100, 1,132, 1,134, and 1,178 signatories — not a contradiction, just different snapshot times.[5] Anyone citing a number has to cite the timestamp too; here's mine. I only have the Anthropic statement via two independent trade reports quoting identical wording — on anthropic.com's newsroom overview, I found no matching post as of 07/30.[4]
My take Two readings, and both are true. Taken seriously: the people with the best view of the curve are saying, in public, that the curve could outrun oversight — that's the clearest signal to come out of the labs so far, and Anthropic ties it to its own research. Taken strategically: a workforce petition that the employer co-signs the same day isn't dissent from below, it's coordinated positioning. Whoever defines the pacing tools defines who gets to cross the frontier — and nothing is being slowed today: the same newsroom page that shows nothing on "Pacing" carries, from the same days, announcements about open weights and a new flagship model.[4] Consequence for a buyer: no rollout changes this quarter. But if the builders themselves say capability jumps could outrun control, then a three-year lock-in to one vendor is the wrong contract shape. Not because of doomsday — because of predictability. That becomes idea 2, below.

On Sunday, Brussels Gets the Access That Washington Is Being Asked For

Update since yesterday The letter is addressed to the US government and asks for tools that don't exist yet. In Europe, a toolkit that's already decided takes effect in three days: as of 08/02/2026, per the implementation timeline, "the remainder of the AI Act" applies, Article 6(1) excepted.[6] The obligations for general-purpose AI models themselves have applied since 08/02/2025; providers whose models were already on the market before that have until 08/02/2027.[6]

A common legal reading dates the start of enforcement against model providers to that Sunday: requests for information, model access, recalls — capped at the greater of €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual revenue.[7]

General-purpose AI model (GPAI): A model that isn't built for one task, but serves as a raw material for many — like steel, which can become bridges or spoons. That's why it's regulated twice: the steel-mill operator (model provider) and the bridge builder (application). Anyone who fine-tunes an open model and redistributes it can legally turn from a bridge builder into a steel mill.
What's documented here — and what isn't The timeline I pulled says only that, as of 08/02/2026, "the rest of the Regulation applies." It does not itself call that date the start of GPAI enforcement; that link comes from a law-firm/vendor analysis.[7] That's good enough for scheduling: the deadline is the same either way. It's not good enough for a statement to a contract partner — for that, read the Regulation's own application article. The Article 50 transparency obligations, which take effect on the same date, were covered in detail in the 07/29 brief; not repeated here.

DMA Thread: Google Can't Sue in Advance [8][9]

The specification decisions from 07/16/2026 were covered in detail in the 07/26 brief. What's new is the lever behind them: on 07/08, the EU General Court, in the Apple case, established a sequencing rule — a gatekeeper can't get a DMA obligation reviewed by a court before the Commission issues the actual enforcement decision.[9] In practice, that means: the timeline runs while you litigate, not instead of it. The scale is on the Commission's own page — roughly 60% of EU users with an Android device are affected, and rival assistants are meant to be invokable by voice command, "similar to 'Hey Google'," and allowed to take actions in apps.[8]

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Agent Acceptance Testing: the Rollout Gate for Purchased AI Agents

ELI5 & value: More and more mid-sized companies are buying AI agents — from their ERP vendor, from an agency, as an add-on module in the CRM. What's missing is the moment someone gets to say: pass or fail. An acceptance test is a fixed set of real cases the agent has to solve, with a pass threshold agreed in writing beforehand — before go-live and before the final invoice. Like a building inspection with a defect list instead of a walkthrough staged by the developer. The value: it ends the pilot project that runs for a year without anyone being able to say whether it actually works.
Prototype (V0): (1) 30–50 real cases from the customer's own history — tickets, emails, orders — each with the known correct outcome, deliberately including the ugly ones. (2) A test run against the agent's actual interface, nothing built in-house: Langfuse (open, self-hostable) for traces and evaluation runs, LangSmith if the customer is already in the LangChain ecosystem. (3) Only three scoring classes: solved correctly, confidently wrong (the expensive class), escalated to a human. (4) The threshold agreed before the run, not negotiated afterward. (5) Monthly repeats with the same case set — the regression test for when the vendor swaps the model under the hood. Deliberately out of scope: scientific evaluation, red-teaming, and "model judges model" as the sole arbiter on money-relevant cases.
Market & gap: The tools exist and are good. Langfuse describes itself as "open, self-hostable, and extensible" with tracing plus evaluation via "LLM-as-a-judge, code evaluators, user feedback, and manual labeling"; LangSmith sells "Score and improve agent performance" with a free tier for development and pricing by trace volume, targeting "development teams and enterprises building AI agents."[15] That's exactly the gap: both are built for whoever builds the agent. The mid-sized company doesn't build, it buys — and 16.6% of them already run agents, up from 8.7% two years earlier.[14] For these buyers, nobody sells the acceptance procedure: the case set, the threshold, the report a managing director signs off on. The output isn't a dashboard — it's a signed acceptance protocol plus a repeatable regression run.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. 5–8 days per customer, most of it spent on the case set — the data sits with the customer, so it's not a sourcing problem. The heavy technology is bought (Langfuse self-hosted); what gets built is the integration and the scoring. Maintenance runs about 2 hours a month per customer for the regression run, billable as a small retainer and therefore predictable rather than a nuisance. Honest weakness: half the work is a workshop, not code. If a customer can't say what "correct" is, the project fails — better to decline than to invent the truth yourself. Scale via industry templates for the case set plus one contractor per industry. langfuse.com · langchain.com · mittelstandsbund.de

2 · The Migration Test: Proving a Vendor Switch Takes Two Days

ELI5 & value: Almost every AI function at a mid-sized company today hangs off the API of exactly one vendor. If price, terms, availability, or capabilities change, switching costs weeks nobody budgeted for. The offer: measure what a migration actually costs, and make one workflow provably migration-ready. Not "we're buying a second car," but "we know we could drive the other one — we've done it once." The value is twofold: negotiating leverage at contract renewal, and a solid answer to the outage question that customers and auditors are now asking.
Prototype (V0): (1) An inventory of every place a model API gets called — usually fewer than ten. (2) A gateway in front of it: LiteLLM, open source, "One OpenAI-compatible API to 140+ providers and 1,800+ models," including hard budgets per key and team.[16] The switching layer thereby delivers a cost brake as a side effect. (3) Run idea 1's case set against vendor B — including an EU-hosted or open-weight option. (4) A report on the difference: quality, response time, cost, which prompts break. (5) One workflow actually migrated, as proof. Out of scope: multi-cloud, running two vendors in parallel, self-hosted GPU inference — a different mandate.
Market & gap: The pipework is a commodity and free: LiteLLM claims to cover 140+ providers and roughly 1,900 models, and the hyperscalers have their own gateways.[16] That's the point — what's sold is the pipe, not the proof. For a 150-person company, what's missing is someone to run the inventory, run the comparison on their own cases, and hand over a document: "workflow X survives a vendor switch in two days, workflow Y doesn't, and here's why." Demand rests on two things from this brief: the builders themselves consider it possible that capability development could outrun control[1] — in that kind of world, a three-year single-vendor lock-in is the wrong shape. And as of 08/02, the rest of the AI Act applies to anyone offering a model in the EU[6]: the supplier's compliance status becomes your own risk — one you should be able to exit. twiceD's leverage: German contract and data-protection reflexes, plus the EU-hosted option as a serious choice instead of a footnote.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium to high. V0 in two to three weeks; the gateway is open source and off the shelf, what gets built is the inventory, test runs, and report. No service to operate, no on-call rotation, maintenance is practically zero — repeat on request whenever a vendor changes its terms. Honest risk: the second run costs real tokens, which is a budget line, not an afterthought. And the deliverable is a document — harder to sell than software. So offer it as a two-week fixed-price audit, reusing idea 1's case set. Scale via a contractor for the actual migration work per customer. litellm.ai · pacingthefrontier.com · artificialintelligenceact.eu
