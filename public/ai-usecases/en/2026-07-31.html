On Sunday, the Rest Applies — and It Applies to Deployers [1][2][3]

The AI Act entered into force on 08/01/2024. The Commission writes that it will be "fully applicable 2 years later on 2 August 2026, with some exceptions."[1] The Regulation tracker puts it more precisely: "The remainder of the AI Act starts to apply, except Article 6(1)."[2] Both name the same day. That's the moment the Regulation stops being a topic for model makers in California and becomes one for whoever runs a support bot on their website.

Article 50 (transparency obligations): the only part of the Regulation that practically affects anyone shipping anything with AI in it. Core rule: a person interacting with an AI system has to be told so — unless it's obvious. Machine-generated images, audio, video, and text must be machine-readably marked as synthetic, deepfakes additionally labeled visibly. Like the ingredients list on a package: it bans nothing. It only bans hiding it.
Chatbot starts→Notice before the first sentence→Generated content marked→Deepfake labeled

The penalty framework in Article 99 has three tiers: up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual revenue for prohibited practices, up to €15 million or 3% for the remaining obligations — that's where Article 50 sits —, up to €7.5 million or 1% for false statements to authorities. Whichever figure is higher governs; for SMEs, the lower one.[3]

Date trap The research on this deadline is a small teaching example. A vendor blog dates the chatbot disclosure to 07/10/2026 and has been cited ever since. That date doesn't appear on the Commission page or in the Regulation tracker; both name 08/02/2026. Anyone who takes a compliance deadline from a blog is planning against a figure nobody has substantiated.

What doesn't arrive on Sunday is the expensive part. After the Digital Omnibus deal, the Commission writes: "Rules for systems used in certain high-risk areas … will apply from 2 December 2027" and "For systems integrated into products such as lifts or toys, the rules will apply from 2 August 2028."[1] The Regulation tracker still lists the original date of 08/02/2027 for Article 6(1) and hasn't been updated here.[2] Where they conflict, the Commission wins.

Annex III vs. Annex I: the Regulation sorts "high risk" into two buckets. Annex III is the list of sensitive use cases — candidate screening, creditworthiness, education, critical infrastructure, law enforcement. Annex I is AI built into a product that already needs CE certification anyway: elevator, toy, medical device. First bucket from December 2027, second from August 2028.

My take: the delay isn't a free pass, it's a reversal of order — first the cheap obligation, then the expensive one. That's exactly why Sunday matters: Article 50 costs an afternoon and a document; ignoring it costs nothing, until it costs something. Anyone selling in Europe should be able to say on Monday where in their own house a machine talks to a human. That's not a legal question — it's an inventory question.

Seven Gigafactories, One Billion in Real Money [4][5][6]

The EuroHPC Joint Undertaking reports for 07/30/2026: "The EuroHPC Joint Undertaking (EuroHPC JU) has launched a call for tenders to select consortia that will build and operate future AI Gigafactories (AIGF) in the European Union."[4] The number of sites was raised from the original plan of four to five to up to seven, and the total volume stands at roughly €30 billion.[5]

AI Gigafactories — where the roughly €30 billion is supposed to come from (as of 07/30/2026). Only the first bar is already committed; the other three are expectations.

That's the real story. Firmly committed today is €1 billion from the current EU budget. Roughly €4 billion is supposed to come from the next multiannual financial framework, about €5 billion from the member states, roughly €20 billion from private investors. A senior Commission official put it bluntly: "We cannot pre-empt the decisions about the next MFF. We gave you our best estimate."[5]

MFF (Multiannual Financial Framework): the EU's budget plan, set for seven years at a time. The next one hasn't been decided yet. So four of the ten public billions sit in a budget that's still being negotiated — like a down payment planned against the salary of a job you don't have yet.

Construction is planned to start in early 2027, operations from mid-2028. The design assumption of "well over 100,000 advanced AI chips" per site comes from the Commission's 2025 communication — including the addendum that the chips are "most of which are manufactured outside Europe."[6] The EU has letters of intent with Nvidia, AMD, and Qualcomm; euronews notes concerns about dependence on individual suppliers.[5]

Unconfirmed The reporting cites 11/12/2026 as the bid deadline. It doesn't appear in the EuroHPC notice I was able to access, and the official Commission press release returned only the page header with no content when I checked. I'm therefore treating the date as unconfirmed.

My take: a sovereignty program built on American silicon, two-thirds of whose funding consists of expectations, isn't capacity — it's a letter of intent with a concrete foundation. In practical terms: no usable GPU-hour comes out of this before 2028. Anyone who needs data sovereignty today builds it on existing European hardware, not on a tender.

China Regulates the Agent Like a Device — Including Recalls [8][9]

The Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC), together with the planning agency NDRC and the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT), published "Implementation Opinions" on the standardized application and innovative development of intelligent agents on 05/08/2026 — with "19 application scenarios across scientific research, industrial development, consumption, public well-being, and social governance."[8] A law-firm analysis dates it taking effect to 07/15/2026 and describes the core this way: agents in sensitive sectors — "healthcare, transportation, media, public safety" — are subject to "filing requirements, mandatory testing, product recalls, and oversight by both cyberspace regulators and sector-specific authorities."[9]

Sourcing The date 07/15 comes from a law-firm summary, not the government notice — the gov.cn release only names the issue date of 05/08/2026 and gives no effective date. I'm therefore treating the deadline as plausible, but secondarily sourced.
Product recall, applied to software: a recall is what a carmaker does when a model line has a dangerous flaw — back to the shop, fix removed. Applied to an AI agent, that means: an authority can order a specific agent shut down or rolled back to an older version for all customers. That presupposes the operator even knows where their agents are running and how to pull them back.

My take: the two regulatory philosophies this week sit side by side instructively. Europe regulates the model and the disclosure. China regulates the agent as a thing with a serial number — who filed it, who tested it, how you pull it back. As law, I prefer the European version. As operational discipline, the Chinese question is the more useful one: whoever can't say which agents are running in their own house, with what permissions and what way back, doesn't have a compliance problem — they have an inventory problem, independent of any regulation.

41 Percent — and Three Numbers Rarely Cited Alongside It [10][11]

The Bitkom figure is making the rounds again this week, usually with no date attached. So here it is: the press release is dated Berlin, March 11, 2026. It says "41 percent already use artificial intelligence," "A year ago, only 17 percent had AI in use," and "48 percent are planning or discussing deployment." For "three-quarters (77 percent) of the companies currently using AI, this has improved their competitive position."[10]

From the same survey, cited far less often: "A third (33 percent) found that AI led to markedly higher costs," and "19 percent say they've cut jobs because of AI."[10]

Methodology A phone survey of 604 companies with 20+ employees in Germany, fieldwork from week 2 to week 6 of 2026, commissioned by the digital industry association itself — the same association whose members sell AI. Every figure is self-reported by respondents, not measured: "uses AI" doesn't mean "has changed a process." And the number is four and a half months old.

The counter-check comes out wild: a Hamburg consultancy for AI automation arrived, on 01/28/2026, at "94% of German Mittelstand firms still without AI implementation."[11] That's not a fresh survey, but a compilation of studies from 2020–2025 — from a vendor that sells exactly this gap. Not comparable to Bitkom, and not to be passed on without that caveat.

My take: the interesting pair of numbers isn't 41 against 17, but 77 against 33. The same companies report, by a majority, a competitive advantage and, in a third of cases, higher costs than expected. Both can be true if nobody does the math — the advantage is felt, the costs are on the invoice. That gap is a consulting business, and an unglamorous one. See idea 2.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Article 50 Transparency Sign-Off — Fixed Price, Two Days

ELI5 & value: From Sunday, every chatbot, every machine-generated text, and every generated image in the EU has to be recognizable as what it is.[1][2] One exception: for synthetic content from systems already on the market before Aug 2, 2026, the marking obligation itself doesn't kick in until Dec 2, 2026 — the chatbot-disclosure duty has no such grace period.[16] For a mid-sized company with a support bot and an image generator in marketing, that's not a governance question, it's an afternoon of work — plus a document proving you did it. The value isn't in the penalty range of €15 million or 3% (nobody pulls that against a 150-person company), but in the question that'll be on every tender form starting this fall: "How do you comply with Article 50?" Whoever can produce two pages is done; whoever can't, negotiates.
Prototype (V0): (1) Inventory — every place a machine talks to a human or generates synthetic content: website bot, voicebot on the phone system, AI summaries in the customer portal, marketing images, generated product copy. Experience shows fewer than ten touchpoints, two of which management doesn't know about. (2) For each touchpoint, define the mandatory notice and its placement — before the first sentence, not in the imprint. (3) Technical marking with existing building blocks instead of a custom build: C2PA/Content Credentials for image and video, the generator's own labeling features, visible markup in the UI. (4) A two-page proof: touchpoint, implementation, screenshot, date, responsible person. (5) Follow-up in six months. Out of scope: anything high-risk-related (which has until 2027/2028[1]), model documentation, GDPR reassessment.
Market & gap: governance platforms are plentiful and good: Credo AI, Holistic AI, Modulos, Trustible, and the European LatticeFlow AI (Zurich), Saidot (Helsinki), and trail.[12] They just play in a different price class: vendor comparisons cite $80,000–$300,000 a year for enterprise suites, from roughly $50,000 for a focused mid-market deployment, and $25,000–$120,000 for implementation projects; Credo AI doesn't publish pricing at all.[12][13] Source honesty: ranges from comparison portals, not list prices — order of magnitude, not a quote. That's exactly where the gap is: for "one chatbot, one image generator," an annual license for a governance registry is absurd. The mid-sized company doesn't need a registry, it needs a sign-off — two days, fixed price, a document in German, from someone who can build the change themselves. Nobody in this segment is selling that right now.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. No hosting, no operations, no on-call — one to two days per customer, maintenance practically zero, follow-up as a small retainer. Two honest weaknesses: a short half-life (the window is the second half of 2026, after which labeling becomes standard and the price drops), and twiceD can't provide legal advice — the proof is technical, the legal assessment belongs to a lawyer. Both belong in the offer. Scale via an industry template plus one contractor. digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu · artificialintelligenceact.eu · aicompliancevendors.com

2 · AI Cost Autopsy: What a Case Really Costs

ELI5 & value: a third of German AI users report markedly higher costs than expected — in the same survey where three-quarters report a competitive advantage.[10] Both statements sit side by side unchallenged, because almost nobody does the math. The offer: for one concrete workflow, determine the real cost per case — tokens and API, but also the minutes a human spends on review and rework — and set it against the promised savings. The result is either a solid "it pays off, here's the number" or a well-founded shutdown. Either is worth more than the gut feeling.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick one workflow with real volume — support tickets, quote generation, invoice extraction. (2) Measure the machine side: a gateway in front, LiteLLM or Helicone (drop-in proxy, one line of setup), Langfuse for traces — all open and self-hostable, nothing custom-built.[15] (3) Measure the human side: a sample of 50 cases, timed review and correction, error rate by category. (4) Comparison baseline: the same workflow before AI, from old time-tracking records. (5) One page of results: cost per case before/after, the volume where it tips, three levers. Out of scope: company-wide FinOps, GPU capacity planning, cross-department cost allocation.
Market & gap: the tool category is mature: Vantage, CloudZero, Finout, and Amnic on the FinOps side, Helicone, Langfuse, and LiteLLM on the developer side.[14][15] But their stated target question is expense attribution — "how much does our Q&A chatbot cost per 1,000 inferences?"[15] That's exactly where it stops. Cost per request isn't cost per case, and cost per case isn't value per case. The most expensive line item is almost always the human quality check — and that's on no cloud bill, so it shows up on none of these dashboards. Gap for twiceD: not another dashboard, but a one-time, independent calculation for one workflow that a managing director understands without a translator. The vendor of the AI function will never run that calculation.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. Four to six days per customer, no ongoing operation, no on-call; the measurement tools are open and run at the customer's site. Honest risk: the result can come back "doesn't pay off" — unpleasant to sell, and probably why almost nobody offers this. So strictly fixed price, regardless of the finding; the independence is the product. Second: the human-side time measurement is fieldwork, not software — without access to staff and backing from the top, it's better to decline. Scale via measurement templates per workflow type and one contractor for the sampling. vantage.sh · amnic.com · bitkom.org

It's Friday, July thirty-first. In two days, on Sunday, a two-year transition period under the European Union's AI Act runs out. From August second, the rest of the regulation applies — and it no longer targets only the companies that build AI models, it applies to anyone who deploys an AI system in Europe.

The most important obligation in practice is also the least dramatic one. Article fifty says that if you're talking to a machine, you have to be told. Machine-generated images, audio, and text need a machine-readable label marking them as synthetic, and deepfakes need a visible label on top of that. Think of it like the ingredient list on a package: it doesn't ban anything, it just bans hiding it. The penalty for getting this wrong tops out at fifteen million euros or three percent of worldwide annual revenue, whichever is higher.

What isn't arriving on Sunday is the expensive part. The high-risk rules got pushed back after the EU's Digital Omnibus deal — rules for sensitive uses like screening job candidates or assessing creditworthiness now start in December of twenty twenty-seven, and rules for AI built into products like elevators or toys start in August of twenty twenty-eight. One warning: several blogs have been repeating a July tenth date for the chatbot disclosure rule. That date doesn't appear anywhere on the European Commission's own page, and it doesn't appear in the standard regulation tracker either. Both point to August second. If you're planning around a deadline you found on a blog, you're planning around a number nobody can back up.

On Thursday, the EU's supercomputing consortium, EuroHPC, opened the bidding for up to seven AI gigafactories, with a headline price tag of around thirty billion euros. Here's the catch: only one billion of that is actually locked in today. Another four billion is supposed to come from the next multi-year EU budget, which hasn't even been agreed yet. Five billion is expected from member states, and twenty billion from private investors. A senior EU official put it plainly: they can't get ahead of decisions that haven't been made about that next budget, so this is their best guess, not a commitment. Construction is meant to start in early twenty twenty-seven, with the plants running by mid-twenty twenty-eight, built largely on Nvidia, AMD, and Qualcomm chips. A sovereignty project financed two-thirds by hope, running on American silicon.

China offers the sharpest contrast this week. Since mid-July, agents used in health care, transportation, media, and public safety fall under a new regime: they have to be filed with regulators, tested, and — this is the striking part — recalled, the same way a car company recalls a defective model. An authority can order a specific agent pulled or rolled back for every customer running it. Europe is regulating the model and the disclosure. China is regulating the agent itself, like a physical product with a serial number. As a legal framework, I prefer the European approach. As a discipline for how you actually run your business, the Chinese question is the more useful one to ask yourself: do you know every agent running inside your company, and could you pull it back if you had to?

One more number making the rounds again this week, usually stripped of its date: forty-one percent of German companies now use artificial intelligence, up from seventeen percent a year earlier, according to the German digital industry association Bitkom. What almost nobody repeats alongside it: a third of those companies say AI ended up costing significantly more than expected, and nineteen percent say they've cut jobs because of it. The real story isn't forty-one versus seventeen — it's seventy-seven versus thirty-three: the share reporting a competitive edge against the share reporting a cost overrun. Both can be true at once when nobody actually runs the numbers.

For twiceD, two project ideas follow directly from this week's deadline. The first is a fixed-price, two-day transparency check-off against Article fifty — the kind of two-page proof a mid-sized company can hand over the next time a client's procurement form asks how they comply, instead of paying for an enterprise governance platform they don't need. The second is a cost autopsy: pick one real workflow, measure what it actually costs per case, including the time a human spends checking and fixing the output, and compare that to the savings that were promised. Either it holds up, or it doesn't — and knowing which is worth more than a feeling.
