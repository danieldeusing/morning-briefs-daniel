It's in Effect — the Difference from Yesterday Is One Sentence [1][2][3][4]

Update since yesterday Yesterday this space held a deadline, today it holds law. In substance, exactly one thing has changed: the notice a chatbot has to give before its first sentence is no longer a recommendation. The Regulation tracker still lists, for today's date, "The remainder of the AI Act starts to apply, except Article 6(1)" — checked this morning, no last-minute reprieve.[1]

Article 50 in one sentence: anyone talking to a machine has to be told — unless it's obvious anyway; and machine-generated images, audio, video, and text must be marked as such. Like the ingredients list on a package: it bans nothing, it only bans hiding it.

The practical question isn't a legal one, it's an inventory one: where exactly does a machine talk to a human in our company? Website bot, auto-reply in the ticketing system, AI summary in the customer portal, generated product copy, marketing images — management usually doesn't know about two of those.

Inventory→Notice before the first sentence→Marking generated content→Two-page proof

My take: the penalty framework — up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual revenue, whichever is higher[3] — is theater for a 150-person company; nobody invokes it. The real pressure comes from procurement. Starting this fall, "How do you comply with Article 50?" shows up on tender forms, and that's where you lose contracts, not to fines.

Two traps First: the grace period doesn't cover everything — the marking obligation for synthetic content from systems already on the market before today slides to 12/02/2026; the chatbot disclosure has no such grace period.[4] Second: high risk isn't due today (Annex III from 12/02/2027, product-embedded AI from 08/02/2028).[2] If someone's pitching you a high-risk compliance project right now, ask for the date.

Y Combinator Gives Away the Machine That Runs Its Accounting [5][6]

On Thursday, Y Combinator announced it was open-sourcing "a multi-agent harness we use internally at YC." The name: QM. The repository's self-description: "A multiplayer agent harness for work. In Slack and on the web."[5] Its scope of use, per the announcement: "across accounting, legal, events, and engineering (including building QM itself!)." MIT-licensed, "cloud-first," with native Slack and web interfaces.[6]

Harness: not the AI model itself, but the rig around it — the part that gives the model tools, distributes tasks, collects results, and decides when to ask a human. Like the difference between an engine and a car: you buy the engine, you have to build the car. QM is the car.

What's interesting isn't the code, it's what YC is saying about itself through it: accounting, legal, and event planning there don't run with agent support, they run through agents. The jump from roughly 2,000 stars on Friday to 5,300 this morning[5] shows how many people were looking for exactly this template: a back-office operating system you don't rent.

The part to get stuck on is the security section: "the agent acts as the person it's working for, with their credentials and permissions, and everything it does is audited."[5] Honest, because it doesn't define the permissions problem away. Uncomfortable, because the ERP log ends up showing a human's ID, even though a machine performed the action. For a German company with a works council, that's the first question, not a footnote.

At least QM builds the answer in itself. It has three postures: Strict — "every harness tool call pauses for human approval, except the two no-effect turn enders"; Auto (the default) — a classifier screens incoming external data and tool outputs before they reach the model; Dangerous — "no content screening, no pauses between tool calls."[5] Anyone putting QM into production should run the first weeks in Strict, uncomfortable as that is. The default is Auto, and defaults, as we know, win.

Territory line QM's model and tooling side — that Pi, OpenCode, Codex, or Claude Code can all drive the same core — is a dev-tool topic and belongs in the ai-dev brief. What counts here is the use case: a company that runs its back office through agents and gives away the blueprint for it.

88 Percent Use AI. Six Percent Profit From It. [7][8][9]

The Stanford AI Index 2026 reports "Organizational adoption reached 88%" — organizations with AI in at least one business function.[7] The same 88% shows up in McKinsey's "The State of AI." Two sources, one number: that looks like confirmation, but it's probably the same survey cited twice — check before putting it on a slide as a cross-check.

It gets interesting further down the funnel. By the same McKinsey figures, 72% use generative AI (2024: 33%), only 39% report any EBIT impact at all, and roughly 6% count as "AI high performers" with over 5% of their EBIT from AI. Fewer than 20% track defined GenAI metrics.[8]

Brazil's Banks: Priority Rank Two, 1.8% of the Budget [10][11]

Febraban's 34th technology survey (with Deloitte, published June 2026) forecasts a 2026 IT budget of R$50.4 billion, "novo avanço de 8%."[10] AI and generative AI rank high or medium priority at 84% of banks — tied for second place with cloud, behind cybersecurity at 100%.[10]

Now the cross-check no press release runs: AI investment totaled R$826 million in 2025, up from R$596 million in 2024 — plus 39%.[10] Backing the 8% growth out of the total budget puts 2025 at roughly R$46.7 billion. That makes the AI line roughly 1.8% of the technology budget (my own calculation from the two published figures; Febraban may define "AI" more narrowly than the AI share actually embedded in cloud and software line items).

This doesn't debunk the hype, it sorts it: priority rank two means just under two percent of the bank budget. Fittingly, more than half the banks (56%) are still in "testes e construção de casos de uso" ["testing and building use cases"].[11] Anyone selling Brazil's financial sector as an AI pioneer is confusing intent with spending. For twiceD, the gap is the story: "pilot to production" is the bigger market than "one more pilot."

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Back-Office Agent on Your Own Infrastructure

ELI5 & value: Since Friday, the rig a company uses to run its back office through agents has been free and open source.[5] That shifts the question for a mid-sized company from "which agent platform do we buy" to "who wires this up for us with DATEV, our ERP, and the inbox — in-house." Concrete value: one recurring workflow with volume, say incoming invoice → coding suggestion → human approval. What gets saved isn't "AI," it's the retyping between two systems that nobody enjoys and everybody gets wrong.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick exactly one workflow that has volume and a clear "done" state — no customer communication, no payment without a human. (2) Set up QM in the customer's environment, Slack or the bundled web interface as the front end — no custom UI gets built. (3) Run the first weeks in the Strict posture: every tool call pauses for human approval.[5] Only move individual tools to Auto once the log is clean. (4) Build two adapters: reading from inbox/DMS, writing as a draft into the ERP. (5) Article 50 notice everywhere a human sees the agent's output.[1] Deliberately out of scope: anything that posts without a human, and anything with customer contact.
Market & gap: the platform side is taken — Microsoft Copilot Studio and Salesforce Agentforce as suites, n8n and the harness class below them. The decisive difference is in Microsoft's own documentation: billing runs on "Copilot Credits," capacity is enforced monthly, "unused Copilot Credit don't carry over to the next month," and going over means "technical enforcement applies and can result in service denial."[13] That's a rented conveyor belt with a monthly quota — plus your data taking a trip to someone else's cloud. QM is MIT-licensed and model-agnostic; the bill then becomes model and operating costs, not a per-message license. Honest uncertainty: YC calls QM "cloud-first"[6] — whether that means "runs in your cloud" or "runs in ours" is the first question before any proposal, not the last. The gap is the buying trigger: only 53% of executives have an established AI strategy at all[12] — the rest don't need someone to sell them a platform, they need someone to finish a workflow.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium — and that's deliberately not "high." Unlike a one-off sign-off, this is a running system: models cost money per transaction, adapters break with the next ERP update, and someone has to read the log. V0 realistically 4–6 weeks solo, then one to two hours a week per customer plus one maintenance window a month. Sustainable as long as it stays at one workflow per customer; commit to three workflows at once and you've built yourself an on-call rotation. Sovereignty angle as a sales pitch: an open model on European infrastructure, data never leaves the building — in Germany that's more often the buying argument than price. github.com/yc-software/qm · learn.microsoft.com · okta.com

2 · An Action Ledger for Agents That Act as Humans

ELI5 & value: QM says it openly: "the agent acts as the person it's working for, with their credentials and permissions."[5] In practice that means: the system's log shows a human's ID, but the machine did the acting. As long as everything goes right, that's convenient. At the first mistake, it's the only question that matters — in front of the works council, the auditor, the customer. The product is a narrow, tamper-proof action ledger that does exactly one thing: separate what a human decided from what an agent carried out under that human's rights. Value: an incident that turns into a fundamental debate about AI without a record stays a routine matter with one.
Prototype (V0): (1) A single target system, a single agent — not a company-wide initiative. (2) Record at the harness's tool boundary: the acting agent, on whose behalf, which tool, hash of the inputs, output, posture (Strict/Auto), approved yes/no — written append-only and immutable. (3) Two reports instead of a dashboard: the Article 50 view (was the output labeled wherever a human saw it)[1] and the co-determination view (which decisions a human actually made). (4) A monthly, signed PDF extract — the artifact you bring into a meeting. Deliberately out of scope: real-time blocking (that's the harness's job), SIEM replacement, identity management.
Market & gap: the identity side is occupied and well funded: Okta announced "Okta for AI Agents" with general availability as of 04/30/2026, CyberArk plays the same field from the privileges angle.[12] But these products answer the question that comes before — is the agent allowed to do this? They don't answer the question that comes after, in plain terms, in front of a board: who decided what, and where did the machine just execute? The buying trigger is documented in Okta's own survey (Apprize360, 292 executives and 492 knowledge workers in seven countries, 05/27/2026): 53% with an established AI strategy today, versus a year earlier when 91% of organizations already had agents in use against just 10% with a mature strategy for non-human identities.[12] Source honesty: vendor-commissioned research from a company selling the solution to the measured problem — the direction is credible, the magnitude should be taken with caution. The gap is the German fit: works-council agreement, accountability, Article 50 — Okta builds none of that.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium to high. An append-only log plus two reports is a small, durably ownable product — no team needed, as long as you resist the temptation to turn it into a SIEM. V0 3–4 weeks solo, low maintenance because the interface is narrow. Honest risk: the market isn't there yet. This is only sellable where an agent already runs in production — a handful of German mid-sized companies today. It's a 2027 product that you build in 2026; keep it correspondingly small and bundle it with idea 1 instead of marketing it on its own. github.com/yc-software/qm · okta.com · artificialintelligenceact.eu

It's Sunday, the second of August. Today is the day the European Union's AI Act has been building toward for two years. The penalty for getting this wrong tops out at fifteen million euros or three percent of worldwide annual revenue, whichever is higher. My view on that hasn't changed: nobody's going after a mid-sized company with that fine.
