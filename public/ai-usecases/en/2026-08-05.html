Scout: the assistant nobody has to ask [1][2]

Microsoft unveiled Scout at Build on 2026-06-02 — the difference from everything that used to be called "Copilot" comes down to one word: always-on. TechCrunch describes it as a permanently running agentic assistant with a fixed identity: you name your own instance, give it ongoing feedback, and it takes over recurring tasks — calendar upkeep and meeting agendas come built in, with custom "skills" addable on top. It's built on the OpenClaw framework, with a "policy conformance system" that continuously checks whether the agent stays within the set rules. Access: the Frontier early-access program — plus a GitHub Copilot subscription.[1]

Always-on agent: A chat assistant is a reference book — you ask, it answers, then it's gone. An always-on agent is a coworker with a desk in the next room: it constantly sees what comes in and acts on its own. The difference isn't intelligence, it's permission: one reads when you call it. The other reads all the time.

Why is Microsoft building this? The most honest answer is a number. Of roughly 450 million Microsoft 365 seats, a reported 4.5% pay for Copilot features at all; the paying base grew from 15 million in January to 20 million in April 2026, while paying ChatGPT users number over 50 million.[2] Update 2026-07-29 Microsoft's own quarterly earnings release now shows a higher number: "Microsoft 365 Copilot reached over 30 million paid seats" — roughly 6.7% on the same reported 450-million-seat base.[2] Still, the logic stays the same: an assistant you have to invoke simply doesn't get invoked. An agent that's running anyway is harder to ignore — and easier to sell as its own tier. That's exactly where Microsoft is headed, and as of 2026-07-29 it's no longer just a report: Satya Nadella himself announced the merger on the FY26 Q4 earnings call — chat, Cowork, Autopilots, and code are coming "in one super app," spanning consumer and enterprise use, still "this quarter," meaning by the end of September.[2] All that's left unconfirmed is the fine print: the exact date, pricing, and whether the Autopilots become their own paid tier — for that, only the reported memo ("slated for August") still stands. The direction is confirmed; the price tag isn't.

My take: the news isn't the product, it's the business model underneath it. A single-digit pay rate after two years of relentless promotion — 4.5% in April, most recently around 6.7% by Microsoft's own count — is a harsh verdict on the assistant approach. Still, fairness demands a caveat: going from 20 to over 30 million seats in a single quarter is steep growth, and anyone still citing "4.5%" today is citing a stale snapshot. That doesn't change the level, though — and the problem isn't the technology, it's the moment of use. The answer to that isn't "a better model," it's "fewer prompts needed." Which means that for anyone rolling this out, the decisive question shifts from "what can it do?" to "what is it allowed to see without anyone triggering it?" — and that's exactly where it gets uncomfortable.

The emergency brake gets decommissioned [3]

Anyone who's rolled out Copilot in an M365 tenant over the past two years knows this problem isn't theoretical: the assistant answers with content from SharePoint stores half the workforce never knew existed — because nobody ever cleaned up the permissions there. Microsoft's answer was called Restricted SharePoint Search: an allow list of up to 100 sites, outside of which enterprise-wide search — and with it, Copilot — doesn't reach. Its own documentation (as of 2026-07-06) now reads: "Restricted SharePoint Search is retiring. Starting July 31, 2026, new enablement is blocked." The successors are meant to be Restricted Content Discovery, SharePoint Advanced Management, and Microsoft Purview.[3]

Three sentences from the same page matter more than the retirement announcement itself. First: the allow list "isn't a security boundary and doesn't change any permissions" — it was never a security boundary, only a privacy screen. Second, a sentence I've never seen in any rollout presentation: "If a user recently accessed a site, or the site was shared with that user in Teams or Outlook, the site appears in the user's results and responses, even if it's not on the allow list." Third, the reasoning for shutting it down: 100 sites is "not sustainable as your organization scales Copilot and agentic operations."[3]

Agent gets permanent access→allow list disappears→only real permissions still limit it

My take: this isn't a bad decision, it's an honest one. Restricted Search was a curtain in front of an open window — it blocked the view, not the window. The uncomfortable consequence remains: from now on, the organization's permission structure is the only limit on what an agent may say. That's not a switch, it's a project — weeks of cleanup work in stores that grew organically over years. And it lands exactly at the moment the agent shifts from being asked to running constantly. Whoever plans for both together has a rollout. Whoever just buys licenses has an incident.

Replit puts the agent to work on marketing [4]

On 2026-06-03, Replit released the SEO Agent — the date comes from the vendor's own blog byline, since secondary sources cite conflicting dates.[4] It checks how crawlable and indexable a published app's pages are, inspects the semantic structure, audits meta and Open Graph tags, and verifies that sitemap.xml and robots.txt are present and correct — then applies approved fixes with a click. It lives in the new Growth dashboard, where you track traffic for your published apps.[4]

AI search visibility: Your website is a shop, and Google was the street in front of it for years — anyone who walked by saw your storefront. Now there's a concierge standing in front (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google's AI answer box) who tells the customer the recommendation straight into their ear, without them ever passing your window. "AI search visibility" means: making sure the concierge knows you and cites you correctly. Technically, it's surprisingly unglamorous — clean heading structure, machine-readable markup of the facts (JSON-LD), a reachable sitemap, and a robots.txt that doesn't lock out the assistants' crawlers.

My take: the checklist isn't the news — it's been around for fifteen years. The news is the ownership: here, an agent takes over a business function (discoverability, i.e., marketing), not a developer task. That's the direction applied agents are heading — away from "write me some code," toward "keep this metric in good shape." The underestimated side effect: anyone building their app on a platform will soon get basic marketing hygiene built in — and the agency that sold exactly that package as an entry-level offer loses its entry-level offer. Anyone who wants to turn this into a consulting product needs to hurry; see the second twiceD idea below, expiry date included. Two independent surveys, two methods, practically the same value — rare enough to make the level credible.

The trajectory isn't. Bitkom dates the doubling to 2025 → 2026 (17% → 41%), the eco figure to +118% since 2024 (implying roughly 18% in 2024). What comes before the rollout in Germany [7][8]

Since Sunday, 2026-08-02, the EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency obligation applies: anyone interacting with an AI has to be told, and generated content has to be labeled. By the same deadline, every member state had to have set up at least one AI regulatory sandbox; for systems already on the market before that date, the grace period for machine-readable labeling runs until 2026-12-02.[7] Yesterday's brief has the postponed high-risk calendar in detail — here it's enough to note: it doesn't affect the transparency obligations.

For a German company, there's a second layer that no product announcement mentions. § 87(1) no. 6 of the BetrVG gives the works council a co-determination right over the "Einführung und Anwendung von technischen Einrichtungen, die dazu bestimmt sind, das Verhalten oder die Leistung der Arbeitnehmer zu überwachen".[8] An agent that permanently reads inboxes, Teams channels, and file stores is technically exactly such a system.

Context My reading as a developer, not legal advice; the case-by-case assessment belongs to an employment-law attorney. In practice it still means something very concrete: the rollout date doesn't hinge on the license agreement, it hinges on a works agreement — and nobody drafts one of those in a week.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and an honest solo-feasibility read

1 · Agent clearance: permission hygiene before the rollout

ELI5 & value: An AI agent inside a company doesn't invent new access rights — it uses the ones that are already there, just faster and without hesitation. Something nobody found in ten years, because it sat in a forgotten SharePoint store, gets found by the agent in four seconds and read out loud. The value for the customer isn't a saved click, it's an avoided incident: the payroll list showing up in the chatbot is a data-protection and works-council event, not an IT ticket. On top of that, a second, very concrete value — a deadline. Without this groundwork, the works council delays the rollout,[8] and licenses already purchased sit idle for a quarter.
Prototype (V0): (1) Permission inventory using the tenant's own built-in tools rather than interviews: who has access to which site, which shares are set to "everyone in the organization," which stores are orphaned. (2) Blast-radius report: what can the agent surface, for which role — backed by five sample queries demonstrated live in the workshop. That's the moment that sells the project. (3) Remediation list, prioritized by damage potential times effort, split into "before the rollout" and "after." (4) Clearance package: scope description, technical configuration, a draft basis for the works agreement, user notice under Article 50.[7] Deliberately out of scope: ongoing operations, monitoring, DLP rule sets, the final legal document.
Market & gap: Microsoft sells the tools itself and names them in the very documentation that announces the emergency brake's retirement: SharePoint Advanced Management and Microsoft Purview, plus Restricted Content Discovery as the allow list's successor.[3] On the security side, there's Varonis, which lists Microsoft Copilot as a covered integration, describes a "SearchLeak" threat inside M365 Copilot, and advertises "Secure AI adoption in 10 days" plus a free risk assessment.[9] Those are products: they surface the problem and hand you dials. What they don't do — actually clean up the 200 accumulated stores of an 80-person company, sit at the table with the works council, and explain in plain German why the allow list is no longer an option. The gap isn't the tool, it's the execution — and the timing is favorable, because the convenient stopgap solution closed on 2026-07-31[3] and over half of German users themselves say the know-how is missing in-house.[5]
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. Consulting-shaped — inventory, report, clearance, done; no infrastructure, no on-call duty, no ongoing model costs. V0 in two to three weeks solo, because the work lives once in the queries, the grid, and the templates; after that, four to six days per client, scaling via one contractor per parallel project. Two honest limitations: the final version of the works agreement belongs to an employment-law attorney — twiceD delivers the inventory, the configuration, and a draft basis, and that partnership needs to be in place before the first sale. And tenant access is a matter of trust: the first customer is hard to land without a reference, the second comes almost on its own. learn.microsoft.com · varonis.com · gesetze-im-internet.de

2 · AI visibility check for SME websites

ELI5 & value: More and more purchase decisions no longer start on a results page, but inside an assistant: "Which supplier in Ostwestfalen makes small batches of X?" The check answers two questions — do you even get mentioned in answers like that, and if so, with the right details? The value lies in prevention: an outdated fact (an old price list, a discontinued product, a wrong certification) that an assistant keeps repeating for months is a silent revenue loss — and nobody sees it in the analytics dashboard, because the customer never visited the website.
Prototype (V0): (1) Collect 30 to 50 real purchase-decision questions with the customer — pulled from day-to-day sales, not from a keyword tool. (2) Run those questions against several assistants in an automated way, log the answers and cited sources, tally mentions and accuracy. (3) Technical side: check JSON-LD, semantic structure, sitemap.xml, and robots.txt against the assistants' crawlers — exactly the checklist Replit has just built into its platform.[4] (4) Deliverable: visibility report, correction list, a re-measurement after eight weeks using the same catalog. Deliberately out of scope: a monitoring subscription, content production, link building.
Market & gap: The market exists and is well funded. Profound advertises "Optimize Your Brand's Visibility in AI Search," covers Perplexity, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, Microsoft Copilot, DeepSeek, and Google's AI Overviews, and targets "AEO Teams," content and PR teams, and agencies explicitly; no prices are on the page, the path runs through "Get a Demo."[10] A dashboard with a sales call gatekeeping it, built for companies that already have a marketing team. The 60-person machine builder with an organically grown TYPO3 site has no AEO team; it doesn't need a subscription, it needs someone who measures once, fixes it, and re-measures after eight weeks. That exact size of offering is missing.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium — and I'll say explicitly why not "high." Building it is easy: the question catalog comes from the customer, the querying is a script, the technical checklist is craft. Maintenance is low, because it's a project with a handover. But its shelf life is limited: the technical half is exactly what platforms are now building in by default,[4] and funded dashboards are pushing down from above.[10] I'd estimate the window for this as a standalone offering at twelve to eighteen months; after that, only the part no platform takes over still carries weight — the question catalog from real day-to-day sales and the interpretation. As a door-opener, it works right away; as a load-bearing pillar, it doesn't. Anyone who sells it as the latter is selling an expiration date. tryprofound.com · replit.com

A completely different signal came from Replit today. On June third, Replit released an SEO agent. For Germany, there's a new number and a reason for caution. Bitkom Research reports that forty-one percent of companies with twenty or more employees now use artificial intelligence, up from seventeen percent the year before, based on phone interviews with six hundred four companies. Yesterday this spot carried forty percent from a different survey. Two independent studies, practically the same value: that makes the level credible. The trajectory isn't, because both studies place the same doubling in different years. What's more interesting anyway are the numbers behind it. From the same study, verbatim in Bitkom's own release: thirty-three percent say artificial intelligence led to significantly higher costs, while seventy-seven percent simultaneously report an improved competitive position. A different, roughly six-month-older Bitkom survey from September fifteenth, twenty twenty-five, supplies the number that tells me the most: fifty-three percent name a lack of technical know-how as a hurdle, tied with legal uncertainty.

There's an observation that matters to me more than any single study. McKinsey reports eighty-eight percent regular use in at least one business function, and twenty-three percent already scaling an agentic system. That eighty-eight percent already showed up in this brief yesterday, under Stanford's byline. I couldn't find any indication on Stanford's page of which survey it's actually based on. Two reports, one identical number, no demonstrable independence. As of today I'm treating that as one number with two bylines, not two confirmations.

One German point to close, something no product announcement mentions. German law gives the works council a say over technical systems designed to monitor employee behavior or performance. An agent that permanently reads mailboxes, channels, and file stores is technically exactly that. That's my reading as a developer, not legal advice, but the practical consequence is clear: the rollout date doesn't hinge on the license agreement, it hinges on a works agreement.
