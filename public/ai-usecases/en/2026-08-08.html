Jurisdiction Is Decided — and Split in Two [1][2][3]

The AI Office has been able to enforce since 08/02 — that was the story here. But the practically more important question for a German mid-sized company is never Brussels, it's: who comes knocking? That's been answered since 2026-07-29 — and split between two agencies.

The Federal Ministry for Digital Affairs announced the rollout: the AI Market Surveillance and Innovation Promotion Act (KI-MIG) is in force, the Bundesnetzagentur becomes the national coordination point for implementing the AI Regulation, complete with an offering of „AI regulatory sandboxes for testing new applications".[1] The agency itself describes the most consequential part of its mandate this way: „The market-surveillance authority accepts complaints from individuals and legal entities, in particular consumers, and reviews them."[3]

Market-surveillance authority: the body that checks whether a product already on the market follows the rules — and pulls it if it doesn't. Like a food inspector: they don't bake bread and don't issue baking licenses, they show up at the bakery when someone complains. The only thing new here is that the product is software this time.

A sharply defined special rule applies to the financial sector. BaFin states: „The agency takes over market surveillance for AI systems directly tied to regulated financial activity." Covered are „credit institutions, insurers, and other financial companies under BaFin supervision".[2] What gets reviewed are prohibited practices and transparency duties — in practice, that means customer-facing chatbots first. The high-risk part, covering creditworthiness checks and insurance risk assessment, only kicks in „as of December 2, 2027".[2]

The path from annoyance to a letter from the authority is short:

Complaint
customer / competitor→BNetzA or BaFin
depending on sector→Review
documents, system→Measure
up to a fine

My take: the most consequential regulatory news of the past two weeks — it slipped by almost unnoticed on a Wednesday because everyone was staring at 08/02. The difference is concrete: a European enforcement power is an announcement, a German complaints office is an address. For suppliers, that means: anyone selling software to a bank now has a customer whose procurement team will demand assurances nobody had to spell out before. Not a threat, a sales opening — just one with homework attached.

Three Dates That Don't Contradict Each Other [2][3][4][5]

Three deadlines are swirling around this week, pitted against each other. They measure different things: two say when an authority starts working, one says when a company has to deliver.

DateWhat appliesWho it hits
2026-08-02Transparency duties (Art. 50); market surveillance Annex IIIevery operator[4]
2027-12-02High-risk system requirementsprovider + operator[2][5]
2028-08-02Market surveillance Annex I (shifted from 2027-08-02)authority[3]

Only the first row triggers work today: the labeling duty for AI interaction and synthetic content has applied since 08/02; violations cost up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher.[5] The heavy high-risk layer — biometrics, employment, education, essential services, migration — was pushed to 2027-12-02 via the "Digital Omnibus."[5] That same Omnibus — in the EU Official Journal since 2026-07-24 — also pushed the second authority deadline back a year: market surveillance for AI in regulated products (Annex I — machinery, elevators, medical devices) now begins not on 2027-08-02 but only on 2028-08-02; the Bundesnetzagentur page still carried the old date at the time of access.[3] Per the Commission, more than 180 organizations have signed the voluntary code of practice on labeling AI-generated content.[4]

My take: the shift to December 2027 is being read as an all-clear — the most expensive misreading in this dossier. What got postponed was the duty, not the build time: anyone who documents a credit-scoring model only in 2027 is documenting a system whose data lineage nobody can reconstruct anymore. And the 180 signatories aren't a success story, they're the circle of companies that already have a legal department.

FedEx Flips the Switch to Production Scale — and Names No Number [6]

On 2026-07-30, FedEx and robot maker Dexterity announced they're expanding autonomous trailer loading at the Hagerstown hub in Maryland. The key sentence: the site „expands the program beyond the pilot site previously used to validate the technology" and „enables production at a significantly larger operational scale".[6] The system deployed is Dexterity Mech, two-armed, driven by a world model called Foresight.[6]

World model (robotics): a model that doesn't predict text, but what happens physically next — will the box tip if I grab it here? Like a person lifting a crate who already knows the bottle inside will roll. The difference from classic robot programming: there, every movement is written into the script; here, the model estimates the consequences of a movement it has never actually made before.

On scale, the announcement gives only one network-wide figure: FedEx loads „tens of thousands of trailers" daily in the US. Dexterity founder Samir Menon calls Hagerstown „an important milestone … creating a blueprint for network expansion".[6]

What's missing No robot count, no throughput, no error or damage rate, no shift-operation availability, no before-and-after. Even the pilot site stays unnamed. As an event, the announcement holds up — two companies, one site, one date — as proof of impact, it's worthless.

My take: still the most important deployment story of the week. Physical AI has a credibility problem language models don't have: a video proves nothing, and the industry has lived off videos for years. A corporation that pushes a second site into production scale has done the math internally — otherwise it doesn't happen. That it doesn't show the math is normal, and exactly the point where a buyer needs to wake up: the contract belongs over the metric — trailers per shift, damage rate, downtime, measured against your own baseline instead of the vendor's slide deck. The second lesson sits in Menon's word blueprint: what's expensive is repeatability across sites, not the robot. That's where the work sits that a small shop can sell — idea 1 below.

Verbatim: expansion „beyond the pilot site previously used to validate the technology", „enables production at a significantly larger operational scale"; technology: Dexterity Mech (two-armed system) with world model Foresight; quote from Samir Menon, PhD, founder and CEO of Dexterity: „Hagerstown represents an important milestone in demonstrating how physical AI can operate reliably in a high-volume logistics environment"; quote from Kawal Preet, EVP Planning, Engineering and Transformation at FedEx: „This expanded collaboration with Dexterity reflects our commitment to advancing capabilities that improve how we operate today"; network context „tens of thousands of trailers loaded daily across the FedEx network in the U.S." The announcement names neither unit counts nor performance data; that absence was checked at retrieval, not overlooked.

Brazil: the Budget Is Set, Sector Oversight Isn't [7]

Germany's financial sector has had a named AI regulator for ten days now. Brazil's has money. The Febraban survey with Deloitte puts banks' 2026 tech budget at R$50.4 billion (+8%), with the AI line item for 2025 at R$826 million (+39%).[7] Those figures ran here on 08/02 and 08/04, along with our own math that AI accounts for roughly 1.8% of the tech budget — today they're a point of contrast, not news.

What's new is a date: the outstanding part of the survey is set to be presented at Febraban Tech, 2026-08-24 through 26.[7] On the oversight side, the only thing running so far is data-protection authority ANPD's regulatory sandbox with three companies (covered here on 08/07); I'm not aware of a sector-specific AI market surveillance body along BaFin lines for Brazil.

My take: in Germany the oversight question is settled and the investment question is open; in Brazil it's the reverse. For a pitch, that means two different opening lines: German bank procurement wants to know who answers when there's a complaint. Brazilian procurement wants to know what the thing saves in year one.

Old, but Still Circulating [8][9][10]

Three adoption figures are circulating this week as if they were new. They're not — here's the timeline:

  • Bitkom, 2026-03-11: AI use at companies with 20+ employees, 41% up from 17%; 33% say AI turned out more expensive than expected, 19% have cut jobs because of it.[8]
  • KI-Index Mittelstand (DMB × Salesforce): 51.2% use or test AI, up from 33.1%; AI agents 16.6%, up from 8.7%. Fielded November 2025, roughly 700 companies. Last covered here on 07/29–30.[9]
  • Gartner, 2025-06-25: more than 40% of agentic AI projects would be canceled by end of 2027. Fourteen months old, still the industry's most-cited skepticism figure.[10]
Methodology All three are self-reports or forecasts, not measurements. The KI-Index is published jointly with Salesforce — a vendor of exactly the agents whose spread it's measuring; "uses or tests" is also a softer bar than Bitkom's "uses." The Gartner figure is a prediction over a period still in progress: today it's neither confirmed nor disproven.

My take: the pattern weighs more than any of the three numbers. In a week when two authorities take up their mandates, if the most-shared "AI news" dates back to March and last June, what's missing is fresh evidence — not attention. Practically: if you put one of these numbers in a proposal, put the survey date next to it.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Event Bridge for the One Robot Cell

ELI5 & value: a mid-sized logistics company buys one robot cell. The robot knows exactly what it's doing: grabbed, placed, missed grip, blocked, fault. The warehouse management system next to it learns none of this, because it doesn't speak the manufacturer's language. Six months in, nobody can say whether the thing pays for itself — the numbers exist, but locked in a box nobody reads. The event bridge taps the robot's events, merges them with the orders, and turns that into two things: a shift report (operations per hour, downtime minutes, recovery time) and a data stream the inventory system can digest. Value: exactly the number the vendor doesn't supply — see the FedEx story above.[6]
Prototype (V0): (1) One customer, one cell, one event stream — tapped where it already occurs (OPC UA for machine controllers, MQTT or REST for newer cells). (2) Normalize events to a dozen fields and write them to a time-series database; off-the-shelf building blocks instead of custom code: Telegraf as collector, TimescaleDB as storage, Grafana as display. (3) Order context via the inventory system's existing interface — read-only. (4) Shift report as a PDF, in the plant manager's inbox each morning. Deliberately out of scope: any control over the robot. The bridge writes nothing back into the system — that keeps the project out of machine-safety and CE-assessment territory, and that's exactly what decides whether a small shop is allowed to touch it.
Market & gap: the orchestration layer exists — at the top of the market. Blue Yonder markets a Robotics Hub that translates tasks into manufacturer-specific commands and feeds performance data back into the warehouse management system; Manhattan Associates sells the same capability in Active WMS.[11] Both are suites: you get robot integration by switching your warehouse management system — not an option for a 40-employee operation with one cell. On market size, a warning instead of a number: for warehouse robotics in 2026, three market researchers cite US$7.35, US$8.75, and US$10.96 billion — same year, roughly a 50% spread.[12] What holds up is only this: the robot maker sells cells, the suite vendor sells platforms — nobody has the wiring between them on their price list.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High to medium. V0 in two to three weeks, because all the building blocks are ready-made; the time goes into the controller's protocol quirks. Low maintenance as long as the bridge only reads — it runs on an industrial PC at the customer's site rather than as a hosted service, which eases both data protection and availability at once. Scaling via an external developer per site. Honest catch: on-site access is mandatory, a site outside Brazil means travel or a partner — that caps the customer count harder than the code does. info.blueyonder.com · manh.com · newsroom.fedex.com

2 · Feature Screening for Credit and Pricing Models

ELI5 & value: as of now, BaFin reviews AI systems tied to regulated financial activity, and explicitly names creditworthiness checks and insurance risk assessment as a high-risk field.[2] What gets examined isn't the systems as a whole, but the features a scoring model builds its decision from — the column list. That's where the risk sits: a feature that indirectly encodes health or origin; one from a source with no legal basis; one nobody can explain anymore because the colleague who built it left in 2023. The product reads the feature catalog along with its provenance data and flags matches against a rule catalog: proxy features, features without a documented source, mandatory fields for 2027-12-02. Value: the customer finds the three indefensible columns today — while swapping one out still costs a model retrain, not a regulatory response.
Prototype (V0): (1) Input is whatever already exists: feature definitions from the feature store or model documentation (dbt models, Feast definitions, or the warehouse table as a fallback). (2) A versioned rule catalog in YAML — proxy patterns, mandatory provenance fields, prohibited-practice criteria from Article 5. (3) Rule checking plus a language model only for the fuzzy question ("could this feature be a proxy for X?"), output always with a rationale and rule reference, never as a verdict. (4) Findings list with a traffic light plus a review log as a PDF. Deliberately out of scope: bias metrics and ongoing model monitoring — that's model-risk operations, a different product. Runs as a command-line tool inside the customer's network; no dataset leaves the building.
Market & gap: a dense German consulting field has grown up around the AI Act — Proliance, Compound Law, Groenewold-it, and a dozen TÜV-certified independent consultants offering readiness assessments, risk classification, and technical documentation.[13] Their output is a document about systems; nobody touches the feature layer, because that's data engineering, not legal advice. From the other direction come governance platforms built for large companies with their own validation teams. The gap sits technically in between: a small, locally run tool for a shop with five models that checks the column list instead of the system list.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium — the constraint is sales, not the build. V0 in three to four weeks, maintenance one to two days a quarter for the rule catalog. Selling into a supervised institution, though, takes months and needs a first project as a reference; without a credit-risk expert as a sparring partner (external, on a per-day basis), the rule catalog won't be any good. A more realistic entry point is therefore not the bank but its supplier: a scoring-software vendor with ten bank clients. Sovereignty variant: fully offline, an open model on the customer's own hardware — for feature catalogs, that's table stakes for the conversation, not an add-on. bafin.de · proliance.ai · compound.law

It's Saturday, August eighth. Six days after European enforcement got underway, the story catching up with it matters more for a German mid-sized company: Germany has decided who's actually in charge.

Since July twenty-ninth, the AI Market Surveillance and Innovation Promotion Act — KI-MIG for short — has been in force. The Federal Ministry for Digital Affairs put out the announcement: the Bundesnetzagentur becomes the national coordination point for enforcing the European AI Regulation. On its own website, the agency states the sentence that matters day to day: the market-surveillance authority takes complaints from individuals and companies and reviews them. So there's no longer just a rule — there's now an address.

Banks and insurers fall under a separate authority. BaFin takes over market surveillance for AI systems tied directly to regulated financial activity. What gets checked first are prohibited practices and transparency duties — in practice, the customer-facing chatbots. The heavy part — creditworthiness checks and insurance risk assessment — doesn't kick in until December second, twenty twenty-seven.

That sorts out three dates that got tangled together this week. August second, twenty twenty-six, brings transparency duties for every operator. December second, twenty twenty-seven, is the real deadline for companies. And August second, twenty twenty-eight, only marks when the authority itself starts work on another annex — AI embedded in regulated products — a date the Digital Omnibus pushed back a year from the original August second, twenty twenty-seven. My warning here: what got postponed is the review, not the build time. Anyone who documents their scoring model only right before that date is documenting a system whose data lineage nobody can reconstruct anymore.

The second story comes out of logistics. On July thirtieth, FedEx announced it's taking autonomous trailer loading, built with robot maker Dexterity, beyond the pilot site and into production scale at the Hagerstown hub in Maryland. The system is a two-armed robot, driven by what's called a world model — a model that estimates what happens physically next, instead of having every movement written into a script.

And here's the annoying part: the announcement doesn't contain a single performance number. No robot count, no throughput, no error rate, no before-and-after. As a news event, it holds up. As proof of impact, it's worthless. My take: no corporation pushes a second site into production scale without having run the numbers internally. That it doesn't show them is normal — and exactly where a buyer should close the contract on the metric, not on the robot.

Two project ideas to close. First, an event bridge for shops with exactly one robot cell: it reads the machine's events, merges them with the orders, and delivers the shift numbers no manufacturer supplies — deliberately read-only, so the project doesn't drift into machine-safety territory.
