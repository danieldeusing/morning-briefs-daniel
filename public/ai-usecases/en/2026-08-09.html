Eight Billion for the Layer Between Machine and Software [1]

Three acquisitions, six weeks, one pattern. The automation conglomerates no longer build their AI layer themselves — they buy it.

Buyer → TargetWhat the Target DoesPrice
Schneider Electric → CogniteIndustrial AI and data company$3.1 billion
Schneider Electric → AiDASHAI for vegetation, asset, and climate risk$350 million (cash)
ABB → RotorkActuators, valve control — hardware$5.5 billion
Industrial data layer: In a factory, machines speak dozens of old dialects — every controller, every sensor, every maintenance log is different. The data layer is the interpreter that turns all of that into a single, searchable inventory. Like an address book where every phone number is finally in the same format: only after that can any AI model actually say anything useful.

What matters is a distinction the umbrella headline "industrial AI deals" blurs: Rotork is not an AI company. It builds actuators. At $5.5 billion, it's the largest of the three deals — which means 61% of the volume in this supposed AI wave is a purchase of metal. The real software bet is Schneider's, at a combined $3.45 billion.

My take: for a mid-sized company running three plants, this means two things. First, the window for the independent industrial AI vendor is closing — anyone choosing a data platform today is indirectly choosing a corporate owner and its price list. Second, none of the deals changes anything about the actual bottleneck: the bundle is sold top-down to large accounts, and fitting it into a plant's messy, grown-up reality stays manual work at the customer's site. That's exactly where a small shop has something to sell — not out of desperation, but because corporate logic structurally can't reach there.

The Last Mile Is Being Sold — Justified With a Number From 2025 [3][4]

Within three days, the two largest cloud providers founded the same business model: people who sit at the customer's site. Amazon committed $1 billion on 06/30/2026, Microsoft $2.5 billion on 07/02/2026 for its own venture called Frontier, with roughly 6,000 engineers and industry specialists embedded directly inside customer organizations.[3]

Forward-deployed engineer: not a consultant who hands over a slide deck, but a developer who sits at the customer's site for months and makes the software fit. Like a kitchen builder who doesn't ship a catalog but shows up with a tape measure and trims the cabinet door because the wall is crooked.

The number that keeps surfacing in the debate over this shift comes from MIT's NANDA project: 95% of generative AI pilot projects show no measurable effect on the bottom line.[4] Microsoft and Amazon don't actually make that argument in their announcements — what's documented is the timing: $3.5 billion for deployment troops, alongside a widely cited number. That's why its age is worth a look.

Methodology The underlying report — „The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" — dates from July 2025 and was widely covered starting 08/19/2025.[4] It's therefore about twelve months old and describes a generation of tools that predates today's agentic systems. I couldn't reach an MIT-hosted version with sample details today; I'm therefore citing the number as widely cited, not as freshly documented. Anyone basing a purchase decision on it is basing it on the year before last.

My take: the finding still holds up regardless — it matches what Schneider and ABB are saying with their billions: the model isn't the problem. The platform vendors are buying the data layer, the cloud vendors are buying the fit-out crew, because both are missing and neither ships as a product. If Microsoft needs 6,000 people for the last mile, the last mile isn't a side issue — it's the product.

The model and tooling side of this week — coding models, agent CLIs, benchmark shifts — lives in the ai-dev brief. Here, only what actually reaches operations counts.

Yellow.ai: $550 Million Valuation, $34 Million Revenue [2]

On 08/03/2026, Yellow.ai announced it's going public on Nasdaq via merger with Bluerock Acquisition Corp. — future ticker YAI. The key figures from its own release: „pre-money valuation of approximately $300 million", a pro forma equity value of around $550 million, plus „16 billion conversations annually, 650+ enterprise clients, and $34M+ unaudited revenue last fiscal year".[2]

SPAC: an already publicly listed shell with no business of its own, filled with investor money. A real company merges with that shell and is on the stock exchange overnight — without the long, audit-heavy path of a regular IPO. Like moving into an already-registered apartment instead of building your own: faster, but you inherit the floor plan and every question that comes with it.

Three calculations from their own numbers that the release doesn't run:

  • $52,000 in revenue per customer per year ($34 million / 650). That's not an enterprise license, that's a mid-market contract.
  • 0.21 US cents per "conversation" ($34 million / 16 billion). At that price, a "conversation" can't be a resolved support case — more like a single message.
  • Roughly 16x revenue as pro forma valuation, 8.8x pre-money — on an unaudited figure.

My take: the 16 billion conversations are the most interesting number in the release — not as proof of scale, but because they show how cheap the counted unit is. An arbitrarily fine-grained counter measures everything except value. The practical lesson isn't a stock-market lesson: a global agent vendor with 650 customers lands, on average, at a contract value a two-person shop in Germany can also reach. Platform scale doesn't translate into pricing power here.

Brazil: The 2024 Training Target Has Apparently Been Exceeded [5][6]

On 08/05 and 08/06/2026, SECOP, the 53rd national seminar for IT in public administration, met in Brasília under the title „Estado Inteligente: Governança de Dados e IA como Infraestrutura Estratégica de Governo" — intelligent state, data and AI governance as strategic government infrastructure.[5] That's the stage. The calendar behind it is more interesting.

Brazil's AI plan PBIA 2024–2028 names two figures for the administrative portion: „investimento de R$ 1,76 bilhão para aprimorar os serviços públicos por meio do uso de inteligência artificial" and, as a separate line item, „capacitação de 115 mil servidores públicos até 2026, com um investimento de cerca de R$ 7,5 milhões".[6]

Date trap Both figures are currently circulating as if they were fresh news — but they come from an article dated 08/22/2024. Nor do they contradict each other: R$1.76 billion is the overall program for better public services, R$7.5 million is exclusively the training line item. Neither is a result — both are announcements, and the target year is now. The same trap lurks in the implementation status: the most-cited interim tally isn't the newest one (see below).

My take: "115,000 civil servants by 2026" was a two-year target in 2024; today it's a deadline with just under five months left. The interim tally that's easiest to find dates from October 2025: back then the MGI had trained 28,000 of the 115,000 civil servants — just over 24% of the target after 14 of the 28 months of runtime.[6] Stopping there tells the story backward. Because the same outlet reported a new figure on 07/02/2026, five weeks before this brief: „Pelo menos 167 mil servidores já passaram por alguma capacitação dos programas desenvolvidos pelo ministério" — at least 167,000 civil servants have by now gone through some training from the ministry, says Rogério Mascarenhas, Secretário de Governo Digital at the MGI.[6] That's above the 115,000, five months before the deadline.

Two caveats belong here, and I won't paper over them. First, the July report never mentions the 115,000 target at all — it places the figure next to the newly published „Matriz de Competências em IA", not next to the deadline. No ministry sentence says "target met." Second, the counting basis is noticeably broader: the 167,000 are people with „alguma capacitação" — some course from eleven programs at EV.G/ENAP and Serpro, whose topics cover not just generative AI but also LGPD data protection, process automation, data science, and data governance.[6] The pace gives it away: going from 28,000 (Oct. 2025) to 167,000 (July 2026) is 139,000 in a bit over eight months, roughly 17,000 per month versus roughly 2,000 per month before — about eightfold (my own math from the two documented figures). A jump like that is more plausibly a broader counting method than an eightfold training output.

My conclusion is therefore split in two: by the raw number, the target is exceeded — measured cleanly against it, it isn't, because no public tally puts 115,000 and 167,000 side by side under the same definition. In practice that changes little, since the second number is the more interesting one anyway: R$7.5 million for 115,000 people works out to roughly R$65 per head — the scale of an online course, not of skills-building. So Brazil has briefly trained many civil servants rather than deeply trained a few. Anyone looking to sell government AI there is therefore selling not training, but operation — though they're now meeting counterparts who know the vocabulary.

Nubank Leads — But the Index Measures Disclosure [7]

The Evident AI Index for Latin American banks, published 07/21/2026, puts Nubank in first place and Itaú Unibanco in second. It rated 20 of the region's largest banks across four areas: talent, innovation, leadership, transparency.[7]

Methodology The index „scores banks on 60-plus indicators across these four pillars using only public data".[7] Only public data — that means job postings, patents, publications, executive statements. So the index cleanly measures who shows the most, not whose models actually work in production. A bank that builds a lot and publishes little looks bad here.

From coverage of the index — not from the primary report — comes the figure that more than 5% of Nubank's workforce works in AI-adjacent roles, roughly three times the regional bank average.[7]

My take: the ranking still carries weight despite the caveat — not as a performance measure, but as an intent measure. For the supplier side, the index is an early-warning system for RFPs: whoever talks publicly about AI governance is about to go shopping for proof of it.

54.5 Percent — and What the Number Doesn't Say [8]

The ifo Institute measures for May 2026 that „54.5% of companies in Germany use artificial intelligence in their business processes", up from „40.9%" a year earlier. In manufacturing it's 58.7%, among service providers 56.2%.[8]

Methodology The survey runs through ifo's monthly business-climate survey — self-reported. "Uses AI in business processes" is already met if marketing runs a chatbot. The release page doesn't name a sample size. The number measures spread, not depth, and it's two months old.

My take: 13.6 points in twelve months remains an unusually fast move, and the sector order is the real story: manufacturing leads services — matching the acquisitions above, since where machines already produce data, entry is cheapest. Together with the 95% of pilots showing no effect, that's the honest picture of the year: almost everyone has started, almost no one can put a number on it. Not a bubble, an unfinished rollout — and a market for whoever supplies the number.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · A Second Exit for Machine Data

ELI5 & value: when a plant pours its machine data into a vendor's platform, it sits there in that vendor's format — and if that vendor gets bought, data custody suddenly belongs to a conglomerate with its own price list. The idea: lay a second, neutral outflow alongside the vendor platform that writes the same raw values into an open format the customer owns. Like a second copy of the house key: costs almost nothing, and is priceless the day the lock gets changed. Value: negotiating leverage at the next contract renewal, plus the freedom to run a model on your own data without paying per query.
Prototype (V0): (1) Set up a tap on the existing data stream at one line — OPC UA for controllers, MQTT where it's already present; don't rebuild anything, just listen in. (2) Write raw values to a local columnar store (Parquet files plus DuckDB or TimescaleDB are enough for one line). (3) Write a field mapping: vendor identifier ↔ own identifier. (4) A parity test: the same metric from both sources for 30 days, deviation documented. Deliberately out of scope: visualization, alerts, cloud — the existing platform does that better. Building blocks: open62541 or node-opcua, Eclipse Mosquitto, DuckDB, Grafana for visual spot-checks only.
Market & gap: the evidence for demand is the news itself: Schneider Electric bought Cognite for $3.1 billion and AiDASH for $350 million, ABB bought Rotork for $5.5 billion — the independent industrial data layer is disappearing into corporate balance sheets right now.[1] What the buyers sell is a bundle; what they don't sell is the exit from that bundle — that would go against their business model. Big consultancies handle this as a six-figure corporate data strategy. The gap: the three-plant operation that doesn't want a data strategy, just a copy of its own numbers. The ifo survey supports the breadth of demand: 58.7% of manufacturing operations already use AI.[8]
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. All the heavy parts are standard building blocks; the work is the field mapping, and that's only done once per customer. V0 in 3–4 weeks solo, then 2–4 hours per month per customer for inventory updates. Runs entirely on-premise, which here isn't a drawback but the sales pitch. Scaling via one external developer per rollout — no team needed. forbes.com · ifo.de

2 · Labeling as a Deliverable — Deadline 12/02/2026

ELI5 & value: since 08/02/2026, EU rules apply: anyone talking to a person must know when they're talking to a machine instead, and machine-generated content must be identifiable as such.[9] For systems already deployed before that date, a grace period runs until 12/02/2026.[10] This isn't legal advice, it's a coding job: a notice in the dialog, a machine-readable marker in generated images and text, proof that both work. Value for the customer: a countable deadline gets met, instead of a fine risk of up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover.[9]
Prototype (V0): (1) Inventory of all of a customer's AI touchpoints — chat windows, marketing text generators, image generation, voice prompts. (2) One line per touchpoint: visible notice, machine-readable marker, or both. (3) A small library that sets exactly that: a disclosure component for the chat window, provenance metadata for images (open standard C2PA/Content Credentials), logging as proof. (4) An acceptance test that shows, per touchpoint, that the marker arrives and survives an export. Deliberately out of scope: legal interpretation, high-risk classification, anything tied to the 2027 deadline — that's not part of today's pitch.
Market & gap: everything sellable as text around the obligations is already claimed — law-firm briefings and compliance tools explain the rule thoroughly, as Cooley's rundown shows.[10] What's missing is implementation in the product: a mid-sized company with a chatbot and an image generator in marketing gets an assessment, but no pull request. The gap is the handoff from legal text to shipped state — and it has an expiration date, which makes sales conversations unusually simple. The demand base: 54.5% of companies in Germany already use AI, 56.2% in the service sector — exactly where customer-facing chatbots sit.[8]
Feasibility (solo + AI): High, but with a short window. The work is small and repeatable: inventory one to two days, implementation per touchpoint hours to a few days. Near-zero maintenance, because the result lives in the customer's code, not on a twiceD server. The catch is the clock: after 12/02/2026, the same package sells noticeably harder, and then only as cleanup work. Scaling via a cleanly documented library plus an external developer per project. commission.europa.eu · cooley.com

The same trap shows up in the rollout numbers. The most-cited interim tally is from October twenty twenty-five: twenty-eight thousand trained, just over twenty-four percent of the target. That's not the latest figure, though. In early July twenty twenty-six, the secretary for digital government, Rogério Mascarenhas, said at least a hundred sixty-seven thousand civil servants have now gone through some kind of ministry training. That's above the target, five months before the deadline. Two caveats remain: the July announcement never mentions the target at all, and the hundred sixty-seven thousand figure lumps together eleven programs that also cover data protection, process automation, and data governance. The jump from roughly two thousand to roughly seventeen thousand trained per month points more toward a broader counting method than an eightfold speedup. Bottom line: exceeded by the raw number, but not cleanly measured against the target. And seven point five million for a hundred fifteen thousand people works out to about sixty-five reais a head — the scale of an online course, not of real skills-building.

Two smaller points to close. The Evident index puts Nubank ahead of Itaú on AI capability among Latin American banks — though it only scores publicly available data, so the index really measures who shows the most. And the ifo Institute measures fifty-four point five percent AI use among German companies for May, up from forty point nine percent a year earlier. That's self-reported, and it measures spread, not depth. Together with the ninety-five percent of pilots showing no effect, that's the honest picture: almost everyone has started, almost no one can put a number on it.

For twiceD, I take two ideas from this. First, a second, neutral outflow for machine data, so a plant keeps its own numbers even if its platform changes owners. Second, the labeling requirement as a coding job instead of a legal memo — for systems already in use, the grace period runs until December second of this year.
