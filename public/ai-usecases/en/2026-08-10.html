Ten Problems, $2,000 — and a Checker That Doesn't Flatter

On Saturday, 08/01/2026, OpenAI published solutions to ten problems that had been open for years in mathematics and theoretical computer science, generated by Astra — a model not yet publicly available. Among those named: the construction of a non-sofic group, the disproof of Connes' rigidity conjecture, and three problems from the Erdős catalogue. OpenAI puts the compute cost for all ten solutions combined at roughly $2,000 at GPT-5.6 Sol API rates.

I understand nothing about von Neumann algebras, and I don't need to. What's interesting is what comes with it: 249 pages of manuscript and Lean 4 proof certificates in an Apache 2.0-licensed repository where the sorry count stands at zero.

Formal proof assistant (Lean 4): A program you dictate a mathematical proof to, step by step, which only accepts it if every step follows necessarily from the one before. Like a compiler, just for mathematics: it's not impressed by an argument that merely sounds convincing — it either translates, or it stops. sorry is the built-in loophole for "I'm leaving this step open"; a proof with sorry is a proof with a hole. Tally across all ten proofs: zero.
Caveats No peer review: "none of the 10 results has been through peer review".[2] Vendor selection: OpenAI itself decided what got published, its own staff helped prepare it, and the model remains proprietary — the discovery process isn't independently reproducible.[1] Sourcing: OpenAI's announcement page wasn't accessible (HTTP 403); everything above comes from two independent reports dated 08/03/2026.

My take: the caveat and the news don't contradict each other — they sit on different levels. Whether Astra had ten good results or ten out of a thousand attempts, only OpenAI knows. But whether a single result is correct, anyone who installs Lean now knows — the proof is decoupled from trust in the vendor. The $2,000 is only news because a checker stands next to it. Without it, it would be $2,000 for ten very convincingly worded texts, and no one could tell the difference.

This week's tooling angle — the new coding models from DeepSeek and Qwen — lives in the ai-dev brief. Here, only what this pattern means for deployment outside of research counts.

Bradesco Cites a Billion — and Can, Because Credit Gets Booked [3]

The Evident AI Index dated 07/21/2026 rates Nubank, Itaú Unibanco, Bradesco, and Banco do Brasil as the four most AI-mature banks in Latin America. The ranking ran in the 08/09 brief; today's about a figure from that same coverage that didn't fit there: Bradesco states that AI-driven credit-analysis initiatives have already contributed more than R$1 billion in additional revenue.

Credit analysis: The decision on whether an applicant gets money, how much, and at what interest rate. In large banks, this has long run on scores rather than gut feeling: a model estimates default probability from account activity, history, and surrounding factors. Like a bike dealer who estimates how carefully a new bike will be treated from the condition of the old one — just with a few hundred features instead of a glance.
Methodology This figure is the bank's own self-reported statement, relayed in press coverage of the index, not in an annual report. Literally, it says the initiatives „contribuíram com" — contributed to — more than R$1 billion; that's not causal proof. Missing: a time frame (accumulated since when?) and a baseline (a billion out of how much?). Without both, you can't tell whether the number is big or small. So I'm citing it as a claim with an attribution, not as a metric.

My take: I still trust this number more than the usual percentage from a manufacturing case study — for the same reason as with Lean above. Lending brings its own checker along: every case gets booked individually, every decision has an observable outcome (paid or defaulted), and comparing two decision rules on the same stream of applications has been standard practice in credit for decades. A bank can claim a billion because it keeps the books for it anyway. A machinery maker with 200 employees can't say the same sentence — not because its AI use is worse, but because nobody there is counting. The actionable insight: the industries with provable AI numbers aren't the ones with the best models, but the ones with existing measurement infrastructure. Anyone trying to sell without that infrastructure has to sell the measurement first.

Article 50: In Force for Eight Days, No Movement Today

Running thread Since 08/02/2026, providers and deployers must disclose when a human is talking to a machine, when content was machine-generated, when emotion or biometric recognition is running, and for deepfakes or AI text on matters of public interest.[4] Nothing new today on the enforcement situation in Germany; details ran in the 08/07 and 08/08 briefs. Two things regularly get mixed up here:

  • The fine range for transparency violations isn't the big one. Article 99(4) sets a ceiling of €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover. The often-cited €35 million / 7% from paragraph 3 applies to prohibited practices under Article 5 — a different league, a different offense.[5]
  • The deadline for existing systems is 12/02/2026. Generative systems that were already on the market before 08/02/2026 have until then to meet the labeling requirement.[4] That's just under four months from now.
  1. commission.europa.eu — "Safer and more transparent AI", 08/02/2026. Primary source, European Commission. Verbatim: „Starting 2 August 2026, providers and deployers of certain AI systems must comply with the transparency obligations set out in Article 50"; the four areas per the Commission and the law-firm analysis from Cooley (08/03/2026): „i) direct interaction with individuals; ii) AI-generated content; iii) emotion recognition and biometric categorisation; and iv) deep fakes and AI-generated text on public-interest matters". The transition period for existing systems is documented by cadeproject.org: „Providers of generative systems already on the market before 2 August 2026 have been given until 2 December 2026 to comply with this marking obligation." — Aggregator, secondary source; the same deadline is cited in the law-firm briefings from Cooley and Goodwin.
  2. artificialintelligenceact.eu — Article 99 (Penalties), statutory text, accessed 08/10/2026. Paragraph 4: „up to 15 000 000 EUR or, if the offender is an undertaking, up to 3 % of its total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year, whichever is higher". Paragraph 3 (prohibited practices under Article 5, not Article 50): „up to 35 000 000 EUR or, if the offender is an undertaking, up to 7 % of its total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year, whichever is higher".

Two Institutes, One Country, a 13.5-Point Gap

If someone asks today how many German companies use AI, there are two credible answers, and they don't add up. Bitkom reported on 03/11/2026: „41 Prozent nutzen Künstliche Intelligenz bereits, weitere 48 Prozent planen oder diskutieren den Einsatz."[6] The ifo Institute reported on 06/05/2026, for the May survey: „54.5% of companies in Germany use artificial intelligence in their business processes", 58.7% in manufacturing, 56.2% among service providers.[7]

"Does your company use AI?" — the same question, two institutes, share in %
Methodology Both are self-reported, and the populations differ. Bitkom: n = 604, only companies with 20+ employees, surveyed weeks 2–6/2026 (January/February), commissioned by a trade association with an interest in high digitalization numbers. ifo: a monthly business-climate survey across all size classes, fielded May 2026, sample size not stated. Roughly four months apart. And the "uses AI" threshold is met in both cases the moment a chatbot runs anywhere.

My take: the number that shows the problem is ifo's own year-earlier figure: 40.9%[7] — practically the same figure Bitkom reports a year later as a fresh reading, 41%.[6] One year apart, the same number, two institutes. Anyone who writes an adoption rate into a presentation is mostly documenting who they asked and from when they started counting. That doesn't invalidate the surveys — the direction holds up in both, and Bitkom's year-earlier figure of 17% shows genuine movement.[6] But neither number works as a market size. The more honest Bitkom figure is a different one anyway: 52% of users see a measurable contribution to business success, while 77% just feel better positioned.[6] Between "feels better" and "is booked" sit 25 points — that gap is exactly the market today.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Acceptance Harness — the Checker That Belongs to the Customer

ELI5 & value: A language model generates a quote, an order confirmation, or an inspection report. Whether the result is correct can't be seen from the output — it always looks equally polished. An acceptance harness is a small program that checks every output against the customer's reality before a human reads it: Does this item number exist in the ERP? Does the price match the stored list? Is the discount within the case handler's authority? Does the total add up? Whatever fails goes back into the loop or to a human. That's the same trick as the Astra story above: the proof wasn't credible because the model is good, but because a checking program signed off on every line. Value: the error rate becomes a metric instead of a gut feeling, and human sign-off shrinks from "read everything" to "read the outliers."
Prototype (V0): (1) One process, one document type, 200 real historical cases as a test track. (2) Write the house rules as executable checks: structure via JSON Schema/Pydantic, master-data matching via a read-only query against the ERP, calculation and authority rules as plain functions. (3) Generate–check–revise as a loop with a hard cutoff after n attempts. (4) Report two numbers: fail rate per rule and the share of outputs that needed no human correction. Deliberately out of scope: tone, style, anything not mechanically checkable. Building blocks: Pydantic/JSON Schema, promptfoo for the regression suite, NeMo Guardrails or Guardrails AI for the generic safeguards, a local model via Ollama or vLLM if the data can't leave the building.
Market & gap: The generic layer is crowded and mature. NVIDIA's NeMo Guardrails is an „open-source toolkit for easily adding programmable guardrails to LLM-based conversational systems" under Apache 2.0, with rails for input, dialog, retrieval, and output.[8] Guardrails AI markets itself as „the guardrails framework for building, governing, and scaling production GenAI" and detects „policy violations, hallucinations, and data leakage" at runtime.[9] Both check form and safety — schema, moderation, injection. Neither knows whether item number 4711 exists in this customer's ERP. That's exactly where the gap is: the domain-true check has to come from the customer's own master data, differs for every customer, and therefore can't be sold as a product — only as a custom fit, unattractive for the big vendors, exactly the right size for a small shop. The demand base is documented: 58.7% of manufacturing operations already use AI according to ifo[7] — the base is there, the proof is missing.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. All the heavy parts are standard building blocks; the work is translating the house rules into checking code — craft, not a research project. V0 in 3–4 weeks solo, then 2–4 hours per month per customer, because the rules track the master data. Runs entirely on-premise, which here isn't a drawback but the sales pitch. Scales via one external developer per rollout, the core stays a library. Honest catch: selling is harder than building. You're selling a brake, not a miracle — and people only buy brakes after the first near-miss. github.com/NVIDIA · guardrailsai.com · ifo.de

2 · Impact Proof — the Number the Customer Can Cite Themselves

ELI5 & value: Bradesco can say AI-driven credit analysis has contributed more than a billion reais.[3] A supplier with 200 employees can't say that, even though its people have used a language model daily for a year — because nobody's counting. The idea is a tightly scoped engagement that makes an already-running AI deployment measurable: pick a metric, form a comparison group, measure for three months, and end with one page carrying a defensible number and an honest margin of error. Value: without that number, the next budget round is a matter of belief, and the loudest department head wins.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick a metric the customer already books — turnaround time per case, rework rate, quote hit rate. (2) Form a comparison group that works without large case counts: two shifts, two locations, or a withheld share of cases. (3) Measure from existing systems — ERP exports, ticket timestamps — instead of building new tracking into the process. (4) A report with the effect, its spread, and what the measurement doesn't show. Deliberately out of scope: model tuning, dashboards, ongoing operation. Building blocks: Python/pandas, a before-after comparison with a control group, no platform.
Market & gap: For digital products, this problem is solved and well served: Statsig promises to „Test how each release — from features to AI-powered experiences — lands with customers", including holdouts and power analysis.[10] Amplitude and Eppo serve the same class. But all of them assume an event stream with plenty of observations — a web product with millions of sessions. An inside-sales team with 60 quotes a week doesn't have that stream; there, the comparison group is an organizational decision, not random assignment, and nobody sells exactly that translation. The more important part of the gap: the vendor of the AI solution is structurally the wrong party to run this measurement — they'd be grading their own product. Independence is the sales pitch here. That the demand is real is shown by Bitkom's finding: 77% of users feel better positioned, but only 52% can name a measurable contribution.[6] (That big consultancies sell the same thing as a six-figure program is my own assessment, not a documented source.)
Feasibility (solo + AI): High — the cleanest solo fit in this brief. Pure contract work, 10–15 person-days per customer, then no maintenance load, because nothing keeps running. Scaling through repetition: from the third engagement on, the metrics catalog is the edge, and data prep can go to an external developer. Honest risk: sometimes the result is "no measurable effect." That's why the engagement has to be sold from the start as a measurement, not as proof of success — get that backwards and you sell it once, never again. statsig.com · bitkom.org · moneytimes.com.br

Five rail types (Input, Dialog, Retrieval, Execution, Output); safeguards included per the README: „LLM self-checking (input/output moderation, fact-checking, hallucination detection), NVIDIA safety models (content safety, topic safety), jailbreak and injection detection"; license: „The NeMo Guardrails library is licensed under the Apache License, Version 2.0."

It's Monday, August tenth. A quiet news day, but one with a clear thread running through it: it's not the model that decides how useful AI is, but whether someone can check the result mechanically.

The lead story comes from mathematics. A week ago Saturday, OpenAI published solutions to ten problems that had sat open for years, generated by a still-unreleased model called Astra. Among them: the disproof of Connes' rigidity conjecture and three problems from the Erdős catalogue. OpenAI puts the compute cost for all ten combined at roughly two thousand dollars. Forbes and Yahoo Tech covered it the following Monday.

But the two thousand dollars isn't really the point. What matters is what came with it: two hundred forty-nine pages of manuscript, plus proof certificates for a program called Lean. Lean is something like a compiler for mathematics — you dictate a proof to it step by step, and it only accepts it if every step follows necessarily from the last. It's not impressed by an argument that merely sounds convincing. Lean has a built-in escape hatch for unfinished steps, the word sorry. In OpenAI's repository, that count stands at zero across all ten proofs.

The caveats matter, and they're serious. None of the ten results has gone through peer review. OpenAI picked what to publish itself, its own staff helped prepare it, and the model stays proprietary. OpenAI's own page wasn't reachable for me — it returned an access error. Still, here's my read: whether Astra had ten good hits or ten out of a thousand tries, only OpenAI knows. But whether any single result is correct, anyone who installs Lean can now check for themselves. The proof is decoupled from trust in the vendor. Without that checker, it would just be two thousand dollars for ten very convincingly written texts, and nobody could tell the difference.

Two ideas for twiceD come out of this, and they're really the same insight twice.
