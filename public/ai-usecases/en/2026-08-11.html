AgiBot overtakes Unitree — and the number hides a measurement problem [1][2]

Market research firm Smart Analytics Global published half-year figures for humanoid robots yesterday, and the South China Morning Post reported on the same data the same day. The finding: roughly 19,100 units were shipped worldwide in the first half of 2026, up from about 5,100 in the same period a year earlier — an increase of 272 %. The pecking order has flipped: AgiBot shipped roughly 8,400 units for 44 % global market share, Unitree roughly 5,900 units and 31 %. Together, about three-quarters of the world market. Both companies are reportedly preparing an IPO.

Humanoids shipped, H1 2026 — 19,100 units worldwide, +272 % year over year
Shipped vs. installed: Two counts that get mixed up often. "Shipped" means the manufacturer sent the machine and wrote an invoice. "Installed" means the machine is standing in a plant, hooked up, signed off, and running. It's the difference between bikes sold and miles ridden — the dealer knows the first number, nobody knows the second. The 19,100 are invoices. How many of them are working a shift, and how many are sitting in a research lab, a demo hall, or a university hallway, isn't stated in either report.
Methodology Both sources draw on the same survey from Smart Analytics Global — the SCMP report isn't an independent second measurement, it's coverage of the same dataset. I don't have a sampling or methodology description in hand; how SAG turns manufacturer statements into unit counts isn't disclosed. Add the interests at play: both market leaders being measured are heading toward an IPO, and unit counts are the currency of that phase. I'm treating the order of magnitude as solid, not the second decimal place.

My take: The interesting number isn't the change at the top, it's the gap. 44 % vs. 31 % sounds like distance — in units, it's 2,500 machines. A market where a single big order flips the world ranking isn't a market, it's a pilot phase with a press office. And the 272 % is not an argument for the same reason: growing from 5,100 to 19,100 is a manufacturing achievement, not a statement about usefulness.

A fighter jet under agent control — four weeks old, here for the first time [3]

DARPA and the US Air Force announced on 2026-07-16 that a modified F-16 is flying as an autonomous flight test bed at Eglin Air Force Base in Florida — controlled by an AI agent, with a pilot in the cockpit whose sole role is oversight. Flight operations have been underway since June 2026. The program is called VENOM; DARPA program manager Brig. Gen. James Valpiani describes the purpose as advancing "the infrastructure needed to develop trusted, autonomous air combat capabilities."

Autonomous testbed: A real, fully flightworthy aircraft that also gets an interface bolted in through which software can take over the controls — plus a lot of instrumentation that logs exactly what software and machine did at every moment. Like a driving-school car with a second set of pedals and a dashcam: the student is really driving, but every move is recorded and someone can step in at any time. The point isn't the autopilot — that's decades old. The point is the rework around it, which lets you hand the wheel to software and later prove what it did with it.
On the timing This news is 26 days old, outside the window this brief normally covers. It's never run here before, the source is a primary DARPA release, and the case belongs in this beat (applied AI in the public sector). So it's included today as a delayed item — not sold as breaking news.

My take: For a mid-size manufacturer, a fighter jet is not a reference case. What transfers is the sequence the US military followed: first build the testbed — interface, logging, an abort path, human oversight with a clear role — and then hook up the agent. In the projects I see, it runs the other way: the agent is already there, and the question of logging and abort paths comes up the first time something goes wrong. Anyone who means the word "trusted" builds the test rig before the pilot. DARPA rebuilt a jet for this sequence. A sales-support desk gets by with a log and an approval button — the sequence is the same.

The scale — and whose machine it is [5]

To give the 19,100 a yardstick: the International Federation of Robotics reported on 2026-06-18 that the US installed 38,000 industrial robots in 2025, up 11 %. One country, one year, conventional robot arms. Rough-extrapolating the humanoid half-year — my own extrapolation, not an SAG forecast — 2026 lands at roughly the same scale: around 38,000 units, but worldwide and across all vendors.

The comparison is deliberately shaky in both directions. IFR counts installations, SAG counts shipments — so the real humanoid installed base is probably smaller than the comparison suggests. Conversely, the comparison figure is US-only; far more industrial robots get installed worldwide. What remains: by unit count, humanoids aren't yet a category, they're a line item.

Two numbers that need to be kept apart: more than 97 % of humanoids shipped come from Chinese vendors overall — the country question; of that, roughly 75 % from two companies, AgiBot and Unitree — the supplier question.[2] The first figure means: a non-Chinese alternative is practically absent from the 2026 market.

Figure 02 at BMW Spartanburg — this is what a confirmed customer case looks like [10][11]

This brief's gold standard is a named customer with a verifiable before-and-after number. Of the 19,100 units shipped, exactly one big case has been published by both sides, with figures, under their own name: Figure AI reported the pilot's results on 2025-11-19,[10] and the BMW Group confirmed the same figures on 2026-02-27 in its own release.[11] At the Spartanburg (South Carolina) plant, a Figure 02 unit picked sheet-metal parts from racks and placed them into a welding fixture — a classic pick-and-place task in body-in-white production, run in ten-hour shifts Monday through Friday.

The ten-month result: 90,000+ parts moved, roughly 1,250 operating hours, about 1.2 million steps taken — and a contribution to the production of more than 30,000 BMW X3s. BMW has since turned this into a Center of Competence for Physical AI in Production and is standing up a second pilot in Leipzig with the robot AEON.

Operating hours: Not the time the robot sat in the plant, but the time it actually ran — the difference between "the car has been sitting in the garage for a year" and "the car has 1,250 hours on the engine." That distinction is missing from almost every robot press release; here it's spelled out, and that's exactly why this release is worth something.
On the timing, and on what's missing Both releases are old — Figure's by nine months, BMW's by a good five; they run here as a benchmark, not as news. And they don't answer the commercial question: no cost figure, no statement on headcount effects, no before-value for cycle time or defect rate. Volume is confirmed, economics is not. Earlier issues of this brief carried these figures as "unconfirmed" because only deployment trackers turned up — a research gap, since the primary sources were public the whole time.

My take: Run the counter-math: ten months at ten-hour shifts, Mon–Fri, come to roughly 2,100 possible hours; about 1,250 are confirmed — roughly three-fifths (my calculation, not a BMW figure). That's not a knock, it's the most honest part of the release: a pilot that's idle some of the time is normal; one sold as "runs nonstop" is not credible. The transferable point for a mid-size manufacturer: demand exactly these four numbers from your vendor — parts moved, operating hours, time period, units produced. Anyone who can't supply them doesn't have a pilot deployment, they have a demo.

Project ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with a market gap and an honest solo-feasibility read

1 · The last meter at the robot — a German-language task and control layer

ELI5 & value: A plant buys a humanoid. What ships is hardware, a developer kit, and a handful of demo skills. What does not ship: the description of exactly this station — which part, from which bin, in what order, what happens with a jammed part — and an interface a shift supervisor can operate without a robotics degree. Today, that last meter is handled either by an expensive systems integrator selling a turnkey cell, or by the one in-house engineer who happens to know Python and then goes on vacation. The idea is a thin layer on top: tasks as readable recipes, a German-language control interface, and a complete log of every run. Value: the station becomes changeable without the manufacturer, and when something goes wrong, there's a log instead of a memory.
Prototype (V0): (1) One station, one task, filmed and timed for two days — that's the acceptance run. (2) Lay the task out as a declarative recipe over the manufacturer's kit (Unitree SDK2 or the Isaac GR00T runtime), no custom motion planner and no custom model training. (3) Control interface as a plain web UI on the plant network: task list, start/stop, plain-language messages, all in German. (4) A per-run log — item picked, cycle time, abort, reason — as a file the customer keeps. Deliberately out of scope: machine safety and the cell's CE conformity. That stays with the integrator and the inspector; twiceD delivers the control and task layer and puts that in the contract.
Market & gap: The building blocks exist and some are open. Unitree maintains an official SDK2 on GitHub under BSD-3-Clause — usable commercially.[6] NVIDIA markets Isaac GR00T as "an open reference platform for general-purpose humanoid robots" with open base models, simulation, and the Jetson Thor runtime.[8] But watch the provenance: the open research stack behind AgiBot — AgiBot World Colosseo with roughly a million trajectories and the base model GO-1 — is licensed under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, i.e. explicitly non-commercial.[7] A plant building production use on top of that has a license problem nobody involved raises on their own. That's exactly where the gap sits: platform vendors target robotics developers worldwide, systems integrators sell the turnkey cell as a large project. The thin, German-language, station-specific layer in between is too small for either — and given 75 % market share held by two Chinese vendors,[1] it's exactly the point where a German company gains independence from its supplier.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium — and that's deliberately not "high." The software is craft work squarely in twiceD's wheelhouse: recipes, web UI, logging. The catch is the physics. A V0 needs access to a real machine, meaning travel, plant visits, and waiting times that can't be organized away solo. Hence the scoping: only take customers who already own the machine — then it's 4–6 weeks to the first running station, followed by 2–4 hours a month, because recipes follow the station, not the model. Scale via one outside developer per plant, the core stays a library. Honest risk: if the machine stops because a gripper jams, the customer calls anyway. That needs settling before the first quote. github.com/unitreerobotics · github.com/OpenDriveLab · developer.nvidia.com

2 · Provenance check: is this model even allowed into production?

ELI5 & value: Models and datasets all look alike on GitHub or Hugging Face: a repository, a download button, "open." The licenses underneath aren't. BSD-3-Clause means "take it and sell it." CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 means "research yes, making money no." The difference sits in one line nobody reads, and it decides whether a production system is legal — visible today in the very vendor whose machines hold 44 % global market share and whose research stack is licensed non-commercial.[7] The job: an inventory of every third-party weight, dataset, and library in a customer's stack, with a clear verdict per item — cleared for production, not cleared, or needs a purchased license. Value: avoided risk, and specifically the kind that only surfaces once the product is live and someone looks closely.
Prototype (V0): (1) Automated inventory: what does the project actually pull in? Package manifests, model downloads, container layers, model cards. (2) Resolve the license per item and sort into four buckets: free commercial, conditional, non-commercial, unclear. (3) Chase down the "unclear" bucket by hand — that's the real work, and the reason a pure tool doesn't cut it here. (4) Two pages of results: a traffic-light list, concrete swap suggestions for the red items, and the source citations. Deliberately out of scope: legal advice. The result is a technical inventory with citations, not a legal opinion. Building blocks: existing SBOM tools for the package side — the model and dataset side is the gap that gets closed by hand.
Market & gap: For conventional software, this field is occupied: license scanners for package dependencies have existed for years, SBOMs are now mandatory in many supply contracts. For model weights and datasets, that tooling chain doesn't reach: a model isn't an npm package, its license sits in prose on a model card, and the origin of its training data often isn't stated at all. That's exactly where the expensive cases live — the GO-1 stack under CC BY-NC-SA is only today's example.[7] Demand is also driven by regulation: since 2026-08-02 the Commission has been enforcing the AI Act's transparency and GPAI rules, with violations costing up to €15 million or 3 % of annual turnover.[4] Anyone who has to document what's inside their system needs a list first.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. Pure contract work, 5–8 person-days per customer, no maintenance load — nothing gets operated. The repeat advantage is a growing in-house license database: from the third engagement on, most of the models found have already been assessed once. Scale via repetition and one outside developer for the inventory part. Honest catch: it's a one-time sale with small volume. It only pays off as a door opener — whoever knows the list also knows the customer's next three problem areas. github.com/OpenDriveLab · github.com/unitreerobotics · digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu

The ten-month result: more than ninety thousand parts moved, roughly one thousand two hundred fifty operating hours, about one point two million steps, and a contribution to the production of more than thirty thousand BMW X threes. The manufacturer Figure AI published this itself in November twenty twenty-five, and the BMW Group confirmed the same figures in February twenty twenty-six in its own release. So both releases are old — they run here as a benchmark, not as news. And what they don't say matters just as much: no cost figure, no statement on headcount effects, no before-value for cycle time or defect rate. Volume is confirmed, economics is not. Do the math, and ten months of ten-hour shifts Monday through Friday come to roughly two thousand one hundred possible hours — about one thousand two hundred fifty are confirmed, so roughly three-fifths. That's the most honest part of the release. The transferable point: demand exactly these four numbers from your vendor — parts moved, operating hours, time period, units produced.

And a correction to earlier issues. This brief had carried the postponement of the EU AI Act's high-risk obligations as unconfirmed. That was wrong. The primary text exists: Regulation EU two thousand twenty-six slash one thousand seven hundred forty-four, adopted by the European Parliament on June sixteenth, by the Council on June twenty-ninth, published in the Official Journal on July twenty-fourth, and in force since July twenty-seventh. It pushes the obligations for standalone high-risk systems under Annex three to December second, twenty twenty-seven, and for AI acting as a safety component in regulated products under Annex one to August second, twenty twenty-eight. Article fifty stays unaffected — it's been in force for nine days, and the next date that actually reaches customers is December second of this year.

Two ideas follow for twiceD. First, the last meter at the robot: a thin, German-language control and task layer on top of the manufacturer's kit, so a shift supervisor can change the station and every run gets logged. Machine safety stays explicitly with the integrator. Second, a provenance check for models: Unitree's developer package is under a free license, while the research stack behind AgiBot is under one that rules out commercial use. Nobody reads that difference, and it decides whether a production system is legal.
