The Digital Ministry Lets AI Write — and Doesn't Label It

One case doesn't fall under Article 50; the other wouldn't happen in the EU at all — and that difference is today's more interesting story, more than any adoption survey.

Ministerial speeches from the machine — and who gets to know [1]

The affair is older than Monday's headline suggests — and the order of events is the point. Die Zeit reported on 2026-06-13 that speeches and op-eds by Federal Digital Minister Karsten Wildberger (CDU) were wholly or partly produced by generative AI; heise picked it up on 2026-06-14. On 2026-07-17, the Left Party parliamentary group responded with the formal inquiry "Use of generative artificial intelligence by the federal government" (Bundestag document 21/7187), which asks, among other things: "Which AI model from which operator was used in each case?", "With which prompts were the speeches created?", "What was recorded in the BMDS files about each instance?" and "Is it evident from the file in each case that, and to what extent, AI was used?"[1]

What was reported on 2026-08-10 is therefore not the revelation but the federal government's answer to an inquiry that had been on the table for three weeks — and that answer is the actual sticking point. It is remarkably clearly worded: artificial intelligence is "an everyday, supportive tool," and as long as a human checks the result for technical and qualitative accuracy, "no separate labeling is generally required."

What Article 50 actually says here First things first: the ministry is probably legally right. The AI Act's transparency obligations, in force since 2026-08-02, primarily target providers and deployers of AI systems: anyone talking to a chatbot must be told so; artificially generated or manipulated image, audio, and video content must be machine-readably marked; deepfakes must be disclosed.[2][3] A labeling requirement for a speech manuscript written for a minister who uses a tool and then takes personal responsibility for the text is not in there. So this isn't a legal dispute — it's a dispute about expectations.

My take: That's exactly why the answer is still a mistake — not legally, but as a signal. The BMDS coordinates the national implementation of the AI Act; the Bundesnetzagentur, as the coordinating body, sits under this very ministry. And this was no off-the-cuff remark to a reporter: the ministry had a written parliamentary inquiry sitting on its desk since 2026-07-17, roughly three weeks to settle on a stance — and still landed on "it's just a tool, a human checked it." That isn't a slip, it's a deliberate line, and it pre-writes the defense for every mid-sized company's own customer letter. The phrasing is essentially correct — it's just unprovable. "A human checked it" isn't a fact, it's a claim, as long as nobody writes down who checked what and when. The inquiry's file questions — what was recorded in the files, and is it evident from the file to what extent AI was used — aim exactly there, and today almost no ministry can answer them. The same question will later be asked by the auditor, internal review, and the annoyed customer. If you're going to invoke human review, you should log it — otherwise the claim is worthless the one time it actually matters.

The constructive part that gets lost [1]

The same story contains two things nobody talks about, because they're boring. First: the federal government has its own platform, Kipitz, run by the Federal Information Technology Centre (ITZBund), on which classified documents can also be processed, "without external data transfer" — no data leaves the building. Second: the Federal Ministry of Economic Affairs requires an "AI license" as a precondition for system access.

My take: This is exactly the sequence that's usually missing in the private sector — first the sovereign place of operation, then the access threshold, then usage. The license is more than symbolism here: Article 4 of the AI Act has required providers and deployers, since 2025-02-02, to ensure an adequate level of AI literacy among staff.[3] The Economics Ministry simply turned that into an access condition instead of writing a policy nobody reads. For a mid-sized company, that's a copyable pattern that costs nothing but follow-through. The bitter aftertaste remains: a ministry that introduces the license should also have the tachograph.

Google calls the store — three waves, read together [4][5][6][7][8][9]

On the timing Nothing in this section is from today. The three waves are nine, seven, and three months old; this brief never covered them individually, and the pieces only add up to a picture together. So today it runs as a status report — not sold as news.

Wave 1 — the phone call (2025-11-13). For the US holiday shopping season, Google switched on two things. "Let Google Call" calls local stores for a near me search: "AI does the work for you, calling to see if stores nearby have what you're looking for, how much it costs and if there are any special promos." Initially toys, beauty, and electronics, US only. Plus agentic checkout: you name the item, size, color, and price ceiling, and once the target is hit, Google buys via Google Pay — initially at Wayfair, Chewy, Quince, and select Shopify merchants.[4][5]

Wave 2 — the brand answers directly (2026-01-12). The Business Agent went live in Search: a chat directly with the brand, first partners Lowe's, Michael's, Poshmark, and Reebok. Requirements: a verified Merchant Center account, at least 50 approved listings, and a claimed brand profile — in the US.[6]

Wave 3 — the cart that's the same everywhere (2026-05-19). The break is Universal Cart. Google describes it on its own blog as "an intelligent shopping cart and your new hub for shopping on Google"; the crucial sentence is the next one: "It works across merchants and across services, so you can add things to your cart while you're browsing Search, chatting with Gemini, watching YouTube or even reading your Gmail." Merchants named: Nike, Sephora, Target, Ulta Beauty, Walmart, Wayfair, plus Shopify merchants like Fenty and Steve Madden. Rollout: "across Search and the Gemini app in the U.S. this summer, with YouTube and Gmail to follow."[8] Six days later, Google extended the checkout protocol to hotels and food delivery; Klarna joined in.[9]

Agentic checkout and AP2: "Agentic" here just means: the software gets to press "buy" itself at the end, instead of holding the button out for you. So this doesn't end with a drained account, it needs a power of attorney with limits — that's the Agent Payments Protocol (AP2), which Google built specifically for this: "to help agents make secure payments on your behalf, with boundaries and accountability."[8] Like a store card you hand your kid, but with fine print: only this shop, only this brand, thirty euros max. The technically interesting question isn't payment — that's solved. It's: who's liable if the agent buys the wrong thing within its own limits?

My take: Two things stand out. First, the location of the cart. As long as it sits with the retailer, the retailer owns the relationship. If it sits with Google and is identical across Search, Gemini, YouTube, and Gmail, the retailer becomes a fulfillment agent with a logo — it doesn't lose the sale, it loses the customer relationship. That's exactly why the names on the list are interesting: Nike, Sephora, Walmart, and Target are participating even though they know this. Second: after nine months, there's still no EU date, and that itself is a statement. An agent that unsolicitedly calls a European store collides not just with Article 50 but with a whole set of questions around consent and telemarketing. For a German mid-sized company, none of this creates any action item today — anyone selling it otherwise is selling fear. What follows sits in the next column.

Why this call exists at all [10]

The most expensive way to query a database is a phone call. Google is building it anyway — and the reason is in Google's own specification. For local inventory, Merchant Center requires exactly three mandatory fields: id, store code, and availability. Quantity and price are optional. Availability itself is a four-value field: in_stock, limited_availability, on_display_to_order, out_of_stock. The specification doesn't mandate any fixed update frequency.[10]

Local inventory feed: a file a retailer submits to Google that states, per store and item, whether the goods are there. Like a sign in a shop window that someone could update every morning. Whether they actually do, nobody checks — so a feed that's formally flawless can have been wrong for a week. The customer only notices once they're standing in the store.
Store inventory only exists in the ERP system → Feed optional, coarse, no deadline → Google builds a phone agent

My take: This is the genuinely instructive part of the whole Google thread, and it has little to do with agents. A global corporation builds a voice AI so it can call the store and ask "do you have that" — because the answer doesn't exist as a dataset anywhere. Translated into a German business: before anyone thinks about a sales agent, it needs to be clear whether lead time, stock, and price exist anywhere in machine-readable form at all — or whether they live in the head of the colleague who's reachable until 4 p.m. Most of the failed "AI projects" I see fail one level down, exactly here. And that's why the Berlin dispute and the Google thread belong together: in both cases, a machine is replacing information that was never cleanly recorded in the first place.

Trust is missing — and the number behind it is thinner than it looks [7][9]

Alongside the Klarna story, Digiday cited a Quad/Harris Poll survey in May: 54 % of Americans reportedly find it "unappealing" to give an AI access to their shopping history, 73 % are said to be "uneasy" about how AI might handle their personal shopping data.[9]

Methodology The figures come from Quad/Harris Poll (n=2,180 US adults, fieldwork Feb 5–7, 2026, primary source quad.com/newsroom, 2026-04-13) — more methodologically solid than the Digiday report first checked suggested. Still, there's a familiar pattern here: surveys about privacy unease measure stated attitudes, not behavior — the same population that finds access "unappealing" has been handing Google and Amazon its complete purchase history for years. Equally unsubstantiated is the widely cited claim that "20% of global retail sales" were influenced by AI agents during the holiday season[7] — without a definition of "influenced," that's not a measurement. Both figures appear here as a direction, not as evidence.

My take: The direction is still worth taking seriously — for a different reason than the number suggests. The resistance doesn't form around the purchase, but around the history, exactly the part an agent needs to be useful at all. An agent without purchase history is a search box with extra steps. That's the bind at the core of the whole model, and it's tighter in Europe, because here that kind of data use needs a legal basis. Any retailer entering this space answers the data question before the agent question.

Project ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with a market gap and an honest solo-feasibility read

1 · Availability as a dataset — the store inventory a phone call replaces today

ELI5 & value: A retailer knows exactly what's on the shelf — the information sits in their inventory system and in the salesperson's head. Outside the shop, it doesn't exist. That's why Google is building a voice agent that calls the store and asks.[4] The idea is the unglamorous counter-proposal: get inventory cleanly out into the open — once, as both a Google local-inventory feed and as its own public endpoint. Value for the customer: they show up in local results as "in stock" where the competitor shows "no data"; and they save the phone calls that clog up the counter today. The value isn't "AI" — it's avoiding a silent loss: the customer who buys elsewhere because that listing said the item was there.
Prototype (V0): (1) One customer, one store, one product category — otherwise it turns into a data project. (2) Read-only adapter into the existing inventory system (JTL, Xentral, weclapp, Sage), no write access, no migration. (3) Mapping onto Google's local-inventory specification — only id, store code, and availability with four allowed values are mandatory; quantity and price are optional.[10] (4) A second output: the same dataset as a plain JSON endpoint plus schema.org markup on the product page, so the data doesn't belong to only one corporation. (5) A drift watchdog: daily spot-check of the feed against the inventory system, deviation report by email. Deliberately out of scope: price optimization, campaigns, marketplace integration, POS systems.
Market & gap: feed management is a crowded, mature market — Channable, Productsup, Feedonomics (owned by Shopify), Lengow. These vendors solve the transport problem: getting catalog data into many channels. The gap sits one level deeper and is documented in Google's own specification: three fields are mandatory, quantity and price are optional, and there's simply no mandated ongoing update frequency.[10] So a feed can be formally flawless and wrong for a week — and a wrong availability feed is more damaging than no feed at all, because the customer drives there for nothing. That exact data gap is the reason Google built a phone agent for near me searches in toys, beauty, and electronics in the first place.[4] For the German multi-store retailer with a legacy inventory system, there's no off-the-shelf product for this: the tools assume a clean data stream that the customer would first have to create — and that's exactly the work.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium to high. Adapter, mapping, and watchdog are craft work in a core skill area: 3–5 weeks solo to the first live store. Unlike consulting, this involves real ongoing operation — the feed runs daily, so budget 2–4 hours per month per customer plus rework after every inventory-system update. Scales through reuse per ERP type: the second JTL customer costs a fraction of the first, and an outside developer can build adapter number 3 and 4. Honest catch: no Google agent is calling anyone in Germany today, and nobody knows if it ever will. The offer only pays for itself through the local visibility effect that's measurable today — anyone pitching "a machine will call in 2027" is selling nothing. support.google.com · ppc.land · blog.google

2 · The audit-proof AI note — the record the inquiry is asking for

ELI5 & value: the Bundestag inquiry wants to know three things: which model, which prompt, and how the usage is documented in the files.[1] Any ministry can somehow answer the first two. The third is the real gap: the electronic file holds the finished document — not the information that a language model wrote the draft and who checked it. Like a cookbook with only the photo of the finished plate glued in. The product is a slim note that gets written automatically at filing time. Value: the question is coming one way or another — as a parliamentary inquiry, a press inquiry, an internal audit, an external auditor's review. Whoever can answer it in a minute has a process. Whoever can't has a fundamental debate about AI.
Prototype (V0): (1) One department, one document type — speech draft, standard notice, or form letter. (2) Don't capture inside the model — capture at the handoff point where the draft moves into the file: model and version, prompt as plain text or as a hash, output hash, estimated share of AI-generated content kept, reviewer, timestamp, review result. (3) File these as a separate note document in the existing DMS and link it to the case — no new system, an entry in the existing one. (4) A single report: "all AI-assisted documents in case X or time period Y," exportable as a PDF. Deliberately out of scope: the legal assessment of whether labeling is required — the product delivers the evidence, not the legal opinion. Building blocks: the customer's DMS interface and Office/mail integration, nothing exotic beyond that.
Market & gap: e-file vendors have had AI in the product for a while now — but on the reading side. Fabasoft describes the "intelligent file," which uses Mindbreeze AI to "understand" content, recognize connections, summarize by role, classify automatically, and answer in dialogue with "source references for full traceability at any time."[12] What's traceable there is the assistant's answer — not the origin of a document a human wrote with AI help. But that's exactly what the inquiry is asking about. On the other side sits the governance tooling category (model registries, risk classification, policy management); that works at the system and policy level, not the case level. Nothing in between actually writes into the file. And the demand isn't manufactured: the Digital Ministry itself is invoking the idea that labeling is dispensable, "as long as a human checks the result"[1] — that sentence is worth exactly as much as the proof that the check actually happened. Starting 2026-12-02, every operator of generative systems will want to say the same sentence.[2]
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium. The software is small — a write adapter, a note schema, a report; V0 in 4–6 weeks solo if a department plays along, low maintenance after that. The honest catch isn't technical, it's commercial: public buyers practically never purchase directly from a one-person GmbH. The realistic path runs through a framework-contract partner or the DMS vendor as general contractor, with twiceD as subcontractor — with a correspondingly long sales cycle. Hence the second sales channel outside public procurement, which is actually what carries the idea: regulated companies with exactly the same question — banks, insurers, pharma quality assurance — where the decision-maker is directly reachable and internal audit asks every year. It's Wednesday, August twelfth. Nothing new happened today in applied AI. But two older stories connect to the same question, and it's worth sitting with: does the person on the other end actually know they're dealing with a machine? The first story comes out of Berlin, and with it the order of events is what matters. The weekly Die Zeit reported back on June thirteenth that speeches and op-eds by Federal Digital Minister Karsten Wildberger were wholly or partly written by generative AI. In response, on July seventeenth, the Left Party's parliamentary group filed a formal inquiry with the federal government, Bundestag document twenty-one slash seven thousand one hundred eighty-seven, asking: which AI model from which operator was used, with which prompts the speeches were written, what was recorded in the ministry's files about it, and whether the file even shows to what extent AI was involved. So what was reported on Monday isn't the revelation — it's the ministry's answer to that inquiry.

That answer is the real sticking point. Artificial intelligence, it says, is just an everyday, supportive tool — and as long as a human checks the result for accuracy, no separate labeling is generally required. Legally, the ministry is probably right. The European Union's AI Act transparency rules mainly target providers and operators of AI systems: anyone talking to a chatbot has to be told, and AI-generated images and videos need to be marked. There's no requirement to label a speech manuscript that a minister goes on to take personal responsibility for.

Still, my take is this: as a signal, the answer was a mistake. This happens to be the very department coordinating Germany's rollout of the AI Act. And this wasn't an off-the-cuff remark: the ministry had a written parliamentary inquiry and roughly three weeks to prepare its answer — and still landed on "it's just a tool, and a human checked it." That isn't a slip, it's a considered line, and it hands every mid-sized company the same defense for its own customer letters. And the line is basically correct — it's just unprovable. "A human checked it" isn't a fact, it's a claim, unless somebody actually writes down who checked what and when. The inquiry's questions about the files aim right at that gap, and almost no ministry today could answer them. Auditors and internal reviewers will ask the same thing later.

Two things in the same story get lost because they're boring. The federal government runs its own platform, called Kipitz, through the Federal IT Center, where even classified documents can be processed without any data leaving the building. And the Economics Ministry requires an "AI license" before anyone gets system access. That's the right order of operations: sovereign infrastructure first, then an access threshold, then actual use. The license is more than symbolic — Article 4 of the AI Act has required adequate AI literacy among staff since February of twenty twenty-five. The Economics Ministry simply turned that into a gate instead of a policy nobody reads. Still, a ministry that requires the license should keep the logbook too.

The second story runs in the opposite direction, out of the United States. Google built its shopping agents in three waves, and this brief has never covered them individually before — so today it's a status report, explicitly not breaking news. Wave one, back in November: an agent that calls nearby stores directly and asks whether they have an item, what it costs, and whether there are any deals. Alongside that, agentic checkout — you name the item, size, color, and price ceiling, and Google buys it through Google Pay once the price drops. Both US-only. Wave two, in January: brands answer customers directly in Search through something called a Business Agent. Wave three, in May, is the real break: Universal Cart, one shopping cart that's identical across Search, Gemini, YouTube, even Gmail. Payment runs through Google Pay, backed by Google's own payments protocol, which gives the agent authority with limits.

Two things stand out. First, where the cart lives. As long as the cart sits with the retailer, the retailer owns the relationship. Once it sits with Google and looks the same everywhere, the retailer doesn't lose the sale — it loses the customer relationship. Nike, Sephora, Walmart, and Target are signing up anyway, even knowing that. Second: after nine months, there's still no date for Europe, and that's a statement in itself. An agent cold-calling a European store runs into more than just transparency rules — consent and telemarketing law get involved too. For a German mid-sized business, none of that creates any urgency today. Anyone selling it as urgent is selling fear.

On the trust question, there's a number floating around that I'd only take as a general direction. Fifty-four percent of Americans reportedly find it unappealing to give an AI access to their shopping history, and seventy-three percent say they're uneasy about how AI might use their personal shopping data. It's a Quad/Harris Poll of 2,180 US adults, fielded February 5 to 7, more solidly sourced than it first looked. On top of that, though, surveys about privacy discomfort reliably measure stated attitudes, not actual behavior. Still, I'd take the underlying resistance seriously — it centers on purchase history, which happens to be exactly what an agent needs to be useful at all.

The most instructive part of the entire Google thread has the least to do with agents. The most expensive possible way to query a database is a phone call — and Google built one anyway. The reason is right there in Google's own specification for local inventory: only three fields are mandatory — item ID, store code, and availability. Quantity and price are optional, and there's no required update frequency at all. So inventory simply doesn't exist as a reliable dataset, and that's why a global company built a voice AI to call the store and ask. Translated to a German business: before anyone considers a sales agent, figure out whether lead time, stock, and price exist anywhere in machine-readable form — or whether they only live in the head of the one colleague who's reachable until four.

That gives twiceD two ideas. First: availability as a dataset — a read-only adapter into the inventory system, a clean store-level feed, plus a separate public endpoint so the data doesn't belong to just one company, and a watchdog that checks daily whether the feed still matches reality. Second: the audit-proof AI note — automatically recording, at the moment a draft gets filed, which model was used, which prompt, who reviewed it, and with what result — as an entry in the existing document system, not a new one. E-file vendors have only put AI on the reading side so far: they summarize files and answer questions with citations. Where a document actually came from, when a human wrote it with AI help, isn't recorded anywhere.
