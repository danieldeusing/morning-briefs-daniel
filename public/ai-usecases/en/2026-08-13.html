Alibaba Ships Open Weights — No Vision, No Full Context

Anyone with an AI feature in production has an emergency exit planned — and for most, it's called "we'll just switch to an open model." Overnight, that emergency exit got its first public stress test, and it's narrower than expected: Alibaba shipped the promised open weights for Qwen3.8-Max, but trimmed — no vision, a shorter default context, its own license. This isn't a rumor; it's in the model card on Hugging Face: Alibaba itself lists what only the paid cloud version can do. Second story, opposite direction: an F-16 under AI control — interesting not because of the fighter jet, but because of one sentence about how it was built. And in California, a procedural step today decides the fate of roughly 30 AI bills, without anyone having to vote against them.

The open weights that aren't the model [1][2][3][4][5]

The sequence in three sentences: Alibaba unveiled Qwen3.8-Max on 2026-08-03 and announced open weights for the week of August 10.[2][4] On Monday, no repository existed.[4] Late Wednesday, one showed up — and it doesn't contain the model everyone had been talking about for ten days.

What's notable is that this doesn't take detective work: Alibaba spells it out in the model card itself. "Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B is a text-only model that requires thinking mode for all interactions. Multimodal inputs are not supported." Context: "262,144 natively and extensible up to 1,010,000 tokens." And, unmistakably, the comparison to its own API: it reportedly has "more features, such as vision input & non-thinking support, 1M context length by default, official built-in tools".[1] The license isn't Apache or MIT — it's called "qwen3.8-max", a house license you need to read before you plan around it.[1]

Open weights promised→ Deadline passes→ Checkpoint with no vision, no 1M context→ full capability only via the cloud API

The reaction in the repo's discussion thread hits the sore spot more precisely than any analysis: "when a model is this strong in Vision-Language (VL) tasks and you simply strip that capability away, you throw away half its core value" — and, more bluntly: "removing vision is clearly a push for people to use their cloud endpoint so they can make money".[3]

Mixture-of-experts, "2.4 trillion / 95 billion": Instead of running the whole network for every word, the model is split into many specialists; only a few of them respond per token. Like a law firm with 200 lawyers, three of whom walk into the room for your case — you pay three hourly rates, but the firm still has to keep all 200 on staff. Practical consequence: compute cost tracks the 95 billion active parameters, memory requirement tracks the 2.4 trillion total. Roughly — my own back-of-envelope math, not a vendor figure — even rounded way down to 8 bits per weight, that's about 2.4 terabytes for the weights alone, roughly 30 data-center cards in the 80-gigabyte class.

My take: the outrage is the least interesting part. Two sober conclusions remain. First: "open weights" isn't a property of a product, it's a decision by the vendor — and what gets trimmed isn't availability, it's capability. Any sovereignty plan that amounts to "if it gets expensive, we'll just self-host" just failed its first real-world test. Second: even the full version wouldn't have been a way out for a mid-sized company — nothing about 2.4 trillion parameters fits in a server rack. So the honest question isn't "is the model open?" but "does my use case still work on a model I can actually run?" — and a test dataset answers that, not a press release. That's exactly what the first twiceD idea below targets.

One sentence on the performance debate, since that belongs in the ai-dev beat: on Terminal-Bench 2.1, Qwen3.8-Max scores 86.6, ahead of Claude Opus 4.8 (84.6) and behind GPT-5.6 Sol (88.8)[2], while on the Frontend Code Arena it's only rank 4, behind Claude Opus 5 and Kimi K3[5]. So "on par with the top tier" depends entirely on which leaderboard you open.

The F-16 flies itself — the blueprint is the story [6][7]

DARPA and the US Air Force, under the VENOM program at Eglin Air Force Base in Florida, flew an F-16 under the control of an AI agent. The sequence was conservative: baseline safety flights in June 2026, AI-controlled flights in July 2026, made public on 2026-07-16.[6] Six aircraft were modified for the program, the first arriving at Eglin back in 2024.[7] A pilot sits in the cockpit monitoring every flight.

This is deliberately not a same-day story — it's four weeks old. It's here because the transferable part got lost in the coverage back then, and it's in a quote from DARPA program manager Brigadier General James Valpiani: "The Air Force and DARPA team has automated flight controls and sensors on a standard F-16 without changing the jet's core software." Plus the interface that lets a pilot switch between human and AI control "with the flip of a switch."[6]

Why "without changing the core software" is the expensive phrase: A fighter jet's flight-control software is certified — inspected, documented, approved. Any change to it voids that certification and costs years to redo. Like an elevator: you're allowed to hang a display in the cabin, but the moment you touch the brake, the inspector has to recertify the whole system. That's why the autonomy gets built alongside it instead, bolted on through the existing control and sensor interfaces.

My take: the headline "AI flies fighter jet" is the least useful part of this story. What's useful is the pattern, and it maps one-to-one onto a German manufacturing line: don't touch the certified core, build autonomy as a bolt-on, give the human a physical toggle, put a watcher in the loop on every run. Anyone trying to teach an industrial control system AI rarely fails on the model and almost always on the question of what happens to certification; DARPA's answer is unglamorous and correct — nothing happens, because you don't touch it. The second half of the pattern almost always gets dropped in the civilian AI debate: the agent isn't the innovation, the switch and the safety pilot are. That becomes the second twiceD idea below. For context: six test aircraft with a manned cockpit is not evidence that "autonomous fighter jets are here."

California kills bills today without a single no vote [8][9]

Today, Sacramento's budget committees in both chambers begin voting on the so-called suspense file. The source is unambiguous on this point: "The Assembly Appropriations Committee's suspense hearing votes are scheduled to start on Thursday, August 13." and "The Senate Appropriations Committee's suspense hearing votes will also start on August 13." Whatever doesn't get a committee majority today is done for this legislative session.[8]

Suspense file: in California, any bill that costs the state more than a trivial amount goes into a budget-committee holding pattern before the final vote. Like a "decide later" pile on a desk: on a fixed day, the committee works through the pile and only calls up the bills meant to survive. Whatever doesn't get called is dead — without anyone ever casting a recorded no vote.

On the pile: among others, AB 1883 on workplace surveillance and SB 867, a ban on chatbot toys — both parked there since July 1; early August also saw hearings on AI disclosure in real estate (AB 2025), education technology (AB 2392), workforce impact assessments (AB 2545), and AI in healthcare (AB 2575).[8]

Figure with a caveat The widely cited figure of "roughly 30" AI bills comes from a report that refused a direct fetch with a 403; I only have it as a search-result snippet and it's not verified against the original.[9] What's solid today is only the date and the procedure — the primary-source-confirmed fact that both committees start on 08-13.[8]

My take: for a German software company with US customers, California is the practically more relevant jurisdiction than Washington — privacy played out the same way: whatever Sacramento passes effectively becomes the US standard, because nobody maintains two product variants. California's quirk is just that the decision doesn't happen in a debate, it happens in a procedural routine on a Thursday. Anyone affected shouldn't read tonight's results — they should read next week's list of surviving bills, which is short and says more than any announcement.

Two ways to force compliance on AI: fines or audit reports [10][11]

Since 2026-08-02, the EU's enforcement powers for general-purpose AI are in effect: fines, market withdrawal, technical assessments — retroactive to violations since 2025-08-02. The cap for companies runs up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, and up to €750,000 for EU institutions.[10] Among other things, this covers the requirement that "certain AI-generated or manipulated content must be clearly and visibly labelled and include machine-readable marks".[10]

GPAI (general-purpose AI): a model that isn't built for one task, but for all of them — the same language model sorts invoices and writes ad copy. Like a Swiss Army knife versus a screwdriver. The EU regulates these all-rounders separately because, at build time, nobody yet knows what they'll end up being used for — so the obligations attach to the model itself, not just the end product.

Illinois pulled the opposite lever six weeks earlier. The Artificial Intelligence Safety Measures Act, signed 2026-07-06, is the first US state law to mandate independent third-party audits for frontier models: law effective 2027-01-01, audit requirement from 2028-01-01, targeting models beyond 1026 FLOPs and developers with over $500 million in revenue.[11]

My take: the difference isn't severity, it's who does the work. Europe threatens with a number and leaves it to the regulator to prove something went wrong. Illinois requires an annual paper from a third party — that generates no headline, but it does generate a market for people who can produce evidence. For twiceD, the second path is the more interesting one: a fine cap doesn't get anyone a contract, a recurring audit requirement does. And in the end, both regimes ask the same uncomfortable thing — not "do you have a policy?" but "show me, for this specific transaction, what actually happened."

Project ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with a market gap and an honest solo-feasibility read

1 · Model exit — the stress-tested plan B for the AI feature already in production

ELI5 & value: every company with an AI feature in production has an unspoken contract with a vendor: price, capability, and availability change whenever the vendor decides they should. The emergency exit in nearly every pitch deck is "we'll just switch to an open model" — and today showed what that exit actually looks like when someone uses it: the open version arrived, but with no vision and a quarter of the default context.[1] The product isn't an opinion piece, it's proof: the same task, the same real cases, two replacement models, measured — and, at the end, a switchover branch that actually runs. Value for the customer, two ways: leverage for the next pricing round, and a fallback plan that's been tested instead of just claimed.
Prototype (V0): (1) One feature, not the AI strategy — the inbox classifier, the quote summarizer, the receipt scanner. (2) 50–200 real cases from operations as a gold set, with the output the business side considers correct; that's the actual work, and the reason nobody does it. (3) A lean eval run on existing building blocks (promptfoo or DeepEval, a script if needed), metric and acceptance threshold set beforehand. (4) Run it against two alternatives: a Germany-hosted open model and a second cloud provider. (5) Result: a sheet with hit rate, latency, and cost per 1,000 cases — plus a switchover branch in the customer's code. Deliberately out of scope: the migration itself, fine-tuning, contract law.
Market & gap: both halves of the problem are already occupied, the middle isn't. For the plug, there are abstraction layers — LiteLLM, OpenRouter, Portkey — that turn a vendor switch into a config line technically; whether the replacement model can still do the job isn't something they answer. For the location, there are sovereign endpoints: the IONOS AI Model Hub hosts open models up to Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, "100 % DSGVO-konform und sicher in Deutschland gehostet", at €0.60 per 1M input and €3.60 per 1M output tokens.[15] Against Alibaba's $2.00 / $6.00, that looks cheap — but it's a different model, and that exact unmeasured comparison is the mistake the customer would otherwise make on their own.[2] The gap is the proof that sits between them: nobody sells the mid-sized company the one clean test that says whether their use case survives the switch. The demand base is there — 51.2% of SMEs use or test AI[12], only 21% have a mature governance model for autonomous systems[13]: exactly the group that's dependent without having checked it.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High — the rare case where that's honest. Time-boxed engagement, no ongoing operation, no maintenance load after sign-off: 1–2 weeks per feature solo, the eval run becomes reusable from the second customer on; scales via an outside developer per parallel engagement. Honest catch: it's a one-time deal. The only way it recurs is as an annual repeat — "the test runs again when the model changes," which isn't a hard sell with a vendor that pulls vision capability overnight. Second catch: no gold set, no product. Anyone who can't say what the correct output would be isn't ready yet — in that case, the gold set is the first project. huggingface.co · cloud.ionos.de · mittelstandsbund.de

2 · The switch — human handoff as a verifiable component

ELI5 & value: the DARPA F-16 has a switch: human or AI, flippable mid-flight, and a safety pilot sits in the cockpit on every single flight.[6] Operational business agents almost never have that. They run until someone happens to notice something went wrong — and after that, there's no way to reconstruct who intervened and when. The product isn't the agent, it's the switch built around it: clearly defined abort conditions, an approval gate before any consequential action, a path back to a human, and a log of it. Value: the difference between "we shut the agent down" and "we can prove we had it under control." You need the second phrasing in front of internal audit, the external auditor — and, starting 2028 in Illinois, in front of a third-party auditor who asks exactly that.[11]
Prototype (V0): (1) An agent process already running at the customer's — not one you have to build first. (2) Write down abort criteria with the business side: dollar threshold, model uncertainty, customer segment, N steps without progress. (3) A gate before the consequential action — an approval request in whatever tool the business side already has open (Teams, Slack, email), with a time limit; the default-on-timeout is the single most important design decision in the project. (4) Handoff log: trigger, state, decision-maker, timestamp, outcome — attached to the business transaction, not a separate log file. (5) A report: handoff rate per week and per trigger. Deliberately out of scope: the agent itself and any model work.
Market & gap: the agent platforms build the autonomy, not the handback. Microsoft's own documentation on generative orchestration in Copilot Studio says it outright: "An agent that uses generative AI can also perform actions autonomously." The section "More control options for generative orchestration" then offers three things — cancel the running plan, triggers for "AI response generated" and "plan complete," and clearing conversation history. A human approval step before the consequential action isn't in there; on disclosure, it only says it's "a good practice" to inform the user.[16] That's advice, not a component — and it's a statement about this one page, not the product as a whole. The same blank spot shows up in the other major agent-building platforms, because their pitch is autonomy, not stopping distance. The demand gap is quantified: 74% plan to deploy agentic AI within two years, 21% have a mature governance model for it.[13]
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium. Technically it's state management and integration, not model training — V0 in 4–6 weeks solo if a business unit plays along. The honest price: the gate sits in the path of a production action, so maintenance and on-call are real — budget 3–5 hours per month per customer plus rework after every platform update. Scaling works because the gate gets built once per platform; the third platform integration can go to an outside developer. Honest catch: this only sells to people who already have an agent in production and have already been scared once — a much smaller group than the 74% suggests. Access runs through the incident, not the vision. darpa.mil · learn.microsoft.com · helpnetsecurity.com

It's Thursday, August thirteenth. The most important story of the day happened overnight and looks minor at first glance: Alibaba released the open weights it promised for its flagship model. Just not the model everyone had been talking about for ten days. It's missing vision, the default context window shrank to a quarter of what it should be, and the license is a house license instead of a familiar open one.

Here's how it played out. On August third, Alibaba unveiled Qwen three point eight Max and promised open weights for the week of August tenth. Monday came and went with no repository. Then, late Wednesday, one finally appeared. You don't need any detective work to see the catch — Alibaba says it right in the model card: the open model is text-only, multimodal inputs aren't supported, and the context tops out at two hundred sixty-two thousand tokens instead of a million. The paid API, meanwhile, gets more — vision input, a million tokens of context by default, and built-in tools.

Within hours, someone in the model's discussion thread nailed it: strip vision out of a model that's this strong at vision-language tasks, and you throw away half its value. And more bluntly — removing vision is clearly a push to get people onto the paid cloud endpoint so Alibaba can make money.

My take: the outrage is the least interesting part of this. Two sober lessons stick. First, open weights aren't a feature of a product — they're a decision the vendor can revisit any time it wants, and what gets cut isn't availability, it's capability. Any sovereignty plan built on the sentence "we'll just self-host if it gets expensive" just failed its first real test. Second, even the full version wouldn't have solved anything for a mid-sized company. Two point four trillion parameters total, ninety-five billion active per token — compute cost tracks the small number, memory tracks the big one. Rough math, mine, not the vendor's: even rounded way down, that's several terabytes just for the weights, roughly thirty data-center-class GPUs. So the honest question isn't whether a model is open, it's whether your use case actually runs on a model you can operate yourself. A test dataset answers that. A press release doesn't.

The second story comes out of Florida, and it points the opposite direction. DARPA and the US Air Force, under a program called VENOM, flew an F-16 under AI control. Six aircraft were modified for it, safety flights ran in June, AI-controlled flights ran in July, and it went public on July sixteenth. So this isn't breaking news — it's four weeks old — but it's here anyway, because of one sentence.

The program manager, Brigadier General James Valpiani, says the team automated flight controls and sensors on a standard F-16 without changing the jet's core software. There's an interface that lets a pilot flip between human and AI control, literally, with the flip of a switch. That's the transferable part, and it maps directly onto a German factory floor. You don't touch the certified control software on a licensed system, because any change voids the certification. So you build the autonomy alongside it, connect it through the existing interfaces, and give the human a switch. The innovation isn't the agent — it's the switch and the safety pilot. And for the record: six test aircraft with a human in the cockpit is not evidence that autonomous fighter jets have arrived.

Third item, and it's happening today. California's budget committees, in both chambers, start voting on the so-called suspense file — a holding pen for any bill that costs the state real money. On a set day, the committee works through the pile and only calls up the bills meant to survive. Anything not called is dead for the session, without a single recorded no vote. On the pile: a workplace-surveillance bill and a ban on chatbot toys, among others. The widely quoted figure of roughly thirty AI bills, though, I couldn't verify against the original — the site blocked the direct fetch, and all I have is a search snippet. What's solid today is just the date and the procedure.

Two more numbers on regulation, and a contrast. In Europe, enforcement powers for general-purpose AI have been in effect since August second, retroactive to violations going back to August second of last year, with fines up to fifteen million euros or three percent of global revenue. Illinois picked the other lever six weeks earlier: mandatory audits by independent third parties, the law takes effect at the start of twenty twenty-seven, the audit requirement kicks in in twenty twenty-eight. The difference isn't how harsh either one is — it's who does the work. A fine cap doesn't get anyone a contract. A recurring audit requirement does.

And on adoption, a number that needs a warning label. The KI-Index Mittelstand puts fifty-one point two percent of surveyed German SMEs as using or testing AI, and sixteen point six percent already running AI agents. But the survey was fielded back in November twenty twenty-five, published in March, and it's being cited now in August — the number is nine months old. A co-publisher is a vendor that sells exactly this kind of software, and "uses or tests" is a very loose bar — a coworker with a ChatGPT account counts. Against that, a survey from this same week: seventy-four percent of companies plan to deploy agentic AI within two years, but only twenty-one percent say they have a mature governance model for it. Anyone selling consulting today should lead with the twenty-one percent, not the fifty-one.

That gives twiceD two ideas. First, the model exit: a paid proof instead of a promise. One feature, fifty to two hundred real cases as a test set, an automated eval run against two alternatives — a Germany-hosted open model and a second cloud vendor — and at the end, a sheet with hit rate, latency, and cost, plus a switchover branch that actually works. Second, the switch: human handoff as a verifiable part of any agent already in production. Abort criteria, an approval gate before anything consequential happens, and a log of who took over and when. Microsoft's own documentation shows the gap — it says outright that an agent can act autonomously, and the control options it offers are canceling a plan, a couple of triggers, and clearing chat history. A human approval step before the action itself isn't in there.
