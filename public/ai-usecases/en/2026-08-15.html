A January Number Dressed as August News [1][2]

Yesterday, the headline ran through several German press distributors that AI adoption at German companies had doubled "binnen eines Jahres auf 41 Prozent", with one in three reporting higher costs than expected.[2] The numbers check out. But the source isn't an August story: Bitkom's press release for the "Digitalisierung der Wirtschaft 2026" study is dated 2026-03-11, and the survey itself, by its own account, ran "im Zeitraum von KW 2 bis KW 6 2026" — early January to early February.[1]

Verbatim from the original: "41 Prozent nutzen Künstliche Intelligenz bereits, 48 Prozent planen es oder diskutieren darüber", and on the prior year: "Vor einem Jahr hatten erst 17 Prozent KI im Einsatz, 40 Prozent waren in der Diskussionsphase". Base: "604 Unternehmen ab 20 Beschäftigten telefonisch befragt", representative according to Bitkom.[1]

Methodology Self-reported by phone, n = 604, only companies with 20+ employees — microenterprises, which are most German firms, aren't in it at all. The commissioning body is the vendors' own trade association. The release states no margin of error. And "uses AI" is undefined: a sales rep with a ChatGPT subscription counts the same as embedded image recognition on a production line.

The real finding sits in the second line, and none of the distributors picked it up. Deployment jumped from 17% to 41%, but the "planning or discussing" group only moved from 40% to 48%. So the growth came overwhelmingly from the funnel that already existed — companies that were already in the pipeline a year ago turned intent into deployment. The inflow of newly interested companies is small.

Bitkom "Digitalisierung der Wirtschaft" — share of surveyed companies (n = 604, 20+ employees, fieldwork weeks 2–6/2026)

Two more sentences from the original that didn't make the headline: "Ein Drittel (33 Prozent) hat festgestellt, dass KI zu deutlich höheren Kosten geführt hat, als zuvor erwartet wurde" and "Zwei Drittel (65 Prozent) stellen fest, dass ihnen Wettbewerber aus der eigenen Branche, die frühzeitig auf Digitalisierung gesetzt haben, nun voraus sind".[1]

My take: the doubling isn't the story — the cost line is. A third of adopters misjudged their costs, and "significantly" at that. For a pitch, that means: walking into a conversation with "41% are already doing it" sells FOMO and lands on a buyer who still remembers the last invoice. Walking in with "a third are paying more than planned — let's work out your unit costs" lands on a problem the customer actually has. And a second point for the trade: check the fieldwork date on every study number, not the publication date. Six months sit between week 6 and today, and the market has turned over twice in that time.

The Billion Nobody Has Confirmed [3][4][5][6]

The second number of the week is ChatGPT's user base. Since 2026-07-29 it's been circulating as "close to a billion weekly users." The origin is a report from The Information, which cites company data and explicitly notes this milestone comes "seven months later than the artificial intelligence startup had initially projected".[3] OpenAI hasn't confirmed the figure on its own channels — the last number the company itself gave was "900 million weekly active users, announced in February 2026".[4]

Don't confuse these Monthly ≠ weekly. The billion monthly active users was reached in May 2026 and is undisputed.[3] The billion weekly active users is the reported, unconfirmed figure. Both numbers read "1 billion" and mean completely different things — the most common mix-up in this story.

More interesting than the number is what's happening at the same time. Since 2026-08-06, GPT-5.6 Luna has been the default model for Free and Go accounts, with "unlimited text chats, with no text-based rate limits"; limits still apply to "file uploads, images, and other ChatGPT tools".[5] The broad rollout, including the "Think" button, ran in the week starting 2026-08-10.[6]

Growth target missed by seven months→ best model goes into the free tier→ text rate limit drops→ usage numbers before margin

My take: this is a reach decision, not a product improvement, and it's the most honest available statement about the state of growth. A provider that gives away its best everyday model and drops the text limit is optimizing for countability, not margin. For Daniel's situation, two things follow. First, for your own cost planning: the free tier shifts expectations — what end users get for free, they won't want to pay for in company software. Second, for pitch copy: "almost a billion people use this every week" is currently not a defensible claim. 900 million, as of February, straight from the source — that's defensible and still impressive enough.

Two Continents, Two Opposite Bets [7][8][9][10][11][12][13]

Within 24 hours in early August, deadlines fell due on both sides of the Atlantic that couldn't be more different. On 2026-08-01, the deadline of US Executive Order 14409 expired; on 2026-08-02, Article 50 of the EU AI Act took effect. One side created a voluntary offer, the other an enforceable obligation.

Europe, mandatory. Article 50 has required four things since 2026-08-02: an interactive system must be designed so that "a reasonably informed user understands they are interacting with an AI system, unless that fact is already obvious". Anyone generating synthetic content must mark it machine-readably — "effective, interoperable, robust and reliable as far as this is technically feasible". Anyone distributing deepfakes or AI-generated text on matters of public interest must disclose it, unless the content is "evidently artistic, creative, satirical, or fictional". And emotion recognition must be disclosed to the people affected.[11] In everyday terms for German companies: the customer chat has to say it isn't human, and automatically generated content has to be labeled.[13] Fine ceiling: "up to €15 million or 3 percent of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher".[11]

Machine-readable marking: two different things that get mixed up constantly. The visible label is the notice for humans — "AI-generated." The machine-readable mark is an invisible note embedded in the file itself, so a search engine or a social network can detect the origin automatically. Think of a food product: the ingredients list is for reading, the barcode is for scanning — both describe the same product, but only one of them works at the checkout. Today, C2PA provenance credentials, watermarks like Google's SynthID, and hash matching all run side by side for this.[11]
Shaky foundation The Cloud Security Alliance notes in the same paper that watermarking methods have been broken in research for "under $50 per attack", and that the obligation runs against a "moving technical baseline" rather than a fixed standard.[11] Translation: what "technically feasible" means today hasn't been settled yet. Anyone investing now is buying an interim solution — and should budget it as one.

US, voluntary. And an explicit correction to the narrative that shows up in many summaries: this is not a Commerce Department gate. The classification of a model as a "covered frontier model" is made by the NSA Director through the Department of War; the Secretary of Commerce is merely consulted, via the NIST Director, alongside Treasury and Homeland Security.[8] The order's text explicitly rules out a licensing regime: "Nothing in this section shall be construed to authorize the creation of a mandatory governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement for the development, publication, release, or distribution of new AI models, including frontier models."[7] The building blocks — a classified benchmarking process and a voluntary early-access window — were due within 60 days of the signing on 2026-06-02, i.e. by 2026-08-01.[9] The window covers roughly 30 days of access for federal agencies before a model is released.[10]

My take: for twiceD, the asymmetry is the business. A voluntary invitation to five labs in California doesn't create demand at a supplier in Westphalia. A disclosure duty that's been in force for two weeks with a €15M ceiling creates it immediately — and precisely at the companies that, per Bitkom, only started deploying in January and haven't touched their chatbot since. At the same time, the deferral of the high-risk duties to 2027-12-02[12] is the trap in the sales pitch: the big, expensive part of the AI Act is off the table, the small, immediately due part is on it. Anyone claiming the opposite is selling fear instead of work.

Open Weights Nobody Can Actually Run [14][15][16][17][18][19]

In five weeks, three major models shipped with open or semi-open weights. DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 has been on Hugging Face since 2026-07-31; the model card states "304B params" and clarifies: "This repository and the model weights are licensed under the MIT License."[14] Inkling from Thinking Machines landed on 2026-07-15 — "a Mixture-of-Experts transformer with 975B total parameters, 41B active", explicitly positioned as "a good open-weights base for customization", i.e. a customization base for companies, not a chat product.[16] Two weeks later came Inkling-Small with 276B parameters under Apache 2.0.[17] Add Meta Muse Spark 1.1 from 2026-07-09, which has no open weights but sets the price bracket: "$1.25 per million input tokens and $4.25 per million output tokens" — according to TechCrunch, deliberately pegged to Anthropic's Claude Haiku 4.5 and OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Luna, "though Meta's are marginally higher".[18][19]

Contradiction A second figure circulates for DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731: 284B parameters total with 13B active, a 1M token context, $0.14 / $0.28 per million tokens.[15] These figures come from an aggregator and couldn't be cross-checked against the model card. The authoritative figure here is the vendor's own: 304B parameters.[14] I'm therefore naming the price and context length only with a caveat.

My take: "open weights" sounds like sovereignty, and at this scale it isn't. A model with 304B parameters needs several hundred gigabytes just for the weights, even heavily compressed — that's my own back-of-envelope math from the parameter count, not a vendor figure, but the order of magnitude holds: this doesn't fit in any machine-builder's server rack. The real effect of these releases is something else, and the more important thing for a buyer: they're a price ceiling. As long as an MIT-licensed model can be hosted with a European provider, you can negotiate differently with the closed vendors — and Meta's price parity with Haiku 4.5 and Luna shows the vendors are already pricing that in. The leverage isn't self-hosting, it's the credible threat of it. The performance and benchmark debate around these models belongs in ai-dev territory, and that's where it runs.

Project ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with a market gap and an honest solo-feasibility read

1 · The Article-50 inventory — the obligation that's been in force for thirteen days

ELI5 & value: since 2026-08-02, every customer-facing touchpoint in the EU where AI is involved has to say so. The chat on the website has to identify itself, AI-generated product copy and images have to be labeled.[11][13] The product isn't software, it's a documented inventory: we go through a company's customer-facing touchpoints, determine which ones fall under Article 50, and deliver a concrete, implemented disclosure text for each one, plus a file proving when that decision was made. Value: the fine ceiling is "up to €15 million or 3 percent of worldwide annual turnover"[11] — against that, this is a fraction of a percent. The second, more immediate value: the managing director can answer the board's question without guessing.
Prototype (V0): (1) Touchpoint list in a half-day workshop: website chat, service inbox with suggested replies, shop descriptions, newsletter, application pre-screening, phone assistant. (2) Three questions per touchpoint — does AI generate or alter content here, does a human see the result before publication, and does the recipient notice they're talking to a machine. (3) Sort into three buckets: falls under Article 50 (handle now), touches Annex III and has until 2027-12-02[12], or not affected. (4) Implement the visible disclosure at the touchpoints in bucket one — usually a few lines of text and a config toggle, not a project. (5) One sheet per touchpoint: decision, rationale, date, owner. Deliberately out of scope: machine-readable marking (that's idea 2), any risk-management documentation, and anything under Annex III.
Market & gap: the market exists, but it's aimed elsewhere. The established AI governance platforms — Credo AI (in Gartner's Market Guide, in the Forrester Wave), Holistic AI (ready-made "EU AI Act policy packs" with control mapping and gap analysis), OneTrust AI Governance (continuous monitoring and agent detection since March 2026), and IBM watsonx.governance (200+ rule sets) — inventory AI systems across their lifecycle and are built for the high-risk regime.[21] Gartner now runs "AI Governance Platforms" as its own market category with vendor ratings.[22] The gap is both timing and scale: the regime these platforms target has just been pushed to late 2027[12]; the obligation that applies right now is small, concrete, and not a platform decision for a mid-sized company with 80 employees. Nobody's selling that company the two days of work it actually needs. The demand base is quantified: 41% of companies with 20+ employees were using AI in early 2026, up from 17% the year before[1] — a group that mostly went live after the obligation took effect and hasn't looked at its touchpoints through that lens since.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. Time-boxed engagement, 2–3 days per customer after the first one, no operating load after handover, no liability for a running system. The question list and the text building blocks become reusable from the second customer on; scaling works via an external consultant per parallel engagement. Honest catches, two: first, it's a one-off deal with a small ticket — it only recurs as an annual re-check and as an ad-hoc check whenever a new AI touchpoint appears. Second, and more important: this is not legal advice. Classifying a borderline case belongs to a lawyer; twiceD delivers the technical inventory and the implementation. That boundary has to be in the proposal, or a clean work contract turns into a legal-services problem. cloudsecurityalliance.org · modulos.ai · gartner.com · bitkom.org

2 · The marking pipeline — the mark that survives your own publishing path

ELI5 & value: Article 50 doesn't just require the visible notice, it requires a machine-readable mark that's "effective, interoperable, robust and reliable", as far as technically feasible.[11] The big generators now ship that: Adobe Firefly, OpenAI's DALL·E 3 and Sora, and Google Imagen embed provenance credentials under the C2PA standard in their outputs. The problem starts afterward — in-house. The image passes through cropping in the CMS, gets resized for the shop, recompressed for the newsletter, and somewhere along that path the credential is gone. Like a cold-chain shipping label where every repackaging step tears the tag off: the package arrives without papers even though the manufacturer did everything right. Value: the difference between "we mark it" and "we can show the mark reaches the recipient."
Prototype (V0): (1) Map one publishing path at the customer — generation, CMS, resizing, delivery. (2) Measure at every station whether the C2PA credential is still present; that's a check routine using the open C2PA tools, not a build from scratch. (3) Add a repair step at exactly the station where it gets lost — re-sign the credential with the customer's key, noting the edit. (4) Add a second layer, because the regulator doesn't rely on any single technique: a visible label plus archiving the original file with a checksum. (5) A weekly report on what percentage of published media still carries a valid credential at the end. Deliberately out of scope: building your own watermarking method, and anything that looks like detecting other people's AI content.
Market & gap: both ends are covered, the middle isn't. On the generator end, C2PA stands as an open standard with over 6,000 members — Adobe, Microsoft, Google, Intel, BBC, AP — and with ISO 22144 ("Authenticity of information — Content credentials") in a standardization process that, per the vetted sources, is still at the draft stage; Midjourney is the most prominent generator without embedded credentials.[23] On the regulator end sit the European Commission's voluntary symbol set and methods like Google's SynthID.[11] In between lies the customer's publishing path, and nobody's selling anything for that — the platform vendors take care of their own output, not a furniture maker's CMS. The regulator side has even already admitted the problem: the second draft of the code of practice from March 2026 states that no single marking technique is sufficient and a multi-layered approach is needed[23], and the CSA calls the methods vulnerable for "under $50 per attack" against a "moving technical baseline".[11] The claim that "the credential gets lost along the way" is my own assessment based on how file-bound metadata works, not a cited measurement — the first customer meeting should start by measuring exactly that.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium. Technically it's integration and a check routine, not model-building: V0 in 4–6 weeks solo, once a customer discloses their publishing path. The honest cost is maintenance — this sits in the production delivery path, and the standard is still moving; budget 3–5 hours a month per customer plus rework after every CMS update. Scaling works because the repair step is built once per system; the third system integration can go to an external developer. Honest catch: this is the heavier of the two ideas and needs a customer with real media volume — a shop, a publisher, a manufacturer with a large product catalog. For the trade business with twelve photos on its website, it's overkill; idea 1 is enough there. It's Saturday, August fifteenth. Two numbers are driving this week's story about artificial intelligence, and both deserve a second look. One says forty-one percent of German companies are now using artificial intelligence. The other says ChatGPT is closing in on a billion users a week. The first number is real, but it's half a year old. The second one, OpenAI has never confirmed.

Start with the first. Yesterday, a wire item ran through several German press outlets claiming that AI use at German companies had doubled to forty-one percent within a year, with one company in three reporting higher costs than expected. The numbers are accurate. But the source isn't an August story at all. Bitkom's press release on its "Digitalisierung der Wirtschaft" study is dated March eleventh, and the survey itself ran from calendar week two through week six — early January to early February. Six hundred and four companies with twenty or more employees were surveyed by phone, and the trade association calls it representative. Yesterday's wire piece never mentions the original date.

The more interesting line got no attention at all. Deployment jumped from seventeen to forty-one percent, but the group that's merely planning or discussing AI only grew from forty to forty-eight percent. Almost all the growth came from companies that were already leaning in a year ago — not from a new wave of interest. And two more sentences from the original never made the headlines: a third of companies say AI led to noticeably higher costs than they expected, and two-thirds say competitors who digitalized early are now ahead of them.

My take: the doubling isn't the news, the cost line is. Walk into a pitch meeting saying "forty-one percent are already doing it" and you're selling FOMO to a buyer who remembers the last invoice all too well. Walk in saying "a third of adopters are paying more than they planned — let's work out your real unit costs" and you're addressing a problem the client actually has. And for anyone in this trade: always check a study's fieldwork date, not its publication date. Six months sit between that survey and today, and the market has turned over twice since.

Now the second number. Since July twenty-ninth, it's been going around that ChatGPT is nearing a billion weekly users. That comes from a report by The Information, citing company data, which itself notes the milestone arrives seven months later than OpenAI originally projected. OpenAI has never confirmed it on its own channels — the last figure the company gave publicly was nine hundred million weekly active users, back in February. Don't confuse this with the other billion: ChatGPT passed a billion monthly active users in May, and that number is solid. The weekly figure is the one nobody's confirmed, and both get reported simply as "a billion," which is where the confusion comes from.

What's more telling than the number is what's happening at the same time. Since August sixth, GPT-5.6 Luna has been the default model for free and Go accounts, with unlimited text chats and no rate limit on text — limits still apply to uploads, images and other tools. That's a reach decision, not a product upgrade. A company that gives away its best everyday model and drops the text cap is optimizing for a headline user count, not for margin. Two takeaways: your own cost planning should assume free-tier expectations bleed into what customers expect to pay for in business software, and any pitch that leans on "almost a billion people use this weekly" currently isn't defensible. Nine hundred million as of February, straight from the source, still is — and it's impressive enough on its own.

Third story: regulation, and in early August, deadlines landed on both sides of the Atlantic within twenty-four hours of each other that couldn't be more different. In Europe, Article fifty of the AI Act took effect on August second. An interactive system now has to make clear to a reasonably informed user that they're talking to a machine, unless that's already obvious. Anyone generating synthetic content has to mark it in a machine-readable way. Anyone spreading deepfakes or AI-written text on matters of public interest has to disclose it. The fine ceiling is fifteen million euros or three percent of worldwide turnover, whichever is higher.

In the United States, the deadline for Executive Order fourteen-four-oh-nine expired on August first — and here's a correction worth making, because it's repeated wrong almost everywhere: this is not a Commerce Department gate. The call on whether a model counts as a "covered frontier model" is made by the NSA director through the Department of War; Commerce is merely consulted. And the order explicitly rules out any kind of licensing requirement for new AI models — it's a voluntary early-access window, roughly thirty days, before a model ships. The difference between the two continents isn't how strict the rules are, it's whether they're enforceable. A voluntary invitation to five labs in California generates no urgency for a supplier in Westphalia. A disclosure duty that's already been law for two weeks, with a fifteen-million-euro ceiling, generates it immediately.

And there's a trap hiding in the fine print: the expensive, complicated part of the AI Act — the high-risk obligations under Annex III — just got pushed back to December second, twenty twenty-seven. The cheap, immediate part didn't move at all. Anyone selling AI-Act compliance in Germany right now by talking up the big deadline is selling fear about the wrong deadline.

One more thread, because it affects what you can actually buy. In five weeks, three major models shipped with open or semi-open weights. DeepSeek's V4-Flash has been on Hugging Face since July thirty-first under an MIT license, with three hundred and four billion parameters by the vendor's own count. Thinking Machines' Inkling landed on July fifteenth with nine hundred seventy-five billion parameters total, forty-one billion active. A smaller version followed two weeks later with two hundred seventy-six billion parameters under an Apache license. None of that is something a mid-sized manufacturer can run in its own server room — a model that size needs several hundred gigabytes just for the weights. The real effect isn't self-hosting, it's a price ceiling: Meta's own new model prices right alongside Anthropic's Haiku and OpenAI's Luna, which tells you the closed vendors are already pricing in the threat of open alternatives.

That gives twiceD two ideas, both built around the one obligation that's actually in force today. First, an Article-fifty inventory: walk through every customer-facing touchpoint, sort it into what needs disclosure now, what's deferred to twenty twenty-seven, and what's unaffected — then implement the visible disclosure and document the decision. Two to three days per customer, high feasibility, with a hard line that this is not legal advice. Second, a marking pipeline: the big AI generators already embed provenance credentials, but those credentials get lost somewhere between the CMS, the image resize and the newsletter. Measuring where that happens and fixing exactly that step is the harder of the two ideas, and it only pays off for a customer with real media volume.

And in the interest of honesty: McKinsey's own page on the state of AI in twenty twenty-six wouldn't load today. The numbers circulating from it — eighty-eight percent adoption, but only thirty-nine percent reporting any bottom-line impact — fit a little too neatly with Bitkom's cost story, which is exactly why they're only in this brief with a warning label. Next week I'll be watching for the first publicly known enforcement action under Article fifty, and whether OpenAI ever puts its own weekly user number on the table.
