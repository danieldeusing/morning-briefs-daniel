Seven in Ten Business Workflows Fail — and That's the Best Score There Is

A quiet news day — but one number from this week is worth the whole brief, and it hasn't shown up in this space before. Since Thursday, Gemini 3.7 Flash has led the AutomationBench leaderboard, an open test that measures something nobody has cleanly measured before: can an AI agent correctly carry out a real business workflow from start to finish — across CRM, inbox, spreadsheet, and ticketing system? The best score across the entire model landscape is 30.44%.[3] Seven out of ten workflows end wrong. This isn't mockery: in April, the same models were, per the paper, still "below 10%"[1] — the jump is enormous. But the level it lands on is the real story for a buyer. And the pricing Google is using to promote the whole thing has an expiration date, stated right in the announcement.

The First Yardstick That Measures What a Business Actually Needs [1][2][3]

Almost all AI leaderboards measure something that has little to do with a mid-sized company's daily reality: exam questions, coding tasks, trivia. AutomationBench measures something different. From the paper, verbatim: "Existing AI benchmarks for software automation rarely combine cross-application coordination, autonomous API discovery, and policy adherence. Real business workflows demand all three: a single task may span a CRM, inbox, calendar, and messaging platform — requiring the agent to find the right endpoints, follow a policy document, and write correct data to each system."[1] The tasks are spread across six areas: "Sales, Marketing, Operations, Support, Finance, and HR".[1]

What a "workflow" means here: the agent gets a task in plain language, plus the company's rulebook. It has access to the systems, but nobody tells it which button to press — it has to figure out on its own which interface is the right one. Like a new intern on day one: here's the task, here's the manual, here are your logins, good luck. By evening, only one thing gets graded — whether the right thing ends up in every system. There's no partial credit: "Grading is programmatic and end-state only".[1] Zapier puts it even more bluntly: "Either the right records were updated and the right messages were sent, or they weren't."[2]

The tasks aren't made up: they come from Zapier's own operational data — by its own account, "2 billion AI tasks per month across 3.7 million total companies".[2] And the environments are deliberately messy; the agent has to work through "irrelevant and sometimes misleading records".[1] That makes the test closer to everyday office reality than any knowledge exam.

AutomationBench — share of business workflows completed fully correctly. Leaderboard v1.0.6, own retrieval 2026-08-16; best listed score per model family.

Today's leaderboard: Gemini 3.7 Flash (High) 30.44%, Claude Opus 5 (Max) 26.94%, Kimi K3 22.68%, GPT-5.6 Terra (Max) 21.0%, GPT-5.6 Sol (Max) 19.63%, DeepSeek v4 Flash (Max) 18.11%.[3] Claude Opus 5 shows up with five different reasoning levels between rank 2 and rank 8 — the spread within a single model is over six percentage points, depending on how long you let it think. Anyone who writes a number like this into a proposal has to specify the setting that goes with it.

Methodology Three caveats, all of them matter. First: the test is published by Zapier — a company whose core business is rule-based integration of applications without a model. A result showing that agents can't yet replace that wiring is in the publisher's business interest. Second: the applications run as reconstructed environments, not real customer systems — real-world legacy debt, permission issues, and broken master data are milder here than in the original. Third: Google reports the numbers for its own two models in its own announcement.[4] The fact that the independent leaderboard shows practically the same value at 30.44%[3] is therefore the real evidence — not the blog post.

My take: this is the first number in months I can honestly look a managing director in the eye about. Not "88% use AI," not "productivity up 30%," but: the best agent in the world doesn't cleanly finish seven out of ten real workflows, measured by whether the right thing ends up in every system at the end. Anyone selling or buying an unsupervised end-to-end automation of a multi-system workflow today is betting against this number. And anyone who knows it negotiates differently: not "can your agent do this?" but "show me the pass rate on my fifty workflows, measured at end state." That question costs nothing and ends half of all sales conversations in ten minutes.

Not a Price Cut — a Promotion With an Expiration Date [4][5]

Putting the number in context requires knowing what it costs. Google markets Gemini 3.7 Flash at "$0.75/1M input tokens and $3.75/1M output tokens" — and states in the same text that these prices apply only "through the end of the year", and that starting 2027-01-01 the rate becomes "$1.50/1M input tokens and $7.50/1M output tokens".[4] The decisive comparison sits three weeks earlier: "$1.50/1M input tokens and $7.50/1M output tokens" was already the price of the predecessor, Gemini 3.6 Flash, from Jul 21.[5]

So the situation is clear: the price wasn't lowered. There's a discount promotion running with an announced end date, and the regular price hasn't changed. Anyone pricing out an automation today is calculating at half price — and the doubling isn't a forecast, it's stated right in the announcement.

Quote today at the promo price→ Project gets approved→ 2027-01-01 regular price→ Unit cost doubled

A numerical example to make the scale tangible — the token volumes in it are my own assumption, not a sourced figure: a workflow with 40,000 input and 8,000 output tokens costs roughly $0.06 at the promo price, roughly $0.12 at the regular price. At 10,000 workflows a month, that's $1,200 instead of $600 — a footnote line in the approval memo turns into a four-figure monthly cost, on a date that's already known today.

On top of that comes an effect the price list doesn't show and that helps explain the 30.44%: failed attempts cost full price. A workflow that fails at step six of eight has already burned the tokens for steps one through six. At a pass rate around 30%, most of the bill is for runs that leave nothing behind. Every calculation I've seen so far is missing exactly this line.

My take: this isn't a knock on Google — launch pricing is normal, and the text is honest. The mistake happens on the buyer's side. For a mid-sized company, that means in practice: the business case has to be built twice, once at the promo price and once at the regular price, with a failure rate baked in. If it only pencils out at the promo price, the project isn't economical — it's subsidized.

The Objection to My Own Headline [1][3]

"Seven in ten fail" is the honest reading of the number — but it's not the only one, and the counterargument is strong enough that I'm writing it down.

First, the slope. In April, the paper recorded "below 10%" for the best models.[1] Four months later, it reads 30.44%.[3] Anyone basing a purchasing decision on today's level is basing it on a number that has recently tripled every few months. That's an argument for short contract terms and swappable models — not for shelving the topic.

Second, the harshness of the grading. All-or-nothing at end state[1] is the right yardstick for unsupervised automation and the wrong one for everything else. An agent that correctly completes seven of eight steps and stalls on the eighth counts as zero — in an office, it would still be a relief if a human handled the last step. So the 30% doesn't measure "how much work does this take off my plate," it measures "how often am I allowed to look away."

Third, the spread. The fact that a single model ranges from 20.4% to 26.94% depending on the reasoning level[3] means: a good chunk of the performance isn't in the model, it's in the setting and how the task is cut. That's exactly the part a service provider can influence.

My take stands nonetheless: both readings lead to the same recommendation, and that's rare. Don't buy an unsupervised end-to-end chain. Cut the workflow so the agent only gets the pieces where a failure is noticeable and cheap. And measure on your own workflows instead of trusting a leaderboard — including this one.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Per-Workflow Cost Cap — What the Automation Will Actually Cost Starting in January

ELI5 & value: AI automation is billed by tokens consumed — that is, by the amount of text going in and coming out. For a managing director, that's a bill with no unit price: they see a total at month's end, but nobody has ever told them what a single order confirmation costs. That's exactly where a dated trap is opening up right now. Google lists Gemini 3.7 Flash at $0.75 / $3.75 per million tokens, but only "through the end of the year"; starting 2027-01-01 it reads "$1.50/1M input tokens and $7.50/1M output tokens"[4] — the same price the predecessor model already had in July.[5] So nothing got cheaper; there's a promotion running. Anyone approving a business case today has built in a cost doubling in 4.5 months without knowing it. The product: a measured per-workflow unit-cost calculation, at both price levels and with a failure rate, leading to a ceiling that goes into the contract. Value: the difference between "AI costs roughly $600 a month" and "each order confirmation costs 6 cents today, 12 cents starting in January, and switching pays off past this volume."
Prototype (V0): (1) 100 of the client's real workflows as a measurement set, pulled from live operations, not constructed. (2) Run them through a gateway that logs tokens per call — LiteLLM or Langfuse as an off-the-shelf building block, no in-house build. (3) Analysis: cost per workflow, per model, at both price levels — including the runs that fail and still consume tokens. (4) A threshold table: at what volume which model wins, and when switching pays off. (5) A contract clause: a measured cost ceiling per workflow, with a renegotiation trigger on the vendor's price change. Deliberately out of scope: ongoing operations, real-time alerts, dashboards — that's the tools market below, not this one.
Market & gap: the tool category is crowded and currently consolidating. Regularly named: CloudZero, Langfuse, Portkey, LiteLLM, Datadog LLM Observability, and Amnic; plus two acquisitions this year — Langfuse went to ClickHouse on 2026-01-16, Helicone to Mintlify on 2026-03-03 and has been in maintenance mode since.[9] What all these tools do: they show retrospectively what was spent, and they're sold per seat to engineering teams. A mid-sized company with 80 employees has no engineering team to run an observability tool, and the question it actually has is different: what will this cost going forward, at the regular price, per workflow, and where does that live in the contract. So the gap isn't technical — it's translating measured values into a commercial statement and a clause. The trigger is dated and the same for every client: 2027-01-01.[4]
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. A time-boxed engagement, 3–5 days per client after the first one, no ongoing operations, no liability for a running system. The measurement setup becomes reusable from the second client onward; parallel engagements can be covered via a contract developer. Two honest catches: first, this is a small ticket item and only recurs when prices or models move — which, admittedly, is currently happening quarterly and is guaranteed at the turn of the year. Second: twiceD drafts the contract clause as a commercial template; the client's own lawyer makes it signature-ready. That boundary belongs in the proposal. blog.google · cloudzero.com

2 · The 70-Percent Breakdown — Which Steps of the Workflow Need No Model at All

ELI5 & value: AutomationBench grades only the end state, all or nothing.[1] An eight-step workflow fails completely if step six goes wrong — which is why the entire field sits under 31%.[3] That leads to an uncomfortable but very sellable insight: the lever for a mid-sized company isn't a better agent, it's a smaller task for the agent. You break the workflow into three kinds of steps — ones a simple rule can handle (near error-free, fractions of a cent), ones where the model's output can be machine-checked against existing master data, and ones that need genuine judgment. The first two get automated; the third gets a clean handoff. Like an assembly line station: the machine does the same repetitive motions every time, a human does the visual inspection — nobody would hand the whole line to an automaton that spits out the right part three times out of ten. Value: you get the boring bulk of the workflow at near-full reliability, instead of betting the entire workflow on a 30% chance.
Prototype (V0): (1) One workflow, documented across 20–30 real cases: who touches what in which system. (2) Two questions per step — is there a fixed rule, and is there a machine check against existing master data. (3) Build the rule-based steps on existing integration platforms (Zapier, n8n, Power Automate), not custom-coded. (4) Model only where question two comes back "yes" — with the check as a hard gate beforehand, not a recommendation afterward. (5) Measure: pass rate per step and across the whole workflow, against the same cases as at the start. Deliberately out of scope: anything requiring judgment, and any attempt to hand the full chain to the agent.
Market & gap: the integration layer has long been a commodity. Zapier alone advertises "66,000+ triggers and actions, plus 140,000+ private integrations"[2]; UiPath, Microsoft Power Automate, n8n, and Make sell the same layer — and all of them now bolt an agent on top. What nobody sells is the decision of which step of a specific German mid-market workflow belongs in which bucket. That's not a product, it's one or two days of hands-on work inside the client's systems, in German, using their own terminology — exactly what a California platform can't deliver and what a big consultancy doesn't offer below enterprise scale. The proof that this decision even matters is, for the first time, public and hard: across end-to-end business workflows, the entire top field sits under 31%.[3]
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High for the analysis, medium if twiceD also builds it. The breakdown takes 2–4 days, the output is a decision table, and it costs no ongoing operations. Building the rule-based steps is classic integration work, billable and scalable via a contract developer; the maintenance burden stays with the client as long as twiceD doesn't take over operations. Honest catch: the client may not like the result — "a third of your workflow shouldn't touch AI at all" shrinks the budget they were about to approve. So this has to be sold as a precursor to the project, not a replacement for it. And it needs a client with a workflow that runs often enough: at 30 cases a month, a breakdown isn't worth it; at 3,000, it is immediately. arxiv.org · zapier.com/benchmarks · zapier.com
