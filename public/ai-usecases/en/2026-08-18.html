Anthropic Is No Longer Just Selling the Engine [1][2]

On July 15, 2026, "Ode with Anthropic" officially launched: a company whose entire business is building other companies' AI into other companies' business processes. Capital: $1.5 billion. Anchor investors ("founding partners" per the announcement) are Anthropic itself, Blackstone, and Hellman & Friedman; the wider investor consortium also includes Goldman Sachs, Apollo, General Atlantic, Leonard Green, GIC, and Sequoia. The announcement names the target audience, verbatim, as mid-sized companies in "financial services, healthcare, retail, manufacturing, software, and other industries". The team: "Ode currently employs 100 engineers," of whom "over half... are former founders."

Why an "implementation firm" is different from a model vendor: The model vendor builds the engine and sells it by consumption — every bit of text processed costs a few cents. The implementation firm builds the engine into a specific car: it looks at how quotes actually get made at this particular business, wires the model into the ERP, decides who approves what. It sells days, not tokens. This is the business Accenture and IBM have run with SAP for thirty years — except this time, someone's setting it up while the technology is still red hot.

The remarkable part isn't the sum, it's who's putting it up. A model maker investing in a services firm is implicitly saying: our product alone doesn't land with the customer. Ode CEO Chris Taylor states the ambition accordingly big — "It's pretty easy to imagine this as a trillion-dollar company someday if we execute well."

Methodology The split cited everywhere — roughly $300 million each from Anthropic, Blackstone, and Hellman & Friedman, $150 million from Goldman Sachs — comes from secondary reporting on the initial announcement of May 4, 2026, and couldn't be checked against a primary release with this breakdown. Several independent outlets cite the same figures. The $1.5 billion total itself also doesn't appear verbatim in the official announcement — it too rests on broad, consistent secondary reporting; only the investor names and their grouping (anchor investors vs. wider consortium) appear verbatim in the announcement.

My take: for twiceD, this is the most important news of the month — as confirmation, not threat. When capital at this scale bets that the margin sits in deployment rather than in the model, that's exactly the bet a one-person firm with an AI lever can place too, just without having to feed 100 engineers. Because whoever has to service $1.5 billion in returns doesn't sell four-week projects to a 60-person business in rural Germany — a firm like that needs contracts where 100 engineers can bill hours. Everything below that gets left on the table, and "everything below that" is practically all of German Mittelstand.

A Billion Users — and Why the Number Says Less Than It Looks Like [4][5]

On August 11, 2026, Google reported that the Gemini app had "officially surpassed 1 billion monthly users" — by its own account the fastest-growing product in the company's history, and the 14th Google product to reach that mark. Two secondary figures from the same announcement: "Gemini now generates 150 million+ images every day" and "63% percent of users now talk directly to Gemini," i.e. by voice rather than by typing. The jump is steep: the quarterly announcement from late July still put it at "over 950 million monthly users" — roughly 50 million more in about two weeks.

MAU, WAU — and why this isn't nitpicking: "Monthly active users" counts anyone who showed up at least once in 30 days; "weekly active users" is the same thing over 7 days. For the same product, the second number is always smaller — and more telling, because it measures habit rather than curiosity. Like a gym: "a million members who came in at some point this year" sounds like "a million who show up every week," but it's a completely different business.

That's exactly where the comparisons making the rounds fall apart. For ChatGPT, the figure circulating for June 2026 is "1 billion" — the reporting I reviewed labels it monthly active users, while other accounts describe it as weekly active users. Which metric OpenAI itself published, I couldn't verify against a primary text. So no head-to-head comparison appears here.

My take: for the business side, the billion mostly confirms a non-problem. Nobody has to explain to their staff anymore what a chat window is; two-thirds now talk to it instead of typing. Access, usability, and acceptance are settled. What isn't settled: whether any of it lands in a workflow you can measure and bill for. Reach and impact are completely decoupled — the number for that sits right next door.

Standing Thread — Regulation, Kept Short [7]

Since August 2, 2026, the Commission's AI Office and national authorities have been enforcing the EU's AI Act — verbatim: "From 2 August 2026, the European Commission's AI Office, together with national authorities, will begin enforcing the Artificial Intelligence (AI) Act". That also brings the transparency obligations live: a chatbot must identify itself as a machine, and artificially generated or altered content must be labeled. Fine ceiling for model providers: up to 3% of worldwide turnover.

No more on this here today: the August 17 issue covered the Act in depth — in particular the split between providers of general-purpose models (the fines target them) and deployers (they don't). My take: the transparency obligation is the only line from this date that costs an ordinary mid-sized business any work today — and it costs little: anyone running a customer chatbot adds one sentence to the greeting. Anyone who hasn't done that yet doesn't have a legal problem, they have a to-do-list problem.

8.3x: The Number That Shows Where It Really Snags [6]

On August 12, 2026, OpenAI published a report on enterprise usage. The one finding worth quoting: "frontier firms — those in the top 10% of AI usage each month — now generate 8.3× as many output tokens per active user as typical firms". Not 8.3 times as many users. 8.3 times as much per active user. The gap, then, isn't created by more people having access — it's created by what those with access actually do.

Output tokens: A token is a chunk of a word; "output tokens" are the characters the model generates — the unit the vendor bills by. As a success metric, that's as informative as counting a writer's typed pages: lots of text could mean someone has deeply embedded the tool in their work — or that a poorly built automation rewrites the same report three times a day. The number measures load, not output. And it's simultaneously the revenue figure of the party doing the measuring.
Methodology Three caveats, all material. First: the figure comes from OpenAI about OpenAI usage — commissioner, collector, and beneficiary are the same party. Second: the primary page returned HTTP 403 on retrieval; the figure reaches me via matching secondary reporting, so I couldn't verify the definition of "frontier firm" or the data basis (usage logs? a survey?) against the original. Third: "output tokens per active user" is a consumption metric. It doesn't measure an outcome — hours saved, lower error rates, higher throughput.

Still, the finding is useful if read correctly: as a concentration finding. Access is distributed evenly; usage is not — off by almost an order of magnitude. The chain behind it is the same in nearly every business:

Licenses purchased→distributed to everyone→no workflow changed→usage evaporates

My take: this is the number that explains the Ode and IBM deals. If the top ten percent get eight times as much out of it, the difference isn't the model — everyone has the same one — it's the work behind it: who rebuilt a workflow instead of just handing out a tool? That work is exactly what's sellable, and billable by the day. For twiceD, the interesting customer therefore isn't the one who doesn't have AI yet. It's the one who's been paying for licenses for a year and can't say what they've achieved. That becomes Idea 1 below.

Methodology Nubank's own press page returned HTTP 403 on two attempts. Both figures come from secondary sources that agree with each other but aren't primary-sourced. My take: this is the gold standard I measure every consulting slide against: named company, named process, a number with a time frame. The lesson for Mittelstand, then, isn't "build it yourself" — it's the question that comes before that: is the process I want to touch with AI my core business or my administrative overhead? For the core business, depth pays off. For the rest, buying in is enough.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The AI Reality Check — Does the Purchased License Ever Land in a Process?

ELI5 & value: By now, a great many mid-sized companies have bought AI — Copilot licenses, a ChatGPT Team subscription, an AI feature in the CRM. What almost none of them can say: whether any of it has landed in a real workflow. OpenAI's own figure makes the gap visible: the top ten percent of firms by usage generate 8.3x per active user.[6] With the same tool. So the difference isn't the technology — it's whether someone rebuilt a workflow. The product is a short, paid assessment: five days, ending in three pages — what's actually being used, where it evaporates, and the three workflows where a rebuild pays off, with estimated effort and estimated return. Value to the client: for the first time, they hear what their ongoing license costs are actually delivering — and get a reasoned decision against two out of five ideas along with it, which often saves more than the rebuild itself.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pull usage data from what the client already has — for Microsoft shops, the Copilot dashboard in Viva Insights; for others, the respective vendor's admin reports; instrument nothing yourself. (2) Eight to twelve half-hour conversations along one value stream (quote, order, complaint) — not departmental interviews, but the path a case actually takes. (3) Three numbers per process step: processing time, error/callback rate, monthly volume. That's the baseline, without which no return can later be claimed. (4) Score candidates by volume × time share × rule-boundedness, checked strictly against data protection and criticality. (5) Three pages of findings plus a fixed-price offer for exactly one rebuild — that's the bridge to Idea 2. Deliberately out of scope: custom measurement software, a permanent dashboard, any form of operational responsibility.
Market & gap: the obvious incumbent is Microsoft itself. The Copilot dashboard in Viva Insights, per its own description, measures "Readiness, adoption, impact, and sentiment" and is remarkably accessible: "Neither a paid Viva Insights license nor a Microsoft 365 Copilot license is required to view the dashboard."[9] But it rules out two things. First, the sizing: the full analysis — benchmarks, weekly and monthly trends, agent breakdowns — only kicks in at "at least 50 Copilot licenses" or 50 Viva Insights licenses; below that, i.e. across the entire German Mittelstand running 20 licenses, you're stuck with the stripped-down version.[9] Second, the definition: an "Active Microsoft 365 Copilot user" is anyone who "used Copilot at least once … during the last 28 days".[9] One click in four weeks counts as "active" — and that's precisely the number management then sets aside, satisfied. On top of that, the dashboard only sees Microsoft's own tools; the marketing department's ChatGPT subscription and the ERP's AI feature show up nowhere. Demand is there: Bitkom counts "41 percent already using artificial intelligence" versus 17 percent a year earlier, 604 phone-surveyed companies with 20+ employees, fieldwork weeks 2–6/2026.[10] In plain terms, that doubling means a great many of these firms are in their first year after purchase — the year nobody knows yet whether it pays off. The gap is therefore clean: vendor tools measure their own usage and define "active" generously; nobody measures across tools, and nobody translates the result into a build-or-drop decision.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. Pure consulting: no operations, no liability for a running system, no on-call duty. Five days per client, and much of the analysis and write-up can be prepared heavily with AI; the interview guide, scoring grid, and report template become reusable after the second client. An outside developer can take on the data analysis, not the conversations. Two honest catches: first, it's a small ticket — it only pays off because it sells the fixed-price offer from Idea 2 alongside it; as a standalone product it's too thin. Second, the assessment sometimes delivers the result "nothing here is worth it," and you have to be able to live with that and still bill for it. Anyone who can't do that will talk themselves into a rebuild and lose the client in year two. learn.microsoft.com · bitkom.org · openai.com

2 · Fixed-Price Single-Process Deployment — The Ode Template, Two Sizes Smaller

ELI5 & value: Exactly what Ode and IBM set up this summer as a billion-dollar business — building someone else's AI into a real operational workflow — but for one process, in six weeks, at a price named up front. No program, no framework agreement, no transformation consulting. The client buys an outcome: "Incoming goods receipts will now be read and pre-coded by machine, a human confirms, processing time drops from X to Y" — with baseline X coming from the assessment in Idea 1. Value: for the first time, the mid-sized business gets an AI project with an end date and a price tag instead of a day rate with no horizon. And twiceD gets an offer that explains itself in one sentence.
Prototype (V0): (1) Only take on processes for which Idea 1 has produced a measured baseline — no before-figure, no fixed price, no exceptions. (2) Build with purchased building blocks: model via the respective vendor's API, workflow orchestration with an established tool (n8n, Temporal, Camunda), integration via the ERP's existing interface — nothing custom-built where a standard exists. (3) Always with human sign-off at the last step; the expensive mistake happens at the end, and that's where a human is cheapest. (4) Four weeks of parallel operation, running old and new side by side and comparing the numbers — that's the acceptance test. (5) Handover with an operating manual and a small maintenance contract; operations sit with the client or their hosting provider, not with twiceD. Deliberately out of scope: multiple processes at once, model training, anything that forces on-call duty.
Market & gap: competition has grown considerably stronger this summer — precisely one size above this template. Ode with Anthropic launched with $1.5 billion and 100 engineers for mid-sized companies in "financial services, healthcare, retail, manufacturing, software";[1][2] IBM, four weeks later, embedded GPT-5.6, Codex, and ChatGPT Work into IBM Consulting Advantage, moved up into OpenAI's "Elite partner tier," and targets "financial services, government, telecommunications, and retail".[3] Both lists are enterprise-customer lists, and both cost structures force large deals: a firm that has to keep 100 engineers billable can't sell a six-week project for €25,000 — not because it doesn't want to, but because the sales cost would exceed the deal itself. That's exactly the gap, and it's structural, not temporary. On top of that comes the German fit neither of them brings: contract and documentation in German, processing within the EEA, one point of contact instead of a delivery team. Demand is documented and fresh: 41% of German companies with 20+ employees already use AI, up from 17% a year earlier.[10] What they don't have is shown by OpenAI's concentration figure — usage that actually lands in workflows.[6]
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium — and honestly medium. Building is solo-feasible: the heavy lifting comes from the APIs, twiceD builds the integration, validation rules, and sign-off. Six weeks per project, three to four projects a year alone, more only with an outside developer per engagement. The catch sits behind the acceptance test: a deployed workflow is production. If the vendor changes its model or the client updates its ERP, the phone rings — unlike with a consulting engagement. Hence three hard rules: operations stay with the client, the maintenance contract is priced rather than "included in support," and no workflow gets built without a human sign-off. The second catch is the fixed price itself — it's the selling point and also the exact spot where small shops die. It only works because Idea 1 comes first: whoever has measured the workflow for five days can price it. Whoever skips that is guessing. ode.com · newsroom.ibm.com · techcrunch.com · bitkom.org

So the gap isn't created by more access, it's created by what people with access actually do. Three warning labels apply: the figure comes from OpenAI about OpenAI usage, the primary page was blocked on retrieval, and what's being measured is text volume generated — consumption, not outcome. Still, as a concentration finding it holds up, and it explains both billion-dollar bets in one shot.

A counter-image from Brazil, explicitly not news from today. Nubank built its own model, nuFormer, for credit assessment. Reported: a seventy percent drop in projected risk for comparable customer segments, and a gain of fifty basis points in Brazilian credit-card volume market share in the fourth quarter of twenty twenty-five. Both figures come from secondary sources; Nubank's own press page was blocked. The lesson isn't that everyone should build their own model. It's the question that comes first: is the process I want to touch my core business or my administrative overhead? For the core business, depth pays off. For the rest, buying in is enough.

That gives us two ideas for twiceD, and they connect. The first is a short, paid assessment: five days, three pages, the question of whether the AI licenses already purchased ever land in a real workflow. The incumbent here is Microsoft with its Copilot dashboard, but it has two holes. The full analysis with benchmarks and trends only kicks in at fifty licenses — German Mittelstand running twenty licenses gets the stripped-down version. And a user counts as active there after just one click in twenty-eight days. That's exactly the number management then sets aside, satisfied. Feasibility: high — pure consulting, no operations, no liability.

The second idea is the deployment itself, at a fixed price, for exactly one process, in six weeks. In other words, what Ode and IBM are setting up as a billion-dollar business, two sizes smaller. The gap is structural: a firm with a hundred engineers can't sell a six-week project for twenty-five thousand euros, because the sales cost would exceed the deal. And demand is documented — Bitkom counts forty-one percent of German companies with twenty or more employees already using AI, up from seventeen percent a year earlier. Feasibility here is only medium, and honestly so: a deployed workflow is production, the phone rings, and the fixed price is both the selling point and the spot where small shops die. It only works because the assessment comes first.

Last, the gaps. Ode is widely described as a vehicle for sovereign or regulated deployments — the official announcement contains not a word of that. So I'm not writing it, much as I'd like to. The investor split appears only in secondary reports. And on Europe's high-risk deadlines, the sources disagree: a reported omnibus package would push the conformity assessment to December twenty twenty-seven, while the regulation portal's timeline as of yesterday still showed August twenty twenty-seven. The official text wasn't retrievable today, so I'm not treating either deadline as confirmed. Over the next few days, I'll be watching whether Ode or IBM produces a first named customer case study — that, and only that, will show whether this bet turns into an actual business.
