ChatGPT Now Guesses Your Age — Then Swaps the Product

As of yesterday, OpenAI is rolling out ChatGPT for Teens worldwide.[1] The child-safety headline is the less interesting part; the interesting part is in the fine print: the product doesn't switch because someone checks a box, but because a model estimates who's typing — „if we predict you're under 18, or you've told us so, this becomes your default experience".[1] That means a pattern has now gone mainstream that affects anyone embedding third-party AI: the vendor infers a trait from behavior and quietly flips on a different rulebook. Anyone rebuilding this in Europe has two jobs — the switch itself, and proof that it's accurate. For the second, OpenAI supplies no number today.

The Model Guesses Who You Are — That's the Real Story [1][2][3]

On Tuesday, 2026-08-18, OpenAI launched a dedicated ChatGPT version for ages 13 to 17.[3] It blocks conversations about suicide, self-harm, and romantic or sexual topics, and bars the model from claiming feelings or consciousness[3] — verbatim, it „shouldn't call itself a friend, suggest it has personal feelings, or suggest sentience".[1] Also included: study hours, a study mode, break reminders, a parental console. The rollout is worldwide and is set to finish „over the next two weeks"[1] — not the usual US-first pattern.

The part that goes beyond child safety is the assignment. By default, there's no ID check. OpenAI estimates: „age assurance to estimate if someone is under 18 based on factors such as their types of queries"[2], and anyone flagged that way lands in the Teen version automatically.[3] In product language: „if we predict you're under 18, or you've told us so, this becomes your default experience".[1] As a fallback to the estimate, OpenAI itself says it may ask for ID in some cases or countries.

Age assurance vs. age verification: With verification, you show an ID and your age is settled. With assurance, someone just listens to you talk and guesses — like a bartender deciding whether to card you. Verification costs data and friction but is fairly reliable afterward. Assurance costs nothing and is invisible — and sometimes wrong in both directions: a 19-year-old gets a stripped-down product with no explanation, a 15-year-old who types like an adult gets no protection.

The missing number. This is exactly where the reporting stops. None of the sources retrieved today states how accurate this estimate is — no hit rate, no margin of error. For comparison: specialized vendors do publish this. Yoti's facial-analysis estimate is reported at a mean error of 1.04 years for ages 14 to 18, Persona at 1.77 years for 13 to 17.[12] Whether a behavior-based estimate from chat history gets anywhere close is unknown — and for anyone selling or buying something like this, it's the only number that matters.

The second weak point is the parental console: it only kicks in if both the parent and the teen consent.[2] Clean from a privacy-law standpoint, toothless in practice — the households where a teenager is at risk are rarely the ones where both sides agree on a shared setting. What works without consent is the content blocking, and that, again, hinges on the estimate.

Typing behavior→Age estimate→Different rulebook→Different product behavior

My take. The child-safety debate is the backdrop; the mechanism underneath is what matters for business. A vendor infers a trait from usage traces and then changes what its model does — without the operator who built on top of it ever finding out. Anyone who's built a tutoring, school, club, or gaming app on ChatGPT now has to assume that some of their users silently get different model behavior than the rest. Not a vendor bug — a new property of the building blocks, and one that belongs in contracts and acceptance testing. And the pattern is replicable: that becomes Idea 1 below.

The Price Floor Sits at Twenty Cents [4][5][13]

Explicitly not news from today: the cut is dated 2026-07-30 and hasn't run in this brief before. It belongs here anyway because, together with recent issues, it adds up to a buying picture. Since then, GPT-5.6 Luna has cost $0.20 input / $1.20 output per million tokens instead of $1 / $6 — down 80%, about three weeks after the model's July 9 launch.[4] Terra fell 20% to $2 / $12; the flagship model Sol stays at $5 / $30.[4][5] Rationale: „efficiency gains across its models and serving systems"[4] — a phrase that leaves everything open.

The number gets interesting in comparison. Yesterday's brief covered Google's counteroffer: Gemini 3.7 Flash at $0.75 / $3.75, but only „through the end of the year", after which „$1.50/1M input tokens and $7.50/1M output tokens".[13] One vendor cuts permanently, the other lures with a limited-time offer that already has the doubling written into its own announcement text. For a deal spanning the turn of the year, that's a real difference.

My take. Three consequences, and none of them is "AI is getting cheap." First: the price war is happening at the bottom. The everyday tier drops 80%, the flagship tier doesn't move — whoever really needs Sol is negotiating with a vendor that has no reason to budge. Second: model cost is rarely still the bottleneck in Mittelstand projects. At $0.20 per million input tokens — roughly 700,000 words, eight paperbacks — the bill for a single task is smaller than the minute of clerical work it replaces; what jams is the integration. Third, the uncomfortable consequence: value is moving away from access. When end users get the best everyday model for free and developers buy it for pennies, "access to a model" stops being sellable. What stays sellable is everything around it: integration, acceptance, proof, operations.

What Applies in Europe for the Same Product [9][10][11]

Anyone rebuilding the Teen switch in Europe won't find the standard in the AI Act, but in the Digital Services Act. On 2025-07-14, the Commission published guidelines on Article 28(1) DSA — the obligation for online platforms to ensure a high level of privacy, safety, and security for minors.[9] First, the quality standard: age assurance should be „accurate, reliable, robust, non-intrusive, and non-discriminatory"; the guidelines distinguish age verification — for pornography, gambling, statutory minimum ages — from age estimation for the remaining cases.[9] What OpenAI launched yesterday falls into the second category. Second, the chatbot line: recommended measures include „removing persuasive design features aimed predominantly at engagement and putting safeguards around AI chatbots integrated into online platforms"; streaks, autoplay, and push notifications should be disabled by default.[9]

Guidelines aren't a regulation: A regulation says "you must"; a Commission guideline says "this is how we'd assess it." What's binding is Article 28 itself with its general wording — the guideline fills it in with examples. Follow it, and you have a very good argument in a dispute; build clearly against it, and you have a very bad one. It's the assessor's manual, not the law.

And the number that's been shaky in this brief for weeks is now settled. Article 99 has two fine tiers. Paragraph 3, for prohibited practices under Article 5: „up to 35 000 000 EUR … up to 7 % of its total worldwide annual turnover". Paragraph 4, for the remaining provider and deployer obligations including Article 50: „up to 15 000 000 EUR … up to 3 %".[11] That resolves the contradiction that still carried a warning label on 08/16: the circulating €35 million / 7% figures do not apply to the transparency obligation for chatbots — for that, the framework in force since 2026-08-02 is €15 million / 3%.[10] Generative systems already on the market still have until 2026-12-02 to comply with labeling.[10]

My take: anyone selling with fine figures should cite the right one. The difference between €35 million and €15 million is rhetoric in a sales pitch, but in an audit report it's the difference between correct and incorrect work.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Age & Context Switch

ELI5 & Value: A small layer sitting in front of the AI model that answers exactly one question: who's talking right now — and which rulebook applies as a result? Like a bouncer who doesn't run the evening, just decides who gets into which room. The value isn't the technology, it's auditability: a German vendor with a mixed audience — tutoring, textbooks, clubs, gaming — has to be able to prove that its chatbot behaves differently for minors. Without this layer, the answer is "our vendor handles that somehow," and that's not an answer in an audit conversation.
Prototype (V0): (1) A rulebook as a file, not as code — per context (adult / minor / unknown), the allowed topics, system instructions, and logging duties. (2) A classifier that determines the context from signals already available in the customer's system (account data, school affiliation, explicit statement) — without any custom age prediction in V0. (3) The switch call itself: context in, rule set and model endpoint out. (4) A tamper-evident log per request — the actual thing being sold. Deliberately not in the MVP: facial analysis, ID verification, a custom age estimate. Building blocks: an age-assurance vendor as an optional tier (Yoti, Persona, k-ID), open-source guardrails for content review.
Market & gap: The market splits into two halves that never touch. On one side, specialized age assurance — Yoti (facial analysis, mean error 1.04 years for 14–18), Persona (1.77 years for 13–17), k-ID with reusable age credentials on FIDO passkeys.[12] They sell a measurement, not a rulebook. On the other side, the model vendors, who ship the rulebook but as a black box: OpenAI has been switching it on itself since yesterday and publishes no accuracy rate.[1][2] In between sits the gap — the connective layer of context determination, rulebook, and logging, tailored to a German product; it's expected under the Article 28 guidelines (section above).[9] German youth media protection law sits on top as a second layer.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. The expensive measurement piece gets bought in or left out of V0 entirely; what gets built is the rulebook, the switch, and the log — V0 solo in four to six weeks. The maintenance burden sits in the rulebook, not the code, and it changes at the pace of regulation: one to two days per quarter per client. On-prem is the default — only the content review needs a model, and that runs on a small open model in-house. Scale via a contractor per rollout. Risk: if an age-assurance vendor eventually ships the rulebook itself, the gap shrinks — then it's the German fit that has to be defended, not the technology. digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu · biometricupdate.com · engadget.com

2 · The Escalation Console — the Parental Pattern, Built to Survive a Works Council

ELI5 & Value: Yesterday, OpenAI built a console where a second person gets limited visibility and gets notified on defined risk events — parents about teens.[1] The same pattern is missing in the workplace: if an employee hands the AI a customer list, a draft contract, or a health question, nobody notices. The value isn't control, it's a defined escalation path: on specific, predefined events — personal data, legal advice, a binding commitment — a named person gets a notification, and everything else is never even looked at. That's the difference between a works agreement that gets approved and a surveillance system that doesn't.
Prototype (V0): (1) An event catalog worked out with the client: five to eight triggers, each in one sentence. (2) A single tap point — the company's own AI access or browser profile — not the endpoint device. (3) Classification by a small model that reports only the triggers and discards the content; economical at $0.20 per million tokens, and nothing gets stored.[4] (4) A notification to a named person plus a monthly report with no personal data. (5) A draft works agreement included as a companion document. Deliberately not in the MVP: endpoint agents, network capture, a content archive, blocking functions.
Market & gap: The category is crowded — Microsoft Purview (DLP for unmanaged AI apps, strong in Microsoft 365 and Copilot environments), Nightfall AI (a browser extension for ChatGPT, Copilot, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity), Witness AI and Reco for visibility, Cyberhaven against data leaking into prompts; the AI-governance market is put at roughly $492 million for 2026.[14] Two things create the gap. First, the sizing: these are security platforms for organizations with a dedicated security lead; a 120-person Mittelstand firm doesn't have one. Second, the real lever, German labor law: as soon as a tool is objectively capable of monitoring behavior or performance, rolling it out becomes subject to co-determination under Section 87(1) No. 6 of the Works Constitution Act (BetrVG) — mere capability is enough, actual analysis isn't required.[15] That's exactly where US security tools fail in German workplaces: too powerful for a works council to wave through. A product that deliberately reports only triggers and discards content isn't a weaker tool — it's the only one that can actually be deployed.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium. The build itself is manageable — one tap point, one classifier, one notification path, one report; V0 solo in six to eight weeks. Honest about the load: unlike Idea 1, this is an operational product. It runs continuously, and when it goes quiet, someone has to know whether it's broken or the business is just calm — realistically, half a day per week per client for the first three, tapering off after that. What costs more than the code is the sales process: every rollout goes through both management and the works council, two conversations with opposing interests. Hence the deliberately small footprint — with five triggers and no content archive, the works-council conversation is winnable; with an archive, it isn't. Sovereignty variant: the classifier as an open model running in-house, so no prompt content leaves the network — not an add-on here, but the actual selling point. superblocks.com · nightfall.ai · bitkom.org

That's clean from a privacy standpoint and pretty toothless in practice, because the households where a teenager is actually at risk are rarely the ones where both sides agree to turn a setting on together.

Europe already has a benchmark for this, and it doesn't live in the AI Act — it's in the Digital Services Act. Back in July twenty twenty-five, the European Commission published guidance on Article twenty-eight, saying age assurance should be accurate, reliable, robust, non-intrusive, and non-discriminatory, and explicitly recommending safeguards around AI chatbots built into online platforms. Anyone rebuilding this switch in Europe already has the blueprint.

One number that's been shaky in this brief for weeks is now settled. The AI Act has two fine tiers. Thirty-five million euros, or seven percent of global turnover, applies to prohibited practices. Fifteen million, or three percent, applies to everything else, including the transparency requirement for chatbots. So anyone trying to scare a chatbot customer with the thirty-five-million figure is quoting the wrong number.

On pricing: since late July, OpenAI's everyday model, Luna, costs twenty cents per million input tokens instead of a dollar, an eighty percent cut. The flagship model, Sol, hasn't moved at all. So the price war is happening at the bottom of the lineup, not the top. For a mid-sized business customer, that means model costs are rarely the real bottleneck anymore; the bottleneck is integration. And going forward, the money isn't in selling access to a model, it's in everything built around it.

Both project ideas come straight out of this pattern. The first is an age-and-context switch: a small layer in front of the model that figures out who's talking, which rulebook applies, and logs all of it in a tamper-evident way. The second translates the parental pattern into the workplace: an escalation console that notifies one named person when five clearly defined events happen, and throws away everything else. It's built deliberately weak, because a more powerful tool would never get past a German works council.

Tomorrow's angle is probably the countdown to the end of the two-week window for the teen rollout, and whether OpenAI ever publishes a number on how accurate its age guess really is. Until then: a company that guesses who you are ought to say how often it's wrong.
