Brazil Runs Agents Like No Other Country — and Rolls Them Back the Most [1][2]

On 2026-08-17, a survey ran through the Brazilian press crediting the country with a top position: 76% of Brazilian companies run AI agents in production — against 62% globally and 67% in the US.[1] Read only that line, and it reads like a success story. The second number from the same survey sits right next to it, and it's the more interesting one: 80% of Brazilian organizations have already paused or unwound an AI deployment because governance had slipped out of their hands. 39% of those rollbacks started with a data breach or the leak of personal information.[1]

"Agent in production": An AI agent isn't a chat window — it's a program that gets a goal, works out its own intermediate steps, and operates real tools along the way: sending an email, changing a record, placing an order. "In production" means: not in a test system, but against the real customer base, with real consequences. The difference is the one between an intern who writes suggestions on a sticky note and one who types straight into the inventory system.
Methodology Vendor-funded study, self-reported, secondhand. The commissioning party is Sinch — a vendor that sells exactly the thing whose spread it's measuring (customer communication and the AI agents inside it). 2,527 executives were surveyed across ten countries and six industry sectors; the source available to me doesn't give a per-country sample size, and "in production" isn't defined — whether a single customer-service chat assistant already counts remains open. The figures are also executives' self-assessments, not a system count. And I'm not reading them from Sinch directly, but from coverage on a Brazilian IT-workers'-union news site.[1]

My take — and it flips the usual skepticism around. In a vendor study, the flattering numbers are the soft ones and the uncomfortable ones are the hard ones. The 76% costs respondents nothing: checking "yes, we're in production" makes you look modern, and it pleases the client. The 80% costs something — nobody likes going on record that they had to roll back a project, and for Sinch, that number isn't a selling point. That's exactly why I'd correct the adoption figure downward and take the rollback figure seriously. What it describes isn't a Brazilian problem, but a timing one: rollbacks show up first wherever deployment happened first.

Agent goes into production→Incident, a data breach in 39% of cases→Governance concerns→Stop or rollback

That the rollbacks aren't read as a rejection shows up in the third number: 66% of Brazilian respondents want to increase their AI investment by more than 25%, and Latin American organizations plan a jump of over 50% at a rate of 21% — well ahead of North America's 12%.[1] Translated, that means: nobody has decided it doesn't work, only that they started wrong. That's a very different posture from "pilot failed, topic closed" — and, for anyone guiding deployments, the friendlier one.

For someone serving German clients from Paraná, that's an unusually convenient position: you see the mistake first where you live, and sell the lesson where you bill.

The Standing Agent Has Become Subscription Merchandise [3][4][5][6]

Two stories from this month, the same movement. Google's Gemini Spark — an agent that, per reporting, runs „24/7, even if your phone and laptop are turned off" and checks back before bigger steps — launched in late May as an exclusive feature of the $99.99 AI Ultra tier.[4] Since 2026-07-23, it's also included in the $19.99 AI Pro tier; the wording in the report is unambiguous: „launched exclusively on the expensive $99.99-per-month AI Ultra plan, but they're now available to those paying $19.99 per month for the AI Pro tier".[3] A fifth of the price for the same feature, within two months.

Anthropic's Claude Cowork takes the same road from the other direction. The file-and-desktop agent launched for the priciest plan and has been „available on mobile and web for all paid plans" since 2026-08-18.[5] Anthropic's pricing page today lists Cowork under the $20 Pro plan, $17 a month with annual billing.[6] An important clarification versus earlier notes: the opening to the $20 plan is not the 2026-08-18 news — the news is the jump to mobile and browser. On the date the Pro plan first got Cowork, I have conflicting information; I'm leaving it open for that reason (see the Gaps box).

Standing agent: An assistant you don't talk to — you hire it. It gets a standing brief ("watch this mailbox," "keep this spreadsheet current") and keeps working while you sleep — with Spark, explicitly even with the device switched off, because it doesn't run on the phone, but in Google's data center.[4] The difference from a chatbot is the difference between a calculator and a night shift.

My take: the price isn't the story, the ratio is. A German AI agency's hourly rate in 2026 runs „zwischen 90 € und 180 €".[11] The agent itself costs, depending on the vendor, $17 to $20 a month. Which means: the runtime is now a rounding error in any project budget, and practically the entire cost of a deployment is the human who connects the agent to the real data, builds in the approvals, and documents afterward what it did. Anyone still selling "AI access" today is selling water from the office coffee machine. What gets paid is the last mile — and the last mile hasn't gotten cheaper with the price collapse, it's gotten relatively more expensive.

The second point affects Europe directly. At launch, Spark was explicitly not available in the European Economic Area, the UK, or Switzerland.[4] That was the state of reporting as of 2026-05-29; whether the expansion to the Pro tier changed that, I could not confirm — so the caveat sits in the Gaps box, not as today's fact. If it still holds, it's instructive anyway: for a German Mittelstand company, "then we'll just buy the twenty-dollar agent" isn't an option you try out on a Friday afternoon. The sovereignty reservation that's been debated in Germany for two years isn't a matter of stance here at all — it's simply a matter of availability, and the vendor answers that question, not the customer.

Scope note The coding side of Cowork — tools, terminal, repository access — belongs in the ai-dev brief's territory and is deliberately left out here. What counts here is only the office use case: an agent that handles files and processes, for twenty dollars a month.

Two Vertical Agents, Two Price Tags [7][8][9]

While the big vendors are making the general-purpose agent cheaper, two smaller ones are selling it as staff for a specific industry — and the price tags are more revealing than the products.

India, payroll. In mid-August, Kredily introduced the agent KAI in version 3.0, described as „India's first agentic AI payroll agent built directly into an HRMS", with 110+ skills across payroll, attendance, leave, master data, and HR processes.[7] The decisive clause isn't the number, it's the division of labor: KAI „can take an instruction, perform permitted actions, validate outcomes, and surface exceptions for human approval".[7] In other words: permitted actions on its own, exceptions escalated for approval. There's also a sales move worth remembering — a year of free access to the „Bharat Payroll OS Plan" for Indian startups, small businesses, and chartered-accountant firms, packaged as an Independence Day gesture.[7] On a base of 25,000+ businesses and over 1 million employees, that's not charity — it's a land grab.

Texas, gas-station convenience stores. Since 2026-08-14, Octane has been selling an "operating system" for convenience-store chains: per the product page, „a team of 10+ AI employees, each responsible for a different part of your operation: from pricing and inventory to invoices, fuel, cash, labor, and more", with final decisions staying with a human.[8] The price is the real story: „starts at $1/hr" per location, billed monthly, listed as „Early access rate · founding partners only".[8]

Price per operating hour instead of per seat: Classic business software costs per user, per month — you pay for access. An hourly price per location is a payment for presence: when the store is open, the clock runs. That's the billing shape of a shift, not a license — and that's exactly why the number gets compared to an hourly wage in the operator's head immediately, without the vendor ever having to say so.
Sourcing status Octane's primary source was unreachable. The press release (Businesswire, re-syndicated at wboc.com and einnews.com) returned a timeout, HTTP 429, and HTTP 403 respectively. Only the details from the product page are directly documented (price, role description, human approval).[8] The 12 pilot sites and the Houston location as of 2026-08-14 come from a search summary that quotes the release's text — consistent across multiple hits, but not individually reloaded.[9]

My take. Twelve pilot sites are nothing, and "ten AI employees" is the kind of anthropomorphizing you let a sales brochure get away with and never let a procurement department get away with. What I still think is the most important sentence of the week is the billing unit. $1 per operating hour puts the software next to a shift, not next to a license budget — and turns the purchase decision into arithmetic a store operator without an IT department can do in thirty seconds. Same pattern, different packaging, at Kredily: a free year for small businesses is a statement that the vendor considers the connection to the payroll data more valuable than the first twelve monthly invoices. Neither vendor is selling access. They're selling their way into a process — and will then be hard to get rid of. That's exactly the mirror image of the rollback rate from column one: an agent that's cleanly hooked into a single process can be undone; an "operating system" for the whole store cannot.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Recall Path — What the Agent Did, and How to Undo It

ELI5 & Value: Every customer can answer the question of how to turn the agent off. Almost none can answer the second one: what did it actually do over the last three weeks, and how do we get that back? The light switch turns the light off; it doesn't pour the milk back into the fridge once someone's already spilled it. The value is right there in the number from column one: 80% of Brazilian organizations had to stop or unwind an AI deployment, 39% of those after a data breach.[1] What's expensive isn't the day the agent runs, it's the day someone — management, a client, an auditor, a data-protection authority — wants to know which processes were affected and what got reversed. Whoever can answer that question in two hours instead of two weeks isn't saving compute time, they're saving a crisis. This is explicitly not the breakout drill from 2026-08-20: that measured how far an agent could get. This is about the reverse direction — back.
Prototype (V0): (1) One process, one agent — no inventory scan. (2) Every write-capable tool call goes into an append-only journal: which tool, which arguments, which business object (voucher, customer, or order number), which target system, who approved it, when. (3) For each of the typically five to ten write actions in this process, store the counteraction: booking → reversing entry, sent email → correction letter from a template, changed record → restore to the prior state. (4) A kill switch that does two things: stop new work and output the list of open and reversible processes — rehearsed once on a copy of the data, otherwise it doesn't count. (5) A one-page recall report a managing director can read without explanation. Deliberately out of scope: continuous monitoring, on-call duty, a second process, anything that evaluates models. Existing building blocks: the compensating-transaction (saga) pattern from distributed systems — forty years old, just never applied to agents; for storage, a self-hostable trace store like Langfuse (open source, runs in-house).[10]
Market & gap: The neighboring category is crowded and well-funded. A comparison from 2026-08-09 lines up Langfuse, LangSmith, Braintrust, Arize AX/Phoenix, MLflow, Weights & Biases Weave, Helicone, and Datadog LLM Observability side by side.[10] What these tools capture is described precisely there: „A trace contains nested spans: the user input, each retrieval call, each model invocation with its exact prompt and parameters, each tool execution, and the final output."[10] That's a debugging artifact for the team building the agent — it answers "why did the model do that," not "which invoices need to be reversed and who do we need to call." Between the tool call and the business object sits a mapping that nobody can ship as a product, because it looks different in every company — which is why a platform vendor won't build it. It's the last mile, and the last mile is manual work. Honest caveat: the fact that a comparison article doesn't cover rollback doesn't prove no one offers it; it shows that the category defines itself through observation, not restoration. Before pitching this, it needs a reality check against the actual target system.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. Narrowly scoped mandate, V0 solo in three to six weeks for one process, no ongoing operating load afterward — the journal runs in the client's stack, twiceD operates nothing. From the second client on, the journal schema, counteraction catalog, and report template are reusable; scaling happens via an outside developer per parallel mandate. Three honest catches: first, everything hinges on the target system's write interface — if the ERP has no reversal function through the API, the "counteraction" becomes an instruction for a human, not a line of code; that belongs in the proposal, not in the acceptance test. Second, this only works for a process with countable write actions — an agent with an open-ended toolbox is out of scope, and the client should be told that rather than have it attempted anyway. Third, this is neither a security audit nor legal advice; it's bookkeeping for machine actions. sindpd.org.br · marktechpost.com

2 · The Per-Process Agent Priced by the Operating Hour — Octane's Pricing Formula, One Industry, One Process

ELI5 & Value: Octane isn't selling gas-station convenience stores software, it's selling presence: „starts at $1/hr" per location.[8] The operator automatically compares that number to a shift, not a license line item — the purchase decision shrinks to mental arithmetic. The idea is to carry exactly this packaging over to a German multi-location operation, while building the opposite of an "operating system": one recurring, narrow process that runs identically at every location — matching delivery notes against invoices, generating a reorder proposal, checking the shift schedule against time tracking. The value for the client is a number they can verify themselves: minutes per process before and after, error rate before and after. The value for twiceD is a price that can be published.
Prototype (V0): (1) Find an operation with five to twenty locations and one truly identical process — the selection criterion matters more than the tech. (2) Build exactly one narrow agent for that process, not ten. (3) Adopt Kredily's and Octane's division of labor: permitted actions on its own, exceptions escalated for human approval.[7][8] (4) Four weeks of parallel operation alongside the existing workflow — exactly the precaution Octane is using to run its twelve pilot sites[9] — while measuring the two numbers. (5) Billing per location and operating hour, price public. Deliberately out of scope: anything that moves money, point-of-sale systems, hardware, and any second process before the twelfth client. Building blocks: a bought-in model via API (not a proprietary one), the client's existing document and ERP interfaces, and the journal from Idea 1 — the two interlock.
Market & gap: At the top sit platform land grabs: Octane with the full "operating system" made of ten roles across twelve pilot sites,[8][9] Kredily with a free year for Indian small businesses on a base of 25,000 companies.[7] Both require the customer to change its operating model. At the bottom, in the German SME market, sits the other extreme: agencies with hourly rates „zwischen 90 € und 180 €", project costs „zwischen 500 € und 25.000 € einmalig", and ongoing costs of „etwa 30 € bis 500 € pro Monat" — and, the most telling sentence in the source, „zwischen zwei Angeboten für ‚dasselbe' Projekt können 15.000 € liegen".[11] A spread that size means the buyer can't compare quotes, because nobody describes the same object. The gap sits exactly in between — one process, one published price per location and operating hour, one measured before/after. It's too small for the platforms, too fixed for the agencies, because a public price makes their own spread visible. Caveat: the price figures come from a German AI consultant's blog — from the market itself, with no survey behind them; they document the order of magnitude and the spread, not market statistics.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium — and that's deliberately not "high." V0 solo in eight to ten weeks. Unlike Idea 1, this creates real operating load: the thing runs during the client's business hours, realistically three to five hours a week per client in the first six months, trending down. It's only viable under one hard condition — the process has to stay the same across clients. The moment client number two wants "almost the same, just different," it destroys the hourly-price math, because the product turns back into a project. Hence a pre-set abort criterion: less than four-fifths identical workflow after two clients — hourly price out, project price back in. Sovereignty variant: where documents contain personal data, the model runs in the EU or on-premises; that's not an add-on option here but a precondition, because "then we'll just use the American twenty-dollar agent" isn't actually an option for an EEA client, per the state of reporting.[4] Explicitly out of scope: on-call duty outside business hours and liability for the process itself — the client retains approval authority, twiceD operates the tool. getoctane.ai · outlookbusiness.com · sven-jagata.de

It's Friday, August twenty-first. Not much happened today itself — there's no solid news from the last twenty-four hours to carry this brief on its own. But the week behind it has a very clear shape, and it's a pricing story with two sides.

Side one: the entry price for an autonomous AI agent dropped to consumer level this month. Google's Gemini Spark launched in late May as an exclusive feature on a ninety-nine dollar and ninety-nine cent tier. Since July twenty-fourth, that same agent has also been included in the nineteen ninety-nine tier — a fifth of the price, in under two months. Anthropic's Claude Cowork is making the same move from the other direction: since August eighteenth, the file-handling agent runs on mobile and in the browser for every paid plan, and the pricing page today lists it starting at twenty dollars a month.

Side two, and this is the real finding: the exit price didn't fall along with it. A survey out of Brazil made the rounds in the press on Monday. Seventy-six percent of Brazilian companies say they're running AI agents in production — compared with sixty-two percent worldwide and sixty-seven percent in the United States. But the same survey also found that eighty percent of Brazilian organizations have had to pause or roll back an AI rollout. And in thirty-nine percent of those rollbacks, the trigger was a data breach.

There's a serious caveat on methodology here. The survey was commissioned by Sinch, a vendor that sells exactly the thing it's measuring the spread of. It polled twenty-five hundred and twenty-seven executives across ten countries, the numbers are self-reported, and the source never defines what counts as being in production. Even so, my read flips the usual skepticism around. In a vendor-funded study, the flattering numbers are the soft ones and the uncomfortable ones are the hard ones. Seventy-six percent adoption costs a respondent nothing — it sounds modern. Eighty percent forced to roll back costs something: nobody likes admitting that on the record, and it's not a number the sponsor wanted. So I'd trust the rollback figure more than the adoption figure. And the fact that sixty-six percent still plan to invest more tells you something too: nobody's concluded that AI doesn't work — just that they started wrong.

There is a German comparison number, but it isn't really one. The Deutscher Mittelstands-Bund's AI index puts agent-system adoption at sixteen point six percent. That survey was fielded back in November of twenty twenty-five, asked around seven hundred Mittelstand companies, and came from a completely different sponsor. Nine months and two very different populations sit between the two numbers. What the comparison actually supports is a sequence, not a ranking: wherever adoption happens earlier, rollback happens earlier too. That work is happening in Brazil right now — and it'll hit Germany in a year or two.

Third story: two smaller vendors that aren't cutting the agent's price — they're selling it as staff for one specific industry. In India, Kredily introduced a payroll agent in mid-August that covers more than a hundred and ten skills across payroll, attendance, and leave, and kicks exceptions up for a human to approve. It's throwing in a free year for small Indian businesses too — on a base of twenty-five thousand companies, that's not charity, that's a land grab. And in Houston, a company called Octane is selling an operating system for gas-station convenience stores, starting at one dollar an hour per location.

Twelve pilot locations is nothing, and calling it ten AI employees is marketing talk. But I think the billing unit is the most important line of the week. One dollar per operating hour puts the software next to a shift, not a license fee — which turns the buying decision into math a store manager without an IT department can do in thirty seconds. One caveat: Octane's own press release wasn't reachable. The price and the role description come from the product page; the pilot count comes from a search summary.

If there's one number to take away from all this, it's a ratio. A German AI agency charges somewhere between ninety and a hundred and eighty euros an hour. The agent itself costs seventeen to twenty dollars a month. So the software license is now a rounding error in any project budget — what actually gets paid for is the person who connects the agent to real data, builds in approvals, and can prove afterward what it did. Anyone still selling plain AI access today is selling water from the office coffee machine.

Two project ideas came out of that. The first is a recall path: an append-only journal of every write action an agent takes, a stored counteraction for each one, a rehearsed kill switch, and a one-page report — not how far the agent could go, but how you get back. The second borrows Octane's pricing formula: one recurring process, identical at every location, billed per site and per operating hour, with a public price — aimed at a German market where two quotes for the "same" project can differ by fifteen thousand euros. I'd call the first idea highly feasible solo; the second only medium, because it comes with real ongoing workload.
