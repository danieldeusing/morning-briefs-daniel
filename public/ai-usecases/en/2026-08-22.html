A Model Designs Proteins, an Outside Lab Verifies [1][2]

On 2026-08-18, Anthropic published a result that's rare in this category: an AI claim with a measurement behind it that someone else performed. Two model checkpoints — Mythos Preview and Opus 4.8 — were given 15 target proteins and publicly available tools, and designed binder proteins against them. The designs went to two external evaluators: „Our external evaluators, Adaptyv Bio and Twist Bioscience, independently produced and tested Claude's designs in the lab."[1] The tally: „By the end of this effort, we produced 354 binders against 14 of 15 targets using a total of 1,320 designs."[1]

Protein binder, designed de novo: A protein is a folded molecule with a specific surface shape. A "binder" is a second, small protein that docks onto exactly one spot on that surface — like a sticker that only sticks to a single tile in the bathroom and falls off everywhere else. "De novo" means: not copied from nature, but invented from scratch on a computer. Why it matters: almost every modern drug and almost every lab test works this way — something has to hit one specific target and nothing else. Hit rate is then the share of designs that actually stick in a real test tube.

The hit rates are the real news here. In a 48-hour session against all targets at once: „Mythos Preview and Opus 4.8 achieve overall hit rates—how many of the designs are, in fact, binders—of 26.7% and 22.6%, respectively"; when Mythos Preview tackles each target individually, it's 35.1%.[1] As a benchmark, the same page states: „10 to 15% is typical in protein design campaigns today."[1] Even the weakest run measured here comes in above the upper end of what's typical.

Wet-lab hit rate — share of designs that actually bind · Figures: Anthropic, 2026-08-18

Adaptyv Bio followed up with its own account on 2026-08-19, and it matters more for the assessment than any press release. The measurement used a standard assay — „We ran our Affinity Characterization assay, to measure the binding strength on SPR using 5 target concentrations." — and, critically: „Then, designs against all these targets were sent to our wet lab, in an anonymized way. We did not have any information on which Claude model designed which protein."[2] Of 1,320 designs, 354 bound, „giving Claude an average hit rate of 26.8%".[2]

SPR, and what "33.4 nM" means: SPR measures how tightly two molecules stick together by fixing the target onto a chip and flowing the candidate over it. The result is a concentration: the amount of candidate needed for half the target sites to be occupied. Smaller is stronger. Going from 1.7 µM to 33.4 nM means you need roughly fifty times less substance for the same effect — the Velcro grips a lot better.

The individual targets show both strength and spread. For TREM2, Adaptyv reports „an 80% hit rate". For 15-PGDH, the best design clearly beats the winner of the earlier public competition, „going from 1.7 uM to 33.4 nM"; for RBX1, „from 25.7 nM in the competition to 3.9 nM". And then: „One target (MBP) yielded no binders."[2] One target out of fifteen produced nothing — the fact that this line appears in both publications is why I believe the rest.

Model designs 1,320 candidates→Designs sent anonymized to outside lab→Synthesis + SPR measurement→354 bind, 1 target fails
Methodology The commissioning party is interested — the measurement isn't. Anthropic is publishing a result about Anthropic, which is exactly why the numbers would be worthless without the outside measurement. Four caveats remain even with it: (1) According to Adaptyv, the targets come from „our previous public competitions"[2] — a field with publicly known solutions and best scores; a genuinely new target is a different matter. (2) What was blinded was the model→design mapping, not a comparison against a human team working the same targets in parallel — no such comparison exists. (3) Anthropic states „10 to 15%" as the industry benchmark without a source on the page; I have not independently verified that figure. (4) A binder is not a drug: binding in a test tube says nothing about efficacy, tolerability, or manufacturability in humans.

My take. The methodologically most interesting sentence of the week isn't "14 of 15" — it's "we didn't know which model made which design." That's not a biology detail, it's a procedure — one that transfers to any industry where someone is currently claiming their AI does something better. The rest of the week consists of numbers nobody verified independently. What follows from that for twiceD is below, in Idea 2: the blinding is the product.

The Underrated Half: Raw Data Without Vendor Software [1][3]

Next to the protein story, the same page holds a second experiment that sounds less spectacular and is the bigger news for the Mittelstand. A contract lab had run a routine quality-control sample; Anthropic gave the model nothing but the raw files and a short plain-language instruction — „with no vendor software and no operator" — and got back NMR and LC-MS results in 23 and 19 minutes.[1] Checked against the lab's own analysis: „hydrogen counts per peak were within 0.08 ¹H of the lab's, and its purity was measured at 96.4% versus the 96.33% of the lab."[1]

NMR and LC-MS: Two standard instruments in any analytical lab. NMR puts the sample in a strong magnetic field and reads how the atomic nuclei signal back — from that you reconstruct how the molecule is built. LC-MS first splits a mixture into its components, then weighs each one individually. Neither instrument spits out an answer — it spits out a raw file: a measurement curve that still has to be interpreted. That interpretation is, today, the job of a trained person using licensed specialist software.

And that's exactly where the business model this story touches sits. The usual route is a chain of vendor programs: Bruker describes, for its own world, acquisition running through TopSpin or IconNMR, after which „Mnova takes over for processing, analysis, and reports"; automation runs through „scripting tools, as well as reporting- and processing templates".[3] That's not manual labor — it's licensed workstation software with maintained templates. If a general-purpose model can get within 0.07 percentage points of the lab result from the same raw file without that chain, what shifts isn't the science — it's the purchasing decision.

My take. The protein headline gets the attention, but it's out of reach for a German Mittelstand company — by design: „protein design and other dual-use research biology capabilities remain unavailable for general access in Claude Fable 5."[1] The lab-data experiment, by contrast, doesn't use a locked-down capability — it uses one everyone already has. The pattern transfers to any company where a measuring instrument produces a raw file that only one manufacturer's program can read — materials testing, calibration labs, manufacturing quality assurance. That's the use case. Not the drug.

Dual use: Knowledge where the same set of capabilities is useful for both benefit and harm. A model that can design a protein to dock precisely onto one target molecule can, in principle, apply that same ability in the opposite direction. That's why this particular function isn't unlocked for general access, while reading measurement data is.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Raw-Data Report Agent for Testing Labs — No Vendor Software, On-Premises

ELI5 & Value: A measuring instrument in a lab doesn't deliver an answer — it delivers a raw file, a curve that still needs interpreting. Today, a trained person opens a licensed program from the instrument maker and, at the end, exports a test report. Anthropic's side experiment shows what a general-purpose model can do with the same raw files: results in 23 and 19 minutes, purity 96.4% against the lab's own 96.33%, explicitly „with no vendor software and no operator".[1] The value to the customer isn't science — it's turnaround time: a test report that costs half an analyst's workday is the bottleneck where orders pile up.
Prototype (V0): (1) One instrument type, one routine test sample, one customer — no lab-wide rollout. (2) The customer hands over a batch of archived raw files for which their own approved reports already exist; those are the ground truth to measure against. (3) The run is a model with tool access to the raw file and the customer's report template — not a new development of an analysis algorithm. (4) Every run produces a result plus the deviation from the old report — the outcome of the trial is an error distribution, not a demo. (5) Approval stays with a human: the agent fills in the report, it doesn't sign it. Deliberately out of scope: instrument control, the measurement itself, a second sample type before the second customer, and any GxP-regulated lab.
Market & Gap: The existing chain is named and documented: acquisition via TopSpin or IconNMR, then „Mnova takes over for processing, analysis, and reports," automation via „scripting tools, as well as reporting- and processing templates".[3] Important for the honesty of this idea: the incumbent is not manual — it already automates, but through maintained templates and scripts, i.e. through specialist work at a licensed workstation. The gap, then, isn't "automation" — it's who can operate it: a path from raw file to report that a lab manager can kick off in plain language, that runs on the customer's own premises, and that's priced per report instead of per workstation. The proof that a general-purpose model can be lab-accurate at this step is four days old and comes from the model vendor itself[1] — that's a strong sales argument, and also the reason a counter-check against the customer's own historical data belongs before every proposal.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium — and deliberately not "high." In favor of building it: the hard part (interpreting the measurement data) is bought in; twiceD builds the integration, the comparison check, and the reporting path; V0 solo in six to eight weeks, operated in the customer's own stack, so no on-call duty for twiceD. Three things argue the other way, and belong in the proposal, not the acceptance test: first, you need a customer willing to hand over historical data with results — without that, the whole thing is a demo. Second, analytical chemistry is a specialty Daniel doesn't have; technical sign-off has to sit with the customer, in writing. Third, any GxP-regulated lab is out of scope — GxP means: in pharma and medtech, the software used must be formally validated and every change documented, or the result is worthless to regulators. Target customers are therefore non-regulated testing, calibration, and materials labs, plus manufacturing quality assurance. Sovereignty variant: an open-weight model run on-premises, for raw data that carries customer secrets. anthropic.com · bruker.com

2 · The Blind Counter-Check — Proof of Effectiveness for AI Claims, Bought by the Buyer

ELI5 & Value: Every AI number a Mittelstand company gets handed this year comes from someone who benefits from it looking good — the vendor, the systems integrator, the consultancy that also sells the platform. What makes the difference this week is a procedure: the designs went into the outside lab anonymized, and there, „We did not have any information on which Claude model designed which protein."[2] That's exactly what can be sold as a standalone service to the buyer's side: twiceD designs and runs the blind counter-check before the customer extends a pilot. The value is an avoided bad decision — and the number in the report belongs to the customer afterward, not to the vendor.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pin down exactly one decision the AI is meant to improve — quote review, complaint triage, reorder suggestions — along with the metric it's measured against. (2) Pull 100 to 200 closed cases with known outcomes from the customer's own records. (3) Strip every trace of origin from both the AI-path and the prior-path results and mix them; the mapping stays sealed with twiceD. (4) Two of the customer's own experts assess them blind, and the disagreement between them gets reported too — reviewer disagreement is a finding, not an operational hiccup. (5) A two-page report: hit rate before/after, where the AI path was worse, and what that means in hours and euros. The model for the tone is the line „One target (MBP) yielded no binders"[2] — the zero stays in, or nobody believes the rest. Deliberately out of scope: live operation, model selection, implementation.
Market & Gap: Tools for evaluating AI systems are plentiful, but they sit on the build side and answer "why did the model do that" (in ai-dev territory, covered here on 08/21). On the buyer's side, by contrast, stands: vendor indices like Salesforce's, fed by self-reporting and the vendor's own platform telemetry,[5] consultancies that sell the very services whose adoption they measure,[6] and, since 08/13, a consulting practice certifying tens of thousands of consultants on a single vendor's tools.[4] Nowhere in that chain is anyone paid for the result being allowed to come out negative. That's exactly the gap — independence is the product here, not a quality feature. Honest caveat: that I'm not aware of a German provider landscape for blind AI-acceptance testing doesn't prove none exists; a market counter-check belongs before the first proposal, and auditing firms and TÜV-adjacent houses are the obvious competitors once the topic gets big enough.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. It's expert-assessment work with a clear start and end: two to three weeks per engagement, no ongoing operational load, no on-call duty, no hosting. The reusability sits in the procedure — case-sampling protocol, anonymization step, scoring sheet, report template — not in code, and scaling means one outside subject-matter expert per industry, not a team. One hard rule belongs to it and is also the sales pitch: twiceD does not evaluate what twiceD built. Where the two overlap, it gets disclosed or the engagement is declined — otherwise the product breaks the moment the first customer notices. Pricing is a flat fee per engagement, explicitly independent of the outcome. adaptyvbio.com · futurumgroup.com · newsroom.ibm.com

It's Saturday, August twenty-second. There was no solid first report on applied artificial intelligence today itself, but the week behind it has an unusually clear takeaway. This week contains exactly one number that was checked by someone who had nothing to gain from it looking good.

On Tuesday, Anthropic published a result from protein research. Two model checkpoints were given fifteen target proteins and asked to design so-called binders against them — small proteins that dock onto exactly one spot on a larger one, like a sticker that only sticks to a single tile. In total, they produced one thousand three hundred twenty designs. Of those, three hundred fifty-four actually bound their target, across fourteen of the fifteen targets.

The hit rates are the real story. In a forty-eight-hour session against all targets at once, they came in at twenty-six point seven and twenty-two point six percent. When each target was tackled separately, it was thirty-five point one percent. As a benchmark, Anthropic cites ten to fifteen percent as typical for campaigns like this today. So even the weakest run measured here beats the high end of what's normal.

But what matters isn't the number, it's who did the measuring. The designs went out to two outside labs, Adaptyv Bio and Twist Bioscience. In its own write-up on Wednesday, Adaptyv says the designs arrived anonymized and that it had no information on which model designed which protein. The measurement used a standard method called SPR, which determines binding strength. And the failure is in there too: one target, MBP, produced zero binders. That line is exactly why I believe the rest.

The caveat still stands, though. The party footing the bill has a stake in the outcome, the targets come from the same lab's earlier public competitions, there was no human team working the same targets in parallel for comparison, and a binder in a test tube is nowhere near a drug. One more thing I noticed: Adaptyv talks about sixteen targets, Anthropic about fifteen. I couldn't resolve the discrepancy, so I'm using fifteen throughout.

The half of the announcement that matters more for small and mid-sized businesses is further down the same page. A contract lab had run a routine quality-control sample. The model got nothing but the raw files from the measuring instruments and a short plain-language instruction — explicitly with no vendor software and no operator. It delivered the analyses in twenty-three and nineteen minutes. It measured purity at ninety-six point four percent; the lab itself had measured ninety-six point three three.

Here's why that matters: the usual route in a lab is a chain of vendor programs — one piece of software for acquisition, another for processing, analysis, and reporting, automated through maintained templates and scripts on a licensed workstation. If a general-purpose model can get within seven hundredths of a percentage point of the lab's own result from the same raw file, without that chain, what shifts isn't the science — it's the purchasing decision. The protein capability itself, by the way, isn't for sale at all — Anthropic says protein design and related dual-use capabilities remain off-limits for general access. Reading measurement data, on the other hand, very much is.

Everything else this week is a different kind of story. About two weeks ago, Salesforce reported that companies had nearly tripled the number of active agents and cut build time by fifty-three percent. That's based on a survey of four thousand six hundred eighty-nine people in May plus usage data from Salesforce's own platform, and the Salesforce page itself was unreachable. KPMG counts a hundred percent adoption rate for Brazil, which is more a question of definition than a finding; the more interesting number right next to it is forty-five percent running disconnected one-off projects. For the German Mittelstand, the latest agent figure sits at sixteen point six percent, collected in November twenty twenty-five. And nine days ago, IBM announced it would certify tens of thousands of consultants on OpenAI's tools.

That's where the two project ideas come from. The first is a raw-data report agent for non-regulated testing and calibration labs: one instrument type, one test sample, measured against the customer's own approved past reports, with a human keeping final sign-off. Feasibility, honestly, is medium, because it needs a customer willing to hand over historical data along with results. The second idea is the blind counter-check as a standalone service, sold to the buyer's side: a hundred to two hundred closed cases, results mixed with all trace of origin stripped, two of the customer's own experts scoring them blind, a two-page report covering the cases where the AI did worse. Across the whole chain of vendors, systems integrators, and consultancies, nobody gets paid for letting a result come out negative. Independence is the product here.

Two things I'm watching for in the coming days. First, whether the protein result gets an independent follow-up check that Anthropic isn't paying for — that would be the real confirmation. And second, whether the lab-data experiment turns into an actual product, because that's the version that would actually land with a German Mittelstand company.
