The Funnel: From 88 to 12 [1][2][3]

The adoption number is undisputed and quoted everywhere: Stanford's AI Index 2026 records for the year 2025 — "Organizational adoption reached 88%", plus "Generative AI is used by 70% of organizations in at least one business function".[1] Anyone who reads only that sentence treats AI in the enterprise as a solved problem. That's exactly where the usual quoting stops.

The second number comes from PwC, from a survey of 4,454 CEOs in 95 countries, published in January 2026 at the World Economic Forum. It doesn't ask about deployment, but about money: "Only one-in-eight (12%) CEOs say AI has delivered both cost and revenue benefits. Overall, 33% report gains in either cost or revenue, while 56% say they have seen no significant financial benefit to date."[2] One in eight gets both. More than half, by their own account, get nothing measurable.

The third number comes from IBM and measures something else again — not companies, but initiatives: "only 25% of AI initiatives have delivered expected ROI over the last few years", and "only 16% have scaled enterprise wide".[3] The survey is from May 2025, fifteen months old; it stands here as a baseline marker, not as the current state.

"Expected ROI" — what's actually being measured: ROI simply means what comes out compared with what went in. The catch sits in the word expected. What's measured isn't "did it help," but "did it help exactly as much as the proposal said it would." Picture a kitchen renovation you budgeted at €20,000 and promised would cut cooking time in half: if the final bill comes to €24,000 and you save ten minutes, that wasn't a failure — the plan was wrong. So the 25% says less about AI than it does about the promises AI projects get approved on.
From adoption to return — each step a different survey and population · Stanford HAI (year 2025) · PwC (Jan. 2026, 4,454 CEOs) · IBM (May 2025, 2,000 CEOs)
Methodology This isn't a funnel from a single measurement — it's three photos from three cameras. (1) The populations differ: Stanford counts organizations, PwC surveys CEOs, IBM evaluates individual initiatives. A percentage point in one series isn't the same as one in another. (2) The field periods don't line up: IBM spring 2025, PwC late 2025 for Davos in January 2026, Stanford describes the year 2025. (3) All three are self-reported. A CEO who checks "no benefit" is also saying something about their own house — the direction of that bias is unclear, but it exists. (4) PwC and IBM both sell AI consulting. That they publish the uncomfortable number anyway makes them more credible, not neutral. (5) The bar chart is a reading aid for the narrowing, not a cohort tracking: the 12% isn't a subset of the 88% that was tracked through.

My take. The interesting number is neither 88 nor 12, but the 16% scaled enterprise-wide. It explains the other two. A pilot in one department costs little, hurts no one, and shows up in no profit-and-loss statement — that's how 88% gets there. A benefit a CEO recognizes as revenue or cost only shows up once a process has been fully converted, exceptions included, vacation coverage included, and the question of liability included. That step is precisely what doesn't get ordered along with the purchase. A vendor who sells piloting sells into a market that's 88% saturated. A vendor who sells finishing sells into one that's 16%.

Palantir: The Counterexample Sits on the Seller's Side [4]

Age The numbers are from 2026-08-03, twenty days old. They're not here as day-of news, but as the one named case against which the 56% from the section above can be tested.

If more than half of CEOs see no financial benefit, there ought to be a vendor somewhere with the opposite showing on its books. Palantir reported for the second quarter of 2026: "Revenue grew 93% year-over-year and 19% quarter-over-quarter to $1.935 billion", US commercial revenue +149% to $764 million, US government revenue +90% to $809 million. Full-year guidance was raised — "We are raising our revenue guidance to between $8.150 – $8.158 billion", corresponding to 82% growth, with US commercial revenue at "in excess of $3.424 billion, representing a growth rate of at least 134%".[4]

"Rule of 40" — why 155% is an unusual number: A rule of thumb from the software business: add growth rate and profit margin; if it comes out above 40, a company counts as healthy. Behind it is a trade-off — you get to grow fast or be profitable, rarely both at once. Palantir's chief executive put the quarter's "our Rule of 40 score climbed to 155%".[4] That's nearly four times the bar. For you as a reader, that means just one thing: here is demand that doesn't need subsidizing.

My take — and it's uncomfortable for both camps. Setting the 56% against Palantir makes a mistake I've read multiple times this week: the vendor's revenue isn't the buyer's ROI. Every dollar of that $1.935 billion sits in someone else's cost line. Both numbers can be completely true at once: spending is climbing steeply, and for the majority the benefit hasn't yet shown up on the profit-and-loss statement. That's exactly what the early phase of a transition looks like — and it's exactly what a bubble looks like too. Right now that can't be told apart by the revenue curve, only by whether the 16% rises within twelve months. That's my benchmark, not the forecast.

Ryanair Brings Google on Board — and Names Not a Single Saving [5]

On 2026-08-12, Ryanair and Google Cloud announced a five-year partnership for data and AI. Specifically: "Ryanair will deploy Google Workspace and Google Cloud services to 35,000 employees", plus Gemini Enterprise; for fleet operations and maintenance planning, DeepMind models are set to be used — "Ryanair plans to utilise Google DeepMind models, including AlphaEvolve and WeatherNext".[5] It's framed against the goal of 300 million passengers by 2034; Google arrives as a second cloud vendor alongside the existing AWS foundation.[5]

Two cloud vendors instead of one: A "hyperscaler" is simply a very large data-center landlord — Amazon, Microsoft, Google. Renting everything from one gets you better prices and less friction, but leaves you completely dependent on its availability, its price list, and its model lineup. A second vendor is like a second supplier for a component: pricier to buy, but you don't grind to a halt when one fails or raises prices. This exact consideration has just become relevant again for AI models — see the market-restriction power in the next section.

My take. This is an exemplary-described announcement and a textbook case of how the 56% gets made. Described: scope (35,000 employees), term (five years), use areas (crew scheduling, fleet operations, maintenance), and the models deployed, by name. Not described: any figure someone could check back against in two years — no contract value, no expected saving per flight, no baseline. In eighteen months, some CEO will be asked whether AI drove revenue or cut costs — and if nobody defined beforehand what that would even look like, "no significant benefit" is the only honest possible answer. The number isn't missing because the benefit is missing. It's missing because it was never defined.

40% Agents by New Year's Eve — Against an Office That Can Pull Models From the Market [6][7]

Gartner forecast in August 2025 that enterprise applications are entering a new phase of automation: "Gartner forecasting that 40 percent of them will include task-specific AI agents by 2026 – up from less than 5 percent today."[7] From under five to forty percent in roughly sixteen months — that's a very steep claim, and it has one pleasant feature: it expires on 2026-12-31 and becomes checkable then.

"Task-specific agent" — not the chat window: A chatbot answers. An agent does something: it can choose its own steps and call real system functions along the way — create an invoice, reschedule an appointment, triage a complaint. "Task-specific" means it can handle exactly one narrow process, not everything. Think of the difference between an information desk and a caseworker with a stamp. What matters for you: these agents mostly arrive built into software you've already bought — you don't choose them, you get them with the next update.

At the same time, something shifted on the other side on 2026-08-02. Enforcement powers under the EU AI Act became active for general-purpose AI models: the AI Office can request technical documentation, run its own model evaluations, demand corrective steps, and restrict market deployment; the fine range is "up to €15 million or 3% of their worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher".[6] Two related dates: the rules for high-risk systems were pushed back to 2027-12-02 via the "AI Omnibus," while a ban on systems that generate non-consensual sexually explicit content or abuse material takes effect from 2026-12-02.[6]

"General-purpose AI model" (GPAI): This means the foundation model itself — GPT, Gemini, Claude, Llama — not the application you build with it. The obligations first fall on whoever provides the model. Picture it this way: the engine manufacturer has to prove certification, not the trucking company driving the truck. For an SME, that means: you're usually not the addressee here — but your engine can be taken offline.

My take. I think the common reading — "regulation slows the agent wave" — is wrong, in both directions. Enforcement targets model vendors, not the machine-tool maker in Swabia who switches on an agent in his ticketing system. But what does hit him is something almost nobody says out loud: market restriction is a supply risk. If 40% of applications get agents built in, and each of those agents runs behind the scenes on one of five foundation models, then the question "what happens to my process if this model gets restricted in the EU or withdrawn by its provider?" stops being a legal question and becomes an operational one. The best commentary on the Act this month wasn't in a legal text — it was in an airline press release: Ryanair brought a second vendor into the house. That exact reasoning is where idea 1 below comes from.

Number check The "80% of new enterprise applications" figure doesn't exist. A claim is circulating widely that roughly four-fifths of new enterprise applications now come with a built-in AI agent, attributed to Gartner. Research turned up only two SEO pages citing "Gartner" with no report title, no date, and no link — and elsewhere in the same text, each one only supports the documented 40% forecast. This looks like an inflated retelling of the same forecast. It is not used here, not even "with a caveat." Incidentally: even the documented 40% figure itself is only citable via a trade outlet's verbatim reporting — gartner.com returned HTTP 403 on direct retrieval.[7]

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · AI Dependency Register with a Switchover Plan — the Bill of Materials Nobody Has

ELI5 & value: An SME ends up "doing" AI at some point without ever deciding to: the ticketing system gets a summarization feature, the ERP gets a booking suggestion, the inbox gets an auto-reply — all delivered with an update. That's exactly what Gartner forecasts for 40% of enterprise applications by year's end, coming from under five percent.[7] Nobody in the building still knows which process depends on which foundation model from which vendor. The product is the missing bill of materials: a list of every built-in AI feature, with vendor, foundation model, and data path behind each one — and for every business-critical process, a page titled "what do we do if this is gone tomorrow." The value is avoided downtime, not compliance: since 08/02, the EU AI Office can restrict a model's market availability,[6] and vendors retire model versions on their own, with no regulator involved at all.
Prototype (V0): (1) Inventory from three sources every client has — software contracts including sub-processor lists, the admin interfaces of the systems in use, and a walkthrough with the departments (that's where shadow usage lives). (2) Trace every feature found back to its model vendor; the vendor's sub-processor list is the most honest document for this, because it has to be contractually accurate. (3) Two scores per process: how critical it is, and how long operations survive without it. (4) For the critical ones only: a one-page switchover plan — manual fallback, second vendor, or a knowingly accepted risk with a signature. (5) Delivered as a table plus a ten-page report, with an annual refresh as a maintenance contract. Deliberately out of scope: ongoing monitoring, tool operations, legal advice on the Act.
Market & gap: There's a whole market for "AI governance," and it's well populated: Credo AI and Holistic AI as policy-centric platforms with ready-made EU AI Act packages, OneTrust and ServiceNow AI Control Tower as extensions of existing GRC systems, IBM watsonx.governance with very broad rule-set coverage.[9] They have two things in common. First, they're platforms — software someone has to roll out, populate, and maintain; with 16% of AI scaled enterprise-wide,[3] an SME has neither the occasion nor the staff for that. Second, they answer the operator's question ("is our AI use documented as compliant") rather than the buyer's question ("what grinds to a halt if this vendor fails"). So the gap isn't a tool, it's a deliverable: a one-time, paid assessment with a table and an emergency plan, without the client running yet another platform. The out-of-industry model appears in a press release this very month: Ryanair brings a second cloud vendor into the house alongside AWS.[5] What a large corporation does strategically, an SME needs as a single sheet of paper.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. This is assessment work with a clear start and end — an estimated two to three weeks per engagement, no on-call duty, no hosting, no operational load. The reusable part is the process, not code: an inventory questionnaire, a vendor → foundation-model mapping, a scoring grid, a report template. Scaling means one external assessor per industry, not a team. Honest limitations: first, a register like this ages fast — so the annual refresh belongs in the offer, not in goodwill. Second, the line to legal advice has to be drawn sharply; twiceD establishes what depends on what, and doesn't interpret the Act. Third, quality depends on how honestly vendors list their sub-processors — where that list is thin, the report says "cannot be determined," not a guess. Sovereignty variant: where a process wouldn't survive a restriction, an open model run in-house is the fallback — one that should be tested once beforehand. uctoday.com · helpnetsecurity.com · secureprivacy.ai · googlecloudpresscorner.com

2 · Legacy-System Agent Bridge — the 60% Nobody Builds For

ELI5 & value: The Gartner forecast has a second half nobody talks about. If 40% of enterprise applications have a task-specific agent built in by year's end,[7] those are the big vendors' products — and the remaining 60% includes, among others, exactly the systems where the SME actually makes its money: the homegrown order-management system, the industry software from 2011, the tool that exists only in this one company. No suite vendor will ever build an agent for those, because it's not worth it for them. The value for the client is equal treatment for their most important system: the same convenience they get handed free in a purchased ticketing system, also in the place where their distinctiveness lives.
Prototype (V0): (1) One process, chosen by frequency times processing time, not enthusiasm — typically: create an order, triage a complaint, review a purchase suggestion. (2) Record the baseline before building anything: cases per week, minutes per case, error rate. This is the step the Ryanair announcement skips[5] and the one that later decides whether the contract gets renewed. (3) The legacy system gets a thin tool layer — three to five clearly bounded functions with permission checks, nothing more; the agent never gets direct database access. (4) The model stays swappable behind an intermediate layer, so idea 1 doesn't fail on its own construction. (5) Every run logs input, tool call, and result; approval stays with a human until the error rate is proven out. Deliberately out of scope: a second process before the first is finished, a free-form chat window over all data, any automation without approval.
Market & gap: The vendor side is clearly occupied and getting stronger: the agent arrives built into the product — Google is rolling out Gemini Enterprise to 35,000 employees as part of a five-year contract,[5] and Gartner describes exactly this path as the new baseline stage of automation.[7] That's not competition, it's tailwind: the more taken-for-granted the agent becomes in the purchased system, the more conspicuously it's missing in the client's own. The gap isn't technology, it's economics for the vendor — integrating with a one-off legacy system can't be sold as a product, only as a project, and projects at this size are too small for a large corporation and too unprofitable for a big consultancy. For a one-person firm, they're exactly the right size. A second gap, from the main part of this brief: only 25% of AI initiatives reach the expected ROI[3] — an offer that starts with step (2), measuring the baseline, already sets itself apart in the sales conversation from everything else the client usually sees on the table.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium — and no higher, for one reason: unlike idea 1, this creates ongoing operations. In favor of building it: the hard part is bought in; twiceD builds the tool layer, the permission checks, and the logging. V0 solo in four to six weeks, running in the client's own stack, so no on-call duty falls on twiceD. Against it are three things that belong in the offer: first, models and interfaces change several times a year — that requires a paid maintenance contract, or the second client eats the profit from the first. Second, access to the legacy system is the real risk; where no usable interface exists, that's a database project with a different price tag, and it has to be spotted beforehand. Third, this only scales with external developers per engagement, not with more clients per week. Realistically that's two to three parallel engagements, then it's full — that's viable, but it's not a product, it's a craft. uctoday.com · googlecloudpresscorner.com · newsroom.ibm.com

The second number comes from PwC, and it doesn't ask about adoption — it asks about money. The survey polled four thousand four hundred fifty-four CEOs in ninety-five countries, published in January in Davos. Only one in eight, or twelve percent, say AI has delivered both revenue gains and cost savings. Thirty-three percent report one or the other. And fifty-six percent say they've seen no significant financial benefit to date. That's the hardest line of the year, and it comes from a consultancy that sells AI itself.

The third number is from IBM, and it measures something else again — individual initiatives. Only twenty-five percent delivered the expected return. Only sixteen percent have rolled out company-wide. That survey is from May twenty twenty-five, fifteen months old.

A word of caution belongs here: this isn't a funnel from one measurement, it's three photos from three different cameras. Stanford counts organizations, PwC surveys CEOs, IBM evaluates initiatives. All three are self-reported. Still, the direction is clear, and in my view the most interesting number isn't the twelve at all — it's the sixteen percent rolled out company-wide. A pilot in one department costs little and never shows up on a profit-and-loss statement. Real benefit only shows up once a process is fully converted, exceptions and vacation coverage included. Sell piloting, and you're selling into a market that's eighty-eight percent saturated. Sell finishing the job, and you're selling into one that's sixteen.

There's a named counterexample, though. Palantir reported second-quarter revenue in early August of one point nine three five billion dollars, up ninety-three percent from a year earlier, and its US commercial business grew a hundred forty-nine percent. Full-year guidance was raised to just over eight point one billion. The mistake I've read several times this week goes: so the fifty-six percent must be wrong. No — it can still be right. A vendor's revenue isn't the buyer's return. Every one of those dollars sits in someone else's cost column.

Ryanair shows what a real rollout looks like. Eleven days ago, a five-year partnership with Google Cloud was announced: Gemini Enterprise and Workspace for thirty-five thousand employees, plus DeepMind models for fleet operations and maintenance planning. What's described is scope, term, and use area. What's not described is any figure someone could check back against in two years. No contract value, no expected saving, no baseline. That's exactly how the fifty-six percent gets made. The number isn't missing because the benefit is missing — it's missing because it was never defined.

On top of that, there's a collision written into the calendar. Gartner expects forty percent of enterprise applications to include task-specific agents by the end of this year, up from under five percent. At the same time, since August second, enforcement powers under the European AI Act have been active for general-purpose models. The AI Office can request documentation, evaluate models itself, demand corrective steps, and restrict market deployment. The fine range is fifteen million euros or three percent of worldwide annual turnover. My reading: this doesn't slow the agent wave, because it targets model vendors, not the machine-tool maker who switches on an agent. But market restriction is a supply risk, and that's an operational question, not a legal one.

One more warning. A claim is circulating widely that around eighty percent of new enterprise applications now come with a built-in agent, attributed to Gartner. That number couldn't be traced to any report — only to two search-engine pages with no title, date, or link. It's not used here, not even with a caveat. Only the forty is documented.

That's where the two project ideas come from. The first is a dependency register with a switchover plan: a bill of materials for every built-in AI feature in the building, with vendor and foundation model behind each one, and for every critical process, a page on what to do if it disappears tomorrow. Assessment work with a clear end point, feasibility high. The second is the legacy-system agent bridge: if forty percent of purchased applications come with an agent, the other sixty percent includes, among other things, the homegrown systems where SMEs actually make their money. No suite vendor builds anything for those. Feasibility, honestly, is medium, because this one creates ongoing operations.

For the coming days, I've got two things flagged. First, December thirty-first, when the forty-percent forecast expires and becomes checkable. And second, the ongoing search for what's been missing here for weeks: a German case study with a name, a baseline, a target, and a measurement by someone with no stake in the outcome.
