Google Takes Away the Assistant — and Leaves No Way Back [1][2]

Starting 2026-09-04, Google begins removing Google Assistant from Android phones and tablets, Wear OS watches, headphones, and Android Auto, and replacing it with Gemini.[1] The rollout stretches over several weeks. The decisive line, quoted again and again in the coverage: "You will no longer be able to use—or switch back to—the legacy Google Assistant."[2] Not affected, according to the reporting: cars with "Google built-in," Google TV, and the Home speakers.[1]

Why this isn't just a rebrand: The old Assistant was, at its core, a command matcher — it checked what you said against a list of known patterns ("set an alarm for seven") and, on a match, did exactly the same thing every time. Gemini is a language model — it composes its response and decides fresh each time. Picture it this way: you used to have a vending machine with labeled buttons; now you have a very well-read assistant. It can do vastly more — but pressing the button isn't guaranteed to do the same thing every time. For a conversation, that's a gain. For a routine that has reliably opened the garage door for four years, it's a risk.

My take. The interesting part isn't Gemini — it's the missing way back. That AI in the SME world doesn't get ordered but arrives bundled with the next update has been this brief's running theme for weeks. September 4 is exactly that process in its most brutal form: an interaction model that people have built their kitchen, their car, and their morning around gets swapped out with no choice in the matter. In practice, for an Android household that means: in the next ten days, go through what runs by voice once, and know what of that also works with your fingers. For twiceD it means something more sober — if even Google can retire an interaction model with no toggle back, "what do we do if this vendor switches tomorrow" stops being a paranoid question and becomes operational planning. And it's cheaper to answer before the date is on the calendar.

The Token Price Falls 80% — the Agent Price Climbs [3][4][5][6]

On 2026-07-30, OpenAI cut its API prices. For GPT-5.6 Luna: "Before: $1 per million input tokens, $6 per million output tokens. After: $0.20 per million input tokens, $1.20 per million output tokens." — that's −80% on both sides. The larger model, Terra, fell from $2.50 to $2.00 (input) and from $15 to $12 (output), so −20%.[3] A day later came the first formal confirmation that ChatGPT itself has over one billion weekly users.[4]

What "$0.20 per million tokens" means in practice: A token is a word fragment — roughly four characters; a million tokens is about 750,000 words, so around eight fat novels. For twenty cents. More concretely: a company that has 20,000 incoming emails sorted automatically each month might process around 10 million tokens doing it. Model cost before July 30: roughly $10. After: roughly $2. The honest part belongs here too — model cost was almost never what killed these projects. But it was often the number sitting in the spreadsheet.

On the consumer side, the movement runs the opposite way. Google's agentic assistant Gemini Spark — one that doesn't just answer but works through tasks continuously on its own machine — sits behind the "AI Ultra" tier, which costs, per Google's own pricing page here in Brazil, R$ 779.90 to R$ 999.90 a month; "AI Pro" runs R$ 96.99, "AI Plus" R$ 24.99.[5] Ultra offers "acesso antecipado a recursos avançados como o Deep Think e o Gemini Spark (em alguns países)" — so Spark isn't even included everywhere.[5] Anthropic does the opposite with the comparable feature: Claude Cowork, the desktop agent with file access, sits in the $20 Pro plan and is missing only from the free tier.[6]

My take. Two price movements in the same week, and they only appear to contradict each other: the raw material gets dumped, the experience gets expensive. Buy tokens and build it yourself, and you pay a fifth of what you paid before; buy a finished agent subscription, and one vendor charges a multiple of the other for the same category of function. So the most important pricing story of the summer isn't "AI is getting cheap" — it's: the spread between assembling it yourself and buying it finished just exploded. This comparison isn't proof that Google is overpriced — the packages contain different things, and Spark runs on dedicated machines that cost real money. But anyone who sees this spread and still buys per head without doing the math is giving money away. That's where idea 1 below comes from.

The 41 Percent That Doesn't Exist [7][8][9]

Since spring, a striking number of German trade write-ups have carried the same line: „41 % der deutschen Unternehmen nutzen KI bereits aktiv", often with the added „Verdopplung" ("doubling"), attributed to a „Bitkom-KI-Studie 2026."[9] This figure reached this brief on Sunday; I didn't use it, because it only showed up as a search result. Today the study report itself is in hand, and the number isn't in it.

The report says, verbatim: „Inzwischen setzt mehr als jedes dritte Unternehmen (36 Prozent) KI ein. Damit hat sich der Anteil innerhalb eines Jahres fast verdoppelt – 2024 lag dieser noch bei 20 Prozent." Further: „47 Prozent der Unternehmen beschäftigen sich aktuell mit einem möglichen Einsatz", and „Nur noch 17 Prozent geben an, dass sie sich nicht mit KI befassen."[7] The association's own press release on the same survey cites identical figures and hands over the likely origin of the error along with them: „nur noch 17 Prozent, dass KI für sie kein Thema ist, nach 41 Prozent im Vorjahr."[8]

That's the whole story. 41% is the share of companies for which AI was not a topic in the year 2024 — the neighboring bar in the same chart. "41 percent had nothing to do with AI" turned into "41 percent actively use AI," the exact inversion, and from there it kept getting quoted.

Number check The rest of the circulating version doesn't hold up either — and the second error is the same as the first. Both secondary pages write „41 Prozent … nach 17 Prozent im Vorjahr" ("41 percent ... versus 17 percent the year before").[9] That 17 also comes from the "not a topic" series: 41% was that share in 2024, 17% the same share in 2025.[7][8] A line about companies without AI thereby turned into a growth curve for companies with AI — twice, in a single sentence. Both pages further claim „19 Prozent haben bereits Stellen abgebaut" ("19 percent have already cut jobs") and „77 Prozent der Nutzer berichten von einer verbesserten Wettbewerbsposition" ("77 percent of users report an improved competitive position"); I found neither of these in the study report.[9] On employment, the report says: „Zwei Drittel (67 Prozent) gehen davon aus, dass KI keinen Einfluss auf die Anzahl der Stellen haben wird" ("two-thirds, 67 percent, expect AI to have no effect on headcount") and „Ein Fünftel der Unternehmen (20 Prozent) rechnet hingegen mit einem Rückgang von Arbeitsplätzen" ("one-fifth of companies, 20 percent, do expect a decline in jobs") — an expectation for the coming five years, not job cuts already carried out.[7] On the hurdles, skill-sprinters comes closer, but still misses: „Fehlende Kompetenz der Mitarbeiter (53 Prozent)", „Datenschutz und rechtliche Unsicherheiten (44 Prozent)", „Integration in bestehende Prozesse (39 Prozent)" — the report instead has legal uncertainty and lack of technical know-how at 53% each, then lack of staff resources at 51% and data protection at 48% (charted below).[7] And on dating: 2026-04-11, or "early 2026," versus the actual field period of weeks 27 to 32 of 2025.[7]

My take. I nearly printed this number myself, and that's the actual point. It has every property a number needs to pass unchecked: plausible, a credible-sounding source, tells the story everyone expects. The pages spreading it are recognizably machine-generated summaries — text about AI adoption, written by AI, that flips the adoption number in the process. That's no cause for mockery, but a concrete operating risk: a sales pitch built on a number nobody read in the original holds up exactly until the customer who looks it up. And because humility is cheaper than a lecture here: this very box originally cited a hurdles list that doesn't actually appear on the pages it checked — looked up, corrected, disclosed in footnote 9. My rule therefore stands, with one addition: no number goes into this brief whose primary document I haven't opened myself — not even when the number is only there to disprove another one.

Where AI Actually Sits in German Companies [7]

Along the way, the report answers a question that interests me more than the adoption rate: where the stuff is actually built in. Among AI-using companies, customer contact leads by a wide margin at 88%, followed by marketing and communications (57%). Then it drops off steeply: R&D 21%, production processes 20%, controlling 17%, HR 14% — and the IT department itself just 2%. Only 12% have integrated it into their own product. Just 26% of all companies give employees direct access to generative AI at all; among startups, 82% use AI.[7]

My take. This is a picture of AI as facade: it stands where customers look, and it's missing where a company actually makes its money. The 12% in-product figure is the most telling number in the report — AI gets bought for the front desk, and almost nobody builds it into what they sell. Anyone who wants to build a business on this shouldn't look at the 88%. They should look at the 12.

Humanoids: Eleven Months, 90,000 Parts, 30,000 Cars [10][11][12]

The most solidly documented deployment report on humanoid robots comes from a German industrial group operating in the US — and the solid figures come from the manufacturer itself. At BMW's Spartanburg plant, Figure 02 ran for eleven months on an active assembly line: "1,250+ hours of runtime", "Ran 10-hour shift Monday-Friday", "90,000+ parts loaded", "Contributed to the production of 30,000+ X3 vehicles".[10] The often-quoted success rate is stated by Figure explicitly as a target, not a measured result — "Our target was > 99% success per shift" — and neither Figure nor BMW says how many units were deployed on the floor.[10] On manufacturing, Figure reports for the third generation "delivering over 350 of our third generation humanoid robots", "over 9,000 actuators", and a production pace that went "from 1 Figure 03 per day to 1 per hour — a 24x throughput improvement in under 120 days".[11] At Tesla, per secondary reports on an earnings deck from 2026-07-22, "over 1,000 Optimus Gen 3 units" are said to be running in Fremont; the original deck wasn't available to me, so the unit count here is unconfirmed and stands only as an order of magnitude. The point behind it isn't in dispute: exclusively in-house, external customers only announced.[12]

Why "over 99%" says less here than it sounds like: First, the fine print — at Figure, this figure is a per-shift target, not a reported result; what was actually achieved appears nowhere. And even as a result, 99% sounds nearly flawless, but it isn't: out of 90,000 parts, that's still up to 900 misplaced sheet-metal pieces over eleven months, about three per working day on average. In a factory, everything then hinges on what that kind of error costs: if the part audibly falls to the floor, someone picks it up; if it slips unnoticed into the body shell, it gets expensive. So the decisive question for any robot accuracy figure is never "how high is it," but "what happens with the rest" — and whether it was even measured, or only aimed at.
Number check The "10,000 humanoids shipped" figure doesn't exist — and neither does the "over 500." Figure's own manufacturing update from 2026-04-29 states "over 350 of our third generation humanoid robots". The widely cited "over 500 units" appears in the same text, but as "shipped over 500 battery packs" — battery packs, not robots.[11] The 10,000 figure, meanwhile, circulates widely across blog and aggregator sources and isn't an official Figure statement; it isn't used here, not even "with a caveat." Between the circulating figure and the documented one lies a factor of roughly 29. An earlier version of this paragraph itself carried the 500 figure; corrected after directly checking the primary source.

My take. The honest denominator is the most remarkable part of this. Eleven months, one line, one task — and still the best-documented case I know of in this field, because it puts a number on run-time and unit output at all. That's exactly what's missing from almost every other AI announcement. What it does not put a number on is just as telling: the count of units deployed, and the accuracy rate actually achieved. What does not follow from this: that the factory is about to go humanoid. An eleven-month deployment on one line doesn't replace a shift, it proves feasibility. Anyone talking about a humanoid breakthrough should hold this set of figures up alongside it — and ask why the spectacular numbers in this field always come secondhand, and the sober ones come from the source.

Delayed Is Not Dropped [13][14]

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 — the "Digital Omnibus on AI" — was published in the Official Journal on 2026-07-24 and entered into force on 2026-07-27. It pushes back the high-risk obligations: for standalone systems under Annex III (high-risk use cases — candidate screening, creditworthiness, access to public benefits), from 2026-08-02 to 2027-12-02; for AI embedded in products already subject to review — Annex I, i.e. elevators, medical devices, machinery — to 2028-08-02.[13] What was not pushed back: the transparency and labeling obligations under Article 50, applicable since 2026-08-02; the obligations for providers of general-purpose models have applied since August 2025, and the ban on certain practices under Article 5 since February 2025.[14]

My take. Sixteen months of delay is relief for vendors and a trap for buyers. The relief is real — the standards companies would have had to align with weren't finished by summer 2026. The trap is that "postponed" gets read as "cancelled" in a lot of companies, burying the deadline that did hold. Article 50 is the law's least spectacular and simultaneously most broadly applicable rule: anyone running AI that interacts with users, or generating synthetic content, has to disclose it. That doesn't hit the model builder — it hits the SME with the chat window on its website. It requires no certification, just one line of text in the right place — which is exactly why it gets overlooked.

Gaps A "Geneva AI Accord" doesn't exist. Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Recalculating Rejected AI Projects — the Price Crash as a Trigger

ELI5 & value: In almost every SME, there's an AI project sitting in a drawer that was evaluated twelve to eighteen months ago and then rejected — usually with "doesn't pencil out." Since then, one single line item in that calculation has dropped 80%: the price of machine-generated text.[3] Every other line — integration, data prep, training, operations — has stayed roughly the same. The product is a short, paid recalculation of exactly these cases: which rejection hinged on model price and is therefore obsolete, and which hinged on process and remains correct? The value is a recovered project backed by a solid number — and, at least as valuable, a well-founded confirmation for the cases that still don't work. Fitting with that, the Bitkom report has 36% of companies citing lack of financial resources as a hurdle.[7]
Prototype (V0): (1) Collect the rejected projects — they live in meeting notes, proposals, and the heads of two or three people, not in a system. (2) For each case, reconstruct the old calculation and split it into two buckets: what hinges on model price, what doesn't. (3) A real-data volume model instead of an estimate — documents per month, characters per document, tokens from that; one day of measurement beats a week of assumptions. (4) Recalculate with today's list prices, plus for each case a variant using an open model run in-house, where data is the concern. (5) Result: a table with a traffic-light rating, plus a two-page implementation sketch for the best case. Deliberately out of scope: building it, ongoing cost monitoring, tool operations. Building blocks: vendors' price lists, a token counter, and for the sovereignty variant, an open model on in-house hardware.
Market & gap: Tools for AI cost management are plentiful and well-funded — CloudZero, Langfuse, Portkey, Helicone, LiteLLM, Datadog LLM Observability, sorted into a gateway layer that caps budgets before spend happens, and an observability layer that attributes every call after the fact.[15] They all answer the same question: what does what we already run cost? Nobody answers the one before it: what should we have built, now that the price has dropped? That question comes up once, not continuously — so it doesn't fit a subscription, which is exactly why no product vendor builds it. The platform side sells seats instead of business cases anyway: Google's agentic tier costs R$ 779.90 to R$ 999.90 a month here, Anthropic puts its counterpart in the $20 plan[5][6] — both per-head flat rates that tell no CEO whether their specific case now pencils out. And the big consultancies price AI strategy at a level that shuts out a 200-person company.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. Pure assessment work with a clear start and end, one to two weeks per engagement, no hosting, no on-call duty, no operating load. What's reusable is the process, not the code: questionnaire, calculation template, volume model, report template; scaling via one external assessor per industry. Three honest limitations. First, the result decays — prices keep falling, this is a dated snapshot, and the twelve-month repeat needs to be in the offer. Second, the model-price saving is small for many projects compared to integration and staffing; anyone who turns this into an "80% cheaper" sales pitch will lose credibility with the first honest customer. The sales line has to be: we separate the two buckets, and sometimes the result is "stays rejected." Third, this needs access to old calculations and, with that, trust — more a second engagement than a door-opener. ghacks.net · bitkom.org · cloudzero.com · gemini.google

2 · AI in the Product, Not the Inbox — the 12% Nobody Serves

ELI5 & value: German companies have installed AI at the front desk: 88% of AI users deploy it in customer contact, 57% in marketing — but only 12% have built it into what they actually sell.[7] For a company that ships software, a portal, or a machine with control software, that's the real opportunity: not cutting your own costs, but making your own product better and charging more for it. Picture it this way: so far, the company has rented a smart assistant for its own office; here, the point is to build one into the customer's tool. A maintenance portal that turns a plain-text error message into the right spare-part suggestion is a sales argument and a price line — the assistant in your own inbox is neither.
Prototype (V0): (1) One screen in the customer's product, chosen by the question "where does a user call in today instead of getting further on their own." (2) Measure the baseline before building anything: support requests for this step per month, time to resolution. (3) Bring existing product data — manuals, error codes, parts lists — into a searchable store and have the model answer from that, instead of letting it guess. (4) Every answer always cites its source from the company's own documents, so a service technician can check it. (5) AI labeling directly in the interface — mandatory since 2026-08-02 anyway.[14] Deliberately out of scope: a free-form chat window over the whole product, write access to customer data, more than one screen. Building blocks: a bought-in model behind a swappable interface, a standard vector store, no in-house model development.
Market & gap: On the vendor side, everyone is crowding into the horizontal layer: Google sells per-head assistance starting at R$ 96.99 up to R$ 999.90 a month, Anthropic the same starting at $20[5][6] — horizontal products that do the same thing in every company. Which is exactly why they do nothing special in any one product. The gap isn't technical, it's economic: an AI feature for a niche machine-builder's product with 300 customers can't be sold as a product, only as a project — and projects at that size are too small for a large corporation and too unprofitable for a big consultancy. The proof sits in the same survey: the biggest hurdles are lack of technical know-how (53%) and lack of staff resources (51%).[7] Translated: companies want to, but can't do it themselves — the definition of a services market. There's also a tailwind with a date attached: what a feature like this eats in model cost has dropped to a fifth since 07/30,[3] which means it can, for the first time, be priced into a license fee instead of eating it whole.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium — and deliberately not higher. A lot favors building it: one screen, a bought-in model, manageable scope; solo V0 in four to six weeks, running in the client's own stack, so no on-call duty falls on twiceD. Against it are three things that belong in the contract, not in goodwill. First, the result lands inside someone else's product — their release cycles, their customers, their liability questions; where twiceD's responsibility ends has to be in the contract before line one. Second, models and interfaces change several times a year; without a paid maintenance contract, the second client eats the first client's profit. Third, this only scales via external developers per engagement, not via more clients per week — realistically, two to three parallel engagements. Sovereignty variant, where product data can't leave the building: the same pattern with an open model on the customer's own hardware, noticeably weaker answer quality, but no data leakage — a customer decision, not a technical one. bitkom.org · gemini.google · claude.com · ghacks.net

It's Tuesday, August twenty-fifth. Three things today, and they're connected: a forced switchover in ten days, a price crash, and a number half of Germany keeps quoting even though it doesn't exist.

First, the switchover. On September fourth, Google begins removing Google Assistant from Android phones, tablets, Wear OS watches, headphones, and Android Auto, replacing it with Gemini. The rollout stretches over several weeks. The line that keeps getting quoted everywhere: you will no longer be able to use, or switch back to, the legacy Google Assistant. Exempt are cars with built-in Google, Google TV, and the Home speakers.

Why this isn't just a rebrand: the old Assistant was, at its core, a command matcher. It checked what you said against a list of known patterns and, on a match, did exactly the same thing every time. Gemini is a language model — it composes its response and decides fresh each time. For a conversation, that's a gain. For a routine that's reliably opened the garage door for four years, it's a risk. My take: the interesting part isn't Gemini, it's the missing way back. If even Google can retire an interaction model with no toggle to switch back, then asking what happens when a vendor switches tomorrow stops being a paranoid question and becomes operational planning.

Second, the price. On July thirtieth, OpenAI cut its API prices. For the model called Luna, a million input tokens now cost twenty cents instead of a dollar, a million output tokens a dollar twenty instead of six dollars. That's eighty percent off on both sides. For the larger model, Terra, it's twenty percent. A day later came the first formal confirmation that ChatGPT has over one billion weekly users. For scale: a million tokens is roughly eight hundred thousand words, about eight fat novels, for twenty cents.

On the consumer side, it runs exactly the other way. Google's agentic assistant, Spark, sits behind the AI Ultra tier, which costs here in Brazil, per Google's own pricing page, seven hundred seventy-nine reais ninety to nine hundred ninety-nine reais ninety a month. Anthropic puts its directly comparable counterpart, Claude Cowork, in the twenty-dollar plan. My reading: the raw material gets dumped, the experience gets expensive. The spread between assembling it yourself and buying it finished just exploded.

And now, the number that doesn't exist. Since spring, a striking number of German trade write-ups have carried the same line: forty-one percent of German companies already actively use AI, supposedly from a Bitkom study. This figure reached this brief on Sunday, and I didn't use it, because it only showed up as a search result. Today, the study report itself is in hand. It says: more than one in three companies, thirty-six percent, use AI, up from twenty percent in the year twenty twenty-four. And the same association's own press release hands over the likely origin of the error: only seventeen percent now say AI isn't a topic for them, down from forty-one percent the year before.

That's the whole story. Forty-one is the share for which AI wasn't a topic at all back in twenty twenty-four — the neighboring bar in the very same chart. "Forty-one percent had nothing to do with AI" turned into "forty-one percent actively use AI" — the exact inversion — and from there, it kept getting quoted. The rest of it doesn't hold up either, and in the same way. Both pages claim the prior-year figure was seventeen percent. That seventeen comes from the same column: it's the share for which AI wasn't a topic in twenty twenty-five. Likewise, the nineteen percent of jobs already cut and the seventy-seven percent reporting an improved competitive position that those pages cite don't appear in the report at all. The real biggest hurdles are legal uncertainty and lack of technical know-how, at fifty-three percent each, then lack of staff resources at fifty-one, and data protection at forty-eight.

My take on that: I nearly printed this number myself, and that's the actual point. It sounds plausible, it has a credible-sounding source, it tells the story everyone expects. The pages spreading it are recognizably machine-generated summaries — text about AI adoption, written by AI, that flips the adoption number in the process. That's not a reason for mockery, it's an operating risk: a sales pitch built on a number nobody read in the original holds up exactly until the customer who looks it up.

What's more interesting than the adoption rate anyway is where the stuff actually sits. Customer contact, eighty-eight percent. Marketing, fifty-seven. Then it drops off steeply. In the IT department itself, just two percent. And only twelve percent have built it into their own product. That's a picture of AI as facade: it stands where customers look, and it's missing where a company actually makes its money. Anyone who wants to build a business on this shouldn't look at the eighty-eight. They should look at the twelve.

From the factory floor: Figure Zero-Two spent eleven months working an active assembly line at BMW's Spartanburg plant — over one thousand two hundred fifty hours of run time, more than ninety thousand sheet-metal parts loaded, and a hand in building over thirty thousand X-Threes. The widely quoted success rate of over ninety-nine percent is explicitly a per-shift target at Figure, not a measured result, and neither company says how many units were actually on the floor. Figure has shipped over three hundred fifty third-generation robots. The widely circulating figure of ten thousand is not an official number and isn't used here. And from Brussels: the Digital Omnibus pushes the high-risk obligations to December second, twenty twenty-seven, and August second, twenty twenty-eight. Not postponed: the transparency duties under Article fifty. Those have applied since August second, and they don't hit the model builder — they hit the SME with the chat window on its website.

That's where the two project ideas come from. The first: recalculating rejected AI projects, because one single line item in those old calculations has dropped eighty percent while the rest haven't moved. Pure assessment work, feasibility high. The second: AI in the product, not the inbox, built on the twelve percent nobody serves. Feasibility, honestly, is medium, because the result lands in someone else's product. For the coming days, one date on the calendar matters most: September fourth. I'll be checking what the switchover breaks in real households — and whether anyone corrects the forty-one percent.
