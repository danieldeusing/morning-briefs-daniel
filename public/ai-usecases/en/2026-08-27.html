The Lines Have Crossed [1][2][3]

On 2026-08-14, OpenAI CFO Sarah Friar told a closed-door investor session that the enterprise business now generates more revenue than ChatGPT does from consumers. Verbatim: „The majority of our revenue is now enterprise." And on the pace of it: „We entered the year at 60-40, but enterprise has accelerated much faster than expected and those lines have now crossed."[1] This parity had been planned for the end of 2026 — it arrived roughly half a year early.[1]

„$40 billion ARR" is not annual revenue: ARR stands for annualized run rate — a projected yearly figure. You take what came in during the most recent month and multiply by twelve. Picture it this way: your store had a really good August, so you put a sign on the door that says "annual revenue: $40 million." That isn't a lie, but it's a claim about the next eleven months, not a cash-register receipt. For a business that, per the same source, grew 20% month over month in July, this kind of projection is especially fragile — it assumes the most recent month is the new normal.

A second figure from the same session: advertising, which OpenAI has been testing inside ChatGPT since 2026-02-09, is approaching an annualized run rate of $1 billion.[1] Against a $40 billion total run rate, that's still a footnote — but it's the second revenue stream that doesn't depend on the consumer subscription.

On the provenance This is a company's self-reported account from a closed session, relayed through the press. The original report sits at CNBC[2] and returned HTTP 403 on every direct attempt to load it; what's used and quoted here verbatim is therefore a trade outlet carrying the same Friar quotes.[1] No independent verification, no audited figures, no breakdown of what „enterprise" means here — API revenue, seat licenses, or both. And the 32% currently circulating as „enterprise revenue +32% in July" appears, in the source I could load, as „business customer count growing 32% in the same period"[1] — that is, growth in the number of customers. A different secondary report writes „business grew 32% in July"[3] without saying what grew. Until that's cleaned up, it isn't evidence of revenue.

My take. A vendor's revenue mix is normally investor small talk, irrelevant to a user. Not here — because it foreshadows the direction of every future decision this vendor makes. A company paid mostly by businesses builds for businesses: contracts instead of click-through flows, data processing agreements instead of terms of service, regions instead of „global," support with a phone number, prices with room to negotiate — and, less pleasantly, features that migrate into the expensive tier.

Revenue majority tips toward businesses → Roadmap follows the paying customer → Commitments & pricing migrate into enterprise tiers

For an SME that buys AI today through a handful of team subscriptions, that's good news and bad news in a single sentence: reliability has become negotiable, but from now on it costs a contract. My practical recommendation is small and cheap: list, once, everything running in-house on consumer or team plans, and for each item, answer whether it carries a real business process. Anything that answers yes belongs in a procurement process — before the vendor brings the subject up on its own.

Ryanair Buys Five Years of Google — and Stays with AWS Anyway [4]

On 2026-08-12, Ryanair and Google Cloud announced a five-year partnership for data and AI. The scope is stated in the release itself: „Ryanair will deploy Google Workspace and Google Cloud services to 35,000 employees across its network to support the company's growth to 300 million passengers by 2034."[4] DeepMind models are also set to be used: „It will also draw on Google DeepMind models, including AlphaEvolve and WeatherNext, to assist with fleet operations and maintenance scheduling."[4] On the scale of the operation, also from the release: roughly 216 million passengers a year, about 3,900 flights a day, 95 bases, over 220 airports in 35 countries, and nearly 650 aircraft.[4]

Two-cloud strategy: A company doesn't rent compute from one provider but deliberately from two — here, Google alongside the existing AWS. Picture it this way: you don't buy every ingredient at one supermarket, even though it's more hassle, because otherwise you have to absorb every price hike that one store decides to impose. The price you pay is having to maintain operating know-how for both in-house; what you get in return is negotiating leverage and a way out.

My take. Ryanair is exactly the kind of customer this brief wants to see: a named company, a named time frame, a named figure. But the number splashed across every headline — 35,000 employees — is a rollout figure, not a result. It says how many mailboxes get switched over, not what that delivers. Maintenance scheduling is the interesting part, and it's exactly the one with no metric today: no before-and-after, no failure rate, no downtime figure. I'm flagging this as a checkpoint for Q1 2027, when the first operating figures would be due.

What I find notable is the explicit mention of a two-cloud strategy in a press release that Google itself co-signed. A corporation placing gigascale orders gets it confirmed in writing that it can switch providers. For an SME entering the same kind of dependency on a smaller scale, with no negotiating power, that's the real lesson: what Ryanair gets written into its contract, you have to build for yourself instead — as a second path, or as an exit plan.

Salesforce Counts Agents — and I Recount Them [5][6][7][8]

On 2026-08-10, Salesforce published its Agentic Enterprise Index 2026: aggregated usage data from its own Agentforce customers, covering the period from February 2025 to April 2026. The headline figure, in Salesforce's own words: „The average number of agents activated per organization increased nearly 3X over the past year."[5] On build time: „businesses start creating those agents within an average of 2 days. And that number is decreasing month over month, down 53% across the whole analysis period."[5] And on capability breadth, per the coverage: „The average agent able to perform six distinct business actions by the end of 2025, compared with two at the beginning of the year."[7]

The figure quoted everywhere sits on the results page — from a post by Salesforce's own official account on X: „escalation rates to human agents held around 32%. Agents also consistently resolved 7 in 10 customer service chats without human help."[6]

„Six business actions per agent": Here, an agent isn't a chat window — it's a program that carries a task through to completion on its own: checking, deciding, booking, replying. „Six actions" means the same program is now allowed to do six different things — say, check an order status, file a return, reschedule an appointment. Picture it this way: a vending machine with one button just became one with six buttons. That's the real jump in this survey — not the number of agents, but how much each one is allowed to touch.
Methodology The index is a vendor's survey of its own customers — not a market average, and not an independent sample. What matters is the cut: only organizations „that had agents activated in production every month throughout the analysis period"[7] were included — that is, exclusively firms where it ran continuously. Anyone who dropped out or paused during the period doesn't show up. Growth measured only among survivors necessarily grows. So the index shows what agents do at firms where agents work — a reasonable question, but not the one a prospective buyer is asking.

Two recalculations. First: 70% resolved without a human plus 32% escalated to humans adds up to 102%. So these can't be two halves of the same underlying population — either different time periods, different channels, or different denominators. They're still close, though, so the honest summary is: roughly three in ten conversations still land with a human. Anyone building headcount planning on „70% gone" has already priced in the two-point fuzziness, but hasn't answered whether the remaining three are the expensive ones.

Second: the often-quoted pair „from 5 to 13 agents" doesn't actually appear in Salesforce's release — that says only „nearly 3X." The absolute numbers come from an aggregator[8], and a second piece from the same outlet frames it differently: „12 agents today, 20 by 2027." That's the same disease as the Bitkom figure from Tuesday: a primary source states a relative figure, the internet invents the absolute number to go with it, and by the third time it gets reused, it's sitting in quotation marks right after the company's name.

My take. Strip out the methodology and the number-cosmetics, and one claim remains that I find solid and important: the event here isn't the number of agents, it's the build time. An average of two days, and still falling. Two days is shorter than any approval process I've ever seen at an SME — shorter than a works-council meeting, shorter than a data-protection impact assessment, often shorter than procuring an office chair. If something that's allowed to trigger six business actions gets built in two days, then things like that are now being created faster than anyone can write them down. That's no longer a technology question. That's oversight.

The Pipes Go Neutral: A2A Now Sits Alongside MCP [9][10]

Google's agent-to-agent protocol A2A moved into the Agentic AI Foundation of the Linux Foundation on 2026-08-20 — the same foundation where Anthropic's MCP sits as a founding project.[10] Per the report, the foundation has grown from 49 founding members to more than 250; the top membership tier includes AWS, Anthropic, Bloomberg, Cloudflare, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI, among others.[10]

A2A and MCP — two different outlets: The Linux Foundation itself puts it most clearly: „A2A defines how agents communicate and coordinate with each other across organizational boundaries, while MCP defines how agents connect to internal tools and data sources."[9] Picture it this way: MCP is the outlet in your own workshop, where your device reaches the tools and files of your own house. A2A is the phone line between your workshop and the one next door — your program calls over there and asks for the job to get done, without knowing how they work on the other end.

That both plugs now sit under one neutral roof isn't a detail. From a Linux Foundation statement dated 2026-04-09: all three major clouds already support A2A — Microsoft in Azure AI Foundry and Copilot Studio, AWS in Amazon Bedrock AgentCore Runtime, plus Google Cloud; at the time, more than 150 organizations stood behind the standard, up from more than 50 a year earlier.[9] Cited use cases: supply chain, financial services, insurance, IT operations.[9]

My take. For a buyer, the relevant question isn't who invented the protocol, but who owns it once it becomes inconvenient. A protocol housed inside one vendor is a promise; one housed in a foundation is an agreement. That lowers exactly the kind of risk that actually paralyzes an SME: not „does this work?" but „will I be standing alone in three years?" In practice, that still means little today — a standard doesn't mean any two agents can talk to each other, only that they're allowed to speak the same language. But it makes a contract clause formulable that used to be hollow: the interface follows a standard owned by a neutral foundation. Scope note SDKs, implementation details, and what this means for developer tooling are in the ai-dev brief.

Web Search with a Zip Code [11][12]

On 2026-08-19, AWS expanded the Web Search tool in Bedrock AgentCore to two more regions: „The Web Search Tool is expanding to Europe (Ireland) (eu-west-1) and Asia Pacific (Tokyo) (ap-northeast-1)".[11] That comes with domain and date filters, plus an allowlist of up to 100 domains.[11]

Why „region" is the operative word here: A cloud region is a geographically fixed set of data centers. If the agent web search runs in „eu-west-1," the request, the result, and the cache are all generated in Ireland — that is, inside the EU. Picture it this way: it's the difference between „I'll get this translated" and „I'll get this translated in the next town over, and the documents never leave the county." For a data protection officer, that's the difference between a discussion and a checkbox.

A date that doesn't check out. Several summaries have it that AgentCore Web Search went generally available on 2026-08-21. I found no evidence for that. General availability, per the report, was at the AWS Summit in New York in June 2026; a separate, broader Bedrock rollout followed on 2026-08-04.[12] The only dated August event is the region expansion of 08/19.[11]

My take. The most interesting feature in this announcement isn't the region, it's the allowlist. An agent allowed to read only 100 self-selected domains is fundamentally different from one that reads „the internet": it's auditable. You can show an auditor the list. That's precisely where AI answers used to fail in regulated fields — not the quality of the answer, but the impossibility of saying where it was even allowed to come from. That becomes Idea 1 below.

Brazil Buys Compute — with a Partner That Raises Questions [13]

For twiceD, this isn't a business opportunity, but it's a useful line to use with clients: „sovereign" isn't a state you're in, it's a statement about who you depend on and how fast you could switch.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Curated Source List — 100 Domains an Auditor Signs Off On

ELI5 & value: When an AI assistant in a company answers a question by „checking the internet," nobody can say where the answer came from. That's exactly what sinks every project in fields with an audit requirement — medical devices, standards-bound mechanical engineering, tax and legal advice, food. For its agent web search, AWS now has three switches that solve this: domain filters, date filters, and an allowlist of up to 100 domains.[11] The product isn't the switch, though — it's the list: which 100 sources are permissible for this operation, how old a hit is allowed to be, and a document explaining why. The value is an AI assistant that a quality or data-protection officer waves through instead of blocking — plus, cleanly handled along the way, the labeling requirement under Article 50 of the AI Act, in force since 2026-08-02 and not delayed.[14]
Prototype (V0): (1) Collect twenty real questions from the operation that someone today answers by looking things up — standards status, manufacturer specs, approvals, deadlines. (2) For each question, write down which in-house source counts as permissible; that's a subject-matter decision, not a technical one, and takes two meetings with the department. (3) Build the allowlist from that, and set the date window per question type (standards: unlimited; pricing: 30 days). (4) Measure against the twenty questions — how many does the assistant answer with a source inside the list, how many stay unresolved. (5) Two pages of process documentation for the file: the list, the rationale, the maintenance interval, the labeling. Deliberately out of scope: a free-form chat window, write access, more than one department. Building blocks: the vendor's allowlist feature, the EU region for processing[11] — none of it gets built in-house.
Market & gap: The surrounding tool market is booming and aims right past this spot. What exists is a class of AI governance and discovery platforms — Credo AI, Arthur, Holistic AI, IBM watsonx.governance, and similar — that automatically detect existing models, applications, and agents inside a company, log them in a register, assign owners, and score risk.[15] They answer the question „what's even running here?" Nobody answers the one before it: „what is this one thing even allowed to read?" That makes sense, too — the answer differs for every client, it hinges on their standards, suppliers, and auditors, and it can't be discovered automatically, because it's a decision, not a state. That's exactly why no product vendor builds it, and exactly why it suits a service provider willing to sit down with the quality officer for two hours. There's tailwind on the demand side: Salesforce now measures an average of two days to a finished agent, still falling[5], and six business actions per agent instead of two[7] — meaning more and more things are being created that are allowed to do more and more, in less and less time.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. Assessment and configuration work with a clear start and end, one to two weeks per engagement, no hosting, no on-call duty. What's reusable is the process — and, the real asset, an industry starter list: once you've properly justified the 100 domains for medical devices, the second and third sign-off sells for half the effort. Scaling comes via one subject-matter assessor per industry, not via more hours. Three honest limitations. First, the result is vendor-locked: the allowlist in this form is an AWS feature[11]; whether competing offerings can do the same, I haven't checked today and would check before the first proposal. Second, a source list decays — it needs a maintenance interval, and that belongs in the contract, not in goodwill. Third, the sales path isn't IT, it's quality, legal, or data protection; anyone pitching in the server room is pitching in the wrong room. aws.amazon.com · arthur.ai · salesforce.com

2 · The Agent Bridge to the Supplier — One Process, Two Companies, One Standard

ELI5 & value: So far, AI inside a company talks to its own systems. The next step is for it to talk to another company's AI — your program asks the supplier whether the delivery is coming and gets an answer back, without anyone making a call or opening a portal. As of 08/20, there's a standard for this with a neutral owner: A2A sits in the same foundation as MCP.[10] The value is the most expensive time sink in procurement and dispatch: chasing down order confirmations, delivery dates, and complaint statuses by phone. One single process, cleanly automated, saves a dispatch team more time than ten chat assistants in the inbox.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick one process between two companies that already talk daily anyway — a delivery-date inquiry is the usual first case. (2) Measure the baseline: how many inquiries per week, how long until an answer. (3) Deploy one small agent on each side that's allowed only to ask or answer this one question, over A2A on a cloud runtime that already supports the standard.[9] (4) The most important part isn't code — it's a two-sided agreement: which questions the other company's agent is allowed to ask, which fields it gets back, how it identifies itself, what gets logged, and who's liable if the answer was wrong. (5) Four weeks running in parallel with the phone, then compare. Deliberately out of scope: triggering orders, price negotiation, write access to inventory management, more than one supplier.
Market & gap: The pipes are built, and the big players own them: Microsoft supports A2A in Azure AI Foundry and Copilot Studio, AWS in Bedrock AgentCore Runtime, plus Google Cloud; more than 150 organizations already stood behind the standard back in April, with cited use cases in supply chain, financial services, insurance, and IT operations.[9] What the platforms sell is the capability that two agents could talk. What nobody sells is the concrete first call between two SMEs that share neither IT, nor a cloud provider, nor a legal department with the capacity for uncharted territory. That's not a product gap, it's a project gap: the effort lies in coordinating between two companies, and there's no license for that. A project like this is too small for a large corporation and too unprofitable for a big consultancy — and the timing is favorable precisely because the standard is now neutral enough that no supplier reads it as being co-opted by a customer.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium — and deliberately not higher. Technically it's small: two narrow agents, an existing runtime, no in-house model. The hard part is organizational, and it needs to be named honestly. First, the project needs two paying parties, or at least two willing ones; if one drops out, the whole thing stalls — realistically, the way in is through a strong client who brings its supplier along. Second, the standard is young: a move into a foundation is governance, not maturity, and changes are to be expected over the next few years; a maintenance contract here isn't an add-on, it's a prerequisite. Third, operations aren't zero — an interface between two companies fails eventually, and then the phone rings; either you sell ongoing support hours along with it, or you hand off cleanly to one side's IT department. Realistically, one to two such projects in parallel, scaling via external developers per engagement. It's Thursday, August twenty-seventh. Today's about a shift that happened over two weeks and can be seen from three angles: the customer paying for applied AI is no longer the individual — it's the company.

The clearest evidence comes from OpenAI. On August fourteenth, CFO Sarah Friar told a closed-door investor session that the majority of revenue now comes from the enterprise business. She said the year started at sixty-forty, but enterprise accelerated much faster than expected, and the lines have now crossed. That parity was planned for the end of the year. It arrived half a year early. The annualized run rate sits at forty billion dollars, up twenty percent month over month in July.

That number needs context. Forty billion isn't annual revenue — it's a projection: take the most recent month and multiply by twelve. That's not a lie, but it's a claim about the next eleven months. And it's a self-reported account from a closed session, relayed through the press. The original report at CNBC wasn't reachable for this brief, so every quote here comes from a trade outlet carrying the same lines.

One number from that same report is currently traveling the internet wrong. Everywhere you look, it says enterprise revenue grew thirty-two percent in July. In the source I could actually load, that thirty-two percent is the growth in the number of business customers, not their revenue. A different secondary report simply says the business grew thirty-two percent, without saying what grew. So I'm not using that number as revenue evidence.

Why a vendor's revenue mix should interest a user at all: because it foreshadows the direction of every future decision that vendor makes. A company paid mostly by businesses builds for businesses. Contracts instead of click-through flows, regions instead of global, support with a phone number, prices with room to negotiate. And, less pleasantly, features that migrate into the expensive tier. For an SME buying AI today through a handful of team subscriptions, that means: reliability has become negotiable, but it now costs a contract.

The second angle comes from Salesforce. On August tenth, the company published its Agentic Enterprise Index, aggregated usage data from its own customers, covering February twenty twenty-five through April twenty twenty-six. Active agents per organization, it says, are nearly three times what they were a year earlier. Time to a finished agent averages two days and fell fifty-three percent over the period. And the average agent can now trigger six different business actions, up from two at the start of the year.

Two warnings go with that. The first is methodology: only organizations that had agents running in every single month of the period were included. Anyone who dropped out doesn't show up. Growth measured only among survivors necessarily grows. The second is about the numbers themselves. There's a figure going around that the agent count rose from five to thirteen. That's not what Salesforce actually says — its release only says nearly three times. The absolute numbers come from an aggregator, and a second piece from that same outlet instead works with twelve today and twenty by twenty twenty-seven. Same disease as the Bitkom figure from Tuesday.

The success number needs a recheck too. Seven in ten customer-service chats are said to get resolved by agents with no human involved, and at the same time thirty-two percent are said to get escalated to a human. Together that's a hundred and two percent, so these can't be two halves of the same population. The honest summary: roughly three in ten conversations still land with a human. My take on all this: the real event in this survey isn't the agent count, it's the build time. Two days is shorter than any approval process I've ever seen at an SME. If something that can trigger six business actions gets built in two days, things like that are now getting created faster than anyone can document them.

The third angle is infrastructure, and there were two stories there. Google's agent-to-agent protocol, A2A, moved into the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation on August twentieth — the same foundation that hosts Anthropic's MCP. The difference between the two is simple: MCP governs how an agent reaches the tools and data inside its own house. A2A governs how an agent asks an agent in a different house to get something done. All three major clouds already support A2A. For a buyer, the relevant question isn't who invented the protocol, it's who owns it once it gets inconvenient. A protocol housed inside one vendor is a promise. One housed in a foundation is an agreement.

And on August nineteenth, AWS brought web search for its agents to Europe — Ireland, specifically — and to Tokyo, along with domain and date filters and an allowlist of up to a hundred domains. The most interesting feature there isn't the region, it's the allowlist. An agent allowed to read only a hundred handpicked sources is fundamentally different from one that reads the whole internet: it's auditable. You can show the list to an auditor. One side note: the widely repeated claim that this search feature went generally available on August twenty-first couldn't be confirmed. It's been available since June.

One more figure, from Brazil. President Lula announced a two-point-five-billion-real infrastructure package near Natal on August twentieth: a supercomputer worth about one billion in Rio Grande do Norte, a data center in Rio for one-point-two-seven-six billion over five years, built with the research network RNP, with Huawei, and with the Chinese firm iFlytek, operational in twenty twenty-seven, plus a hundred twenty-five million for patent-free chips. The instructive part is the contradiction: a country building national independence, and building it, in its single largest project, with a Chinese corporation. Sovereign, in other words, isn't a state you're in — it's a statement about who you depend on and how fast you could switch.

That's where the two project ideas come from. The first: the curated source list. Which hundred domains an AI assistant is allowed to read inside an audited operation, how old a hit is allowed to be, and a document explaining why. Pure assessment work, feasibility high, and it sells to quality and data-protection officers, not to IT. The second: the agent bridge to the supplier. One process between two companies over A2A, and the hard part isn't the code — it's the two-sided agreement over who's allowed to ask what and who's liable. Feasibility, honestly, is medium, because two companies both have to buy in.
