Fifty Billion for Tech, Three for AI

On 2026-08-25, in the middle of Febraban Tech in São Paulo (Aug 24–26, Distrito Anhembi), Brazil's banking association published its annual tech survey — conducted by Deloitte. The headline figure that ran through the press: Brazil's banks plan to put roughly R$ 3 billion into "Inteligência Artificial, Analytics e Big Data," up 8% from 2025.[1] More precisely: R$ 2.97 billion, up from R$ 2.76 billion.[3]

The more interesting figure sits right next to it and was barely quoted. The sector's total tech budget for 2026 runs to roughly R$ 50 billion[2] — meaning the AI line is about one-sixteenth of it. And it's growing at exactly the same 8% as the total pool.[2] What hasn't happened, in a year full of talk about the "agentic transformation of banking": AI taking money away from other line items.

What is taking money away is the cloud: R$ 3.9 billion for migrating systems and data, up 30%.[1][2] So the banks are spending more on laying the pipes than on what's supposed to flow through them — and the pipe budget is growing nearly four times as fast.

Why cloud comes before AI: "Cloud migration" means moving data and programs out of your own data center and into rented ones — and, in the process, rebuilding them so that systems that ran side by side for 30 years without talking to each other can finally read one another. Picture it this way: before you buy a dishwasher, you need a water hookup in the kitchen. The dishwasher is the exciting part in the brochure, but the plumber's bill is the bigger one. A model can only work as well as its data is reachable, connected, and current — that's exactly what the cloud line item pays for.

That fits with what the banks themselves report as the benefit: 92% name „ganho de velocidade na execução de tarefas rotineiras" — a gain in speed on routine tasks — as the top perceived benefit of adopting AI.[1] No new product, no new revenue channel, no new risk class. Doing the same thing, faster.

On the headcount side, the survey contradicts the usual narrative: 42% of banks say they intend to expand the number of IT staff they hire, precisely because AI adoption is accelerating; IT staff currently make up roughly 11% of the sector's workforce.[3] At the same time, the labor dispute with bank employees continues — so employment isn't growing everywhere, it's shifting.

Methodology Three caveats worth keeping in mind. First, these are planning figures: "pretendem investir" — intend to invest. Not audited spending, but institutions' stated intentions for the current year. Second, the survey is run by Deloitte on behalf of the association — the same firm that sells AI-adoption projects to these exact banks. That doesn't make the numbers wrong, but the questionnaire wasn't collected neutrally. Third, "AI, analytics, and big data" is a combined bucket; how much of it is a language model and how much is a 2019-vintage analytics tool doesn't show up in the summary.

My take. This is the most usable figure of the week, because it comes out of a budget, not an opinion survey. An entire, highly digitized banking sector is setting aside roughly 6% of its tech budget for AI and letting that line grow in lockstep with everything else. That isn't a sign of disillusionment, it's a sign of normalization: AI has arrived in procurement and is being treated there like databases or network gear. Anyone still selling "exponential adoption" today is selling a curve that doesn't show up in real budget plans.

Many Have Access, Few Have Production

Deloitte's "State of AI in the Enterprise" is out in its 2026 edition: 3,235 business and IT executives surveyed across 24 countries.[4] Three figures from it combine into a picture that none of them shows on its own.

60% have access → 25% have production → 84% haven't rebuilt

The share of employees with access to sanctioned AI tools has risen 50% and now sits at roughly 60%.[5] But only 25% of organizations have moved at least 40% of their AI trials into production.[5] And 84% haven't redesigned a single job or workflow around the new capabilities yet.[5]

The fourth figure makes it unambiguous: 74% of organizations hope to grow revenue with AI — 20% are already doing so.[4] Otherwise cited: productivity and efficiency gains (66%) and cost reductions (40%); only 34% use AI to fundamentally change their business model.[4]

Methodology The fieldwork ran from August to September 2025[4] — the report is titled "2026" but describes the state of things from late summer of the prior year. In a field where tools change quarter by quarter, that's not a minor detail. Second, all of it is self-reported by executives about their own organization: "productivity gain" here means "an executive checks a box saying they see one," not "a measured turnaround time went down." The 66% and the 40% are impressions; the 25% and the 84%, by contrast, are structural statements about the organization's own operations — I consider the latter more reliable.

My take. The gap between "74% hope" and "20% do" is the industry in one sentence, and the 84% explains it. A tool bolted onto an unchanged workflow can, at best, shorten a few individual steps — it can't make the process cheaper, because the process itself hasn't changed. On 2026-08-23, this same funnel showed up here from a different survey; the fact that two independent datasets show the same shape is the real finding. In practice, that means for a Mittelstand firm: "which model?" is the second question. The first is which workflow you're willing to change.

"Uses or Tests" Is a Very Wide Door

The KI-Index Mittelstand 2026 — surveyed jointly by the Deutscher Mittelstands-Bund and Salesforce, roughly 700 companies — reports: 51.2% of Mittelstand firms use or test AI solutions, up from 33.1% in the 2024 survey. That's a relative increase of roughly 54%. AI agents are in use at 16.6%, up from 8.7% — nearly doubled.[6]

Caution: the wording "Use or test" is the broadest definition imaginable: a single team subscription that three people are trying out counts exactly the same as a process running in production. Read next to Deloitte's 25% production rate, this figure mostly measures the spread of access, not of results. On top of that: fieldwork from November 2025, and Salesforce is a co-publisher — a company whose product line is exactly the agents whose spread is being measured here.

My take. The direction is right and matches Deloitte: access is spreading fast. The more reliable of the two figures is the smaller one — 16.6% agent usage. Anyone running an agent has made at least one decision about permissions, approvals, and error handling; that's more than a subscription. As a figure from an interested party, I'd read it as a ceiling, not a floor — and it's still the best data point there currently is for the German Mittelstand.

Where It Pays Off, the Assignment Is Small

Two counterexamples that, at first glance, have nothing to do with each other — and, on the second, share the same shape.

German municipalities. In the Federal Digital Ministry's Agentic AI Hub, 19 municipalities worked with nine AI startups across 20 pilot projects, running March to May 2026. Processing time for complex GDPR disclosure requests fell by "more than 90 percent." 70% of the solutions built are in operational use, 80% were already working with real municipal data, and 65% were deployed in under four weeks. For meeting minutes, manual post-processing dropped by roughly 78%; seven municipalities modeled nearly 600 administrative processes in twelve weeks.[7]

These figures already ran in this brief on 2026-07-25. I'm deliberately bringing them back, because they only explain something once set next to Deloitte.

Because that's exactly where the contradiction sits: 70% operational versus Deloitte's 25% that get more than 40% of trials into production. The difference isn't German administrative excellence, it's scope. A GDPR disclosure request is a self-contained process: it has a trigger, an end, a statutory deadline, and a measurable duration. "An AI-powered company" has none of that.

An American candy maker. Hershey has its field reps photograph seasonal displays and runs the images alongside point-of-sale data to work out which display format sells best in which spot in the store — by its own account, in "several thousand stores." Result from the s'mores season: the "pallet trains" outperformed the end caps by 101%.[8]

End cap and pallet train: The end cap is the shelf at the head of an aisle — the classic, expensively bought promotional spot. A pallet train is a row of pallets set one behind the other right in the walkway, impossible to miss. Both are "secondary placements": stock set up somewhere in addition to its regular shelf spot, for which the manufacturer pays the retailer. The question of "which format, in which spot" used to be a matter of gut feel. Here, for the first time, it's been answered with photos and receipts.
Context The 101% is a manufacturer's own figure, reported by Modern Retail, with no independent verification and no disclosed sample size or baseline. I'm citing it as exactly what it is: a claim by Hershey about Hershey.

My take. A government office and a candy conglomerate, and both did the same thing: cut out one decision, made it measurable, and left everything else alone. That's the only design pattern that has reliably produced numbers in this brief for weeks now. Anyone who starts with a Mittelstand firm's "AI strategy" ends up in Deloitte's 84%. Anyone who starts with a single, countable process has something to show after four weeks — in the Hub, 65% of the pilot solutions were deployed in exactly that time.

What Actually Binds Today

Since 2026-08-02, the transparency duties under Article 50 of the EU AI Act have been in force: anyone operating a system that people interact with, or generating synthetic content, has to disclose it.[9] Violations can draw fines of up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.[9]

What did not take effect the same day is the part everyone was dreading.

Annex III / "high-risk": Annex III is simply a list of use cases where the EU says: a wrong decision here can turn someone's life upside down — biometric identification, applicant screening and dismissals, education and exam grading, access to essential services, migration and border control. Anyone deploying AI there has to demonstrate risk management, logging, data quality, and human oversight — not just claim it. For a small firm, that's the expensive part of the law.

These duties have been pushed to 2027-12-02 via the Digital Omnibus package agreed in May 2026.[10] That's a good sixteen extra months — genuine regulatory relief, not just a paper extension.

My take. The balance here is remarkably lopsided. The expensive, labor-intensive part has been pushed back; the part that's already binding today is cheap — essentially one sentence in a chat window and a label on generated content — and still gets overlooked. For a Mittelstand firm running a customer chat on its website, this is the shortest duty checklist the AI Act will ever have. Anyone who doesn't clear it now will clear it later, under supervision.

The Brazilian counterpart, with a caveat. A Brazilian trade blog reports that the data-protection authority ANPD is planning roughly 20 AI-specific inspections over 2026–2027, and that an algorithmic impact assessment would become mandatory for high-risk areas (health, credit, hiring, the justice system, education).[11] That sits in draft legislation, so it isn't law yet, and the source is a blog with no primary citation. I'm carrying it as a watch item, not a fact — and won't use it as a hard number until it shows up on gov.br/anpd.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Process Map — Know What There Even Is to Automate First

ELI5 & value: Before a company can decide which process an AI should take over, someone has to know which processes even exist: how often they run, how long they take, where they get stuck, who's involved. At most Mittelstand firms, none of that is written down anywhere. That's exactly why, per Deloitte, 84% of organizations are still stuck at "bought the tool, workflow unchanged"[5] — and a bolted-on license doesn't save anything. The product is the inventory that makes the rebuild plannable: 20 to 40 named processes, each with frequency, turnaround time, roles, systems involved, and exceptions, ranked by automation value. The client isn't buying software, they're buying a basis for decisions.
Prototype (V0): (1) Two weeks of hands-on fieldwork in one department — interviews, screen recordings, the existing forms and mailboxes as supporting material. (2) Transcribe and structure it with a language model into a machine-readable schema, BPMN-adjacent but deliberately coarse: trigger, steps, roles, systems, exceptions. (3) Build volume figures from whatever's accessible without an IT project — mailbox counts, ticket-system exports, the Excel lists that get kept anyway. (4) Score each process by frequency × duration × error-proneness × data availability. (5) Deliverable: one document, a prioritized backlog, and three processes with effort estimates. Deliberately out of scope: implementation, connectors, ongoing monitoring. Building blocks: a strong language model for structuring, BPMN as the exchange format, no platform of its own.
Market & gap: The category is called process mining and it's well occupied — Celonis, SAP Signavio, UiPath, Microsoft Power Automate Process Mining. But their approach hinges on a precondition Celonis itself describes this way: „process mining software extracts event log data from your informational systems", based on „the data in your systems of record".[12] That's exactly where the German Mittelstand drops out: the expensive processes there run in Outlook, in Excel, and in two people's heads — not in an ERP's event log. That the alternative holds up is exactly what the Agentic AI Hub showed: seven municipalities modeled nearly 600 administrative processes in twelve weeks[7] — captured by hand, not derived from logs. The gap for twiceD: hands-on fieldwork for firms with no event log and no appetite for a platform subscription.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. This is tool-assisted consulting work, not an operation: once delivered, there's nothing to maintain, no uptime to guarantee, no client data to host — the fieldwork can run on-site and on the client's own hardware, which doubles as the data-protection argument. Effort per engagement: three to four weeks, eight to ten of those days on-site with the client. Scaling comes via a second field consultant per engagement, not via headcount growth. Honest risk: the deliverable is a document. Without a follow-on contract, it's one-time revenue — the price has to account for that, rather than betting on a project that might never come. celonis.com · staedtetag.de · digital-skills-jobs.europa.eu

2 · The Shelf-Placement Receipt — Which Secondary Display Pays Off, Without a Corporate Budget

ELI5 & value: When a food or drugstore-goods manufacturer runs a seasonal campaign, it decides in what form its product gets an extra placement in the store: an end cap, a pallet display in the walkway, a counter display. Retailers get paid for those spots — they're a real cost line — and to this day, the decision usually gets made by habit. For the s'mores season, Hershey measured exactly that: field-rep photos plus point-of-sale data, run through image recognition, across several thousand stores; the pallet train beat the end cap by 101%.[8] The value for a German brand manufacturer: a budget that flows every year finally gets a receipt.
Prototype (V0): (1) One brand, one seasonal campaign, one retail chain. (2) Run the photos the field reps already take through an off-the-shelf image model — not at the SKU level, just three questions: which placement format, which spot in the store, how many facings. (3) Merge that with the per-store sales data the brand gets for its campaign anyway. (4) Deliverable: a ranking of placement formats with sample size and an honest uncertainty statement. Deliberately out of scope: shelf-gap detection, planogram compliance, competitor share, real time. Building blocks: an off-the-shelf image model with no custom training, an analysis script, a static report — no platform.
Market & gap: Occupied and well-funded. Trax is the incumbent for shelf image recognition and merged with FORM in 2026; the customers listed on its own site are Anheuser-Busch InBev, Unilever, Heineken, and Labatt.[13] Also in the space: Neurolabs, Vision Group Retail, Asseco/Mobile Touch. That customer list is exactly the gap: these are standing subscriptions for global corporate brands with their own retail-execution team. A manufacturer with €60 million in revenue and twelve field reps doesn't want a platform subscription, it wants one answer per season. The second part of the gap: now that image models can recognize display formats with no custom training, the historically expensive piece — the SKU-precise recognition model — simply isn't necessary for this question.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium — and the reason for "medium" isn't the technology. It's solo-buildable because no model gets trained and the engagement is seasonal rather than ongoing; maintenance load between seasons is near zero. The hard part is the data: per-store sell-through numbers sit with the retailer, and whether the brand can get them at a usable resolution determines the entire value of the exercise. That belongs in the first conversation, not the proposal — if the data doesn't come through, the idea is dead for that client, and that's worth knowing before doing the work. Scaling comes via additional brands per season, with a contractor for data prep. traxretail.com · modernretail.co

It's Friday, August twenty-eighth. There's no single headline today — instead, something rarer: three surveys from three different parts of the world, all showing the same thing from three different angles. Artificial intelligence has become a line item in the budget. Not a rebuild.

The clearest number comes from Brazil. The banking association Febraban published its annual technology survey on Tuesday, conducted by Deloitte, right in the middle of the industry's trade show in São Paulo. Brazil's banks plan to put around fifty billion reais into technology this year. Of that, just under three billion goes to artificial intelligence, analytics, and big data. That's about one-sixteenth of the budget, and it's growing eight percent — exactly as fast as the whole pool. After a year full of talk about the agentic transformation of banking, artificial intelligence isn't taking money away from any other line item.

What is getting more money is the cloud. Three point nine billion reais for moving systems and data, up thirty percent. So the banks are spending more on laying the pipes than on what's supposed to flow through them, and the pipe budget is growing almost four times as fast. That's not disappointment, that's the right order of operations: a model can only work as well as its data is reachable. Fittingly, ninety-two percent of institutions name plain speed on routine tasks as the top benefit. No new products, no new revenue.

The second survey is Deloitte's global enterprise report, three thousand two hundred thirty-five executives across twenty-four countries. Sixty percent of employees now have access to sanctioned AI tools, up by half. But only twenty-five percent of organizations have moved more than forty percent of their trials into actual production. And eighty-four percent haven't redesigned a single workflow around the new capabilities yet. The fourth number makes it unmistakable: seventy-four percent hope for more revenue from artificial intelligence, twenty percent actually see it.

Both studies reward a look at the fine print, and that's really today's point. Deloitte's report is titled twenty twenty-six, but the survey ran in August and September of twenty twenty-five. Germany's KI-Index Mittelstand is also titled twenty twenty-six, but it was fielded in November twenty twenty-five — jointly with Salesforce, a company that sells exactly the agents whose spread it's measuring. The index reports that fifty-one point two percent of Mittelstand firms use or test artificial intelligence. That "or test" is a very wide door.

Then there are two counterexamples, and on a second look, they share the same shape. In Germany, nineteen municipalities ran twenty pilot projects with nine startups. Processing time for complex data-privacy disclosure requests fell by more than ninety percent. Seventy percent of the solutions are in day-to-day use, sixty-five percent were deployed in under four weeks. In the United States, Hershey had its field reps photograph seasonal displays and ran the numbers against point-of-sale data. Result: pallets set out in the walkway outsold the shelf at the end of the aisle by one hundred and one percent.

A government office and a candy company, and both did the same thing. They cut out one single decision, made it measurable, and left the rest alone. That's exactly the difference from Deloitte's eighty-four percent. A disclosure request has a trigger, an end, and a legal deadline. An "AI-powered company" has none of that. Start with a strategy at a mid-size firm, and you land in the statistics. Start with one countable process, and you have something to show after four weeks.

On the legal side, the balance is strikingly lopsided. Since August second, the transparency duties under Article fifty of Europe's AI Act have applied: run a chatbot, or generate synthetic content, and you have to disclose it. Fines up to fifteen million euros or three percent of worldwide annual revenue. The expensive part, though — the high-risk duties for hiring decisions, creditworthiness, or exam grading — has slipped, via the Digital Omnibus package, to December second, twenty twenty-seven. The cheap part is binding today and gets overlooked. The expensive part moved sixteen months further away.
