Two Tools Cleared Out in Five Days

Today, 2026-08-30, OpenAI is permanently removing the DALL·E GPT from ChatGPT. It was announced on 07/31, so users had roughly 30 days to save any images they'd generated there. The replacement is ChatGPT Images, which runs on the newer gpt-image-1 and gpt-image-1-mini models.[1][2] Four days earlier, on 08/26, o3 had already disappeared from the model picker in web and mobile ChatGPT — 90 days after the announcement in the model release notes on 2026-05-28, with the stated reasoning that OpenAI was retiring "older models with limited usage."[3]

Deprecation: A vendor declares a product end-of-life and shuts it down on a fixed date. In software, that's normal and usually harmless — with a library, the old version stays in the archive, you just can't update it anymore. With a hosted tool, it's the opposite: on the cutoff date, it isn't old, it's gone. Picture it this way: the difference between "your toolbox stops being developed further" and "someone takes the toolbox out of your hands."

On its own, neither story is a big deal. The DALL·E GPT had effectively been superseded since ChatGPT Images launched in November 2025, and o3, by the vendor's own account, barely had any usage left. What's interesting is the shape of it: in both cases, a cleanly announced window ran its course — 30 days here, 90 days there — and in both cases, the vendor alone decided when that clock started.

Source situation OpenAI's primary source isn't accessible to me today: the release notes at help.openai.com returned HTTP 403 both to the research request and to my own attempt. So I'm basing the date on two independent secondary sources that agree on the 2026-08-30 cutoff and the 07/31 announcement — one of them (inc.com) was likewise only reachable via the search-result headline, not as a loaded page.[1][2] That's enough for me to say "this is what happened," not "this is what the vendor states verbatim." Anyone building a shutdown notice to their own customers on this should pull up the vendor's page themselves.

My take. The practical conclusion is unspectacular and still gets violated constantly: never build a business process on a vendor's product surface, build it on the interface underneath. A "GPT" inside ChatGPT isn't a platform commitment, it's a menu item. Anyone who had set up their image generation there now has a broken workflow and no contracting party to hold accountable for it. Anyone who ran the same thing through the API with a fixed model ID also gets deprecations — but with a model ID, a deadline, and a documented successor. The difference costs two extra days at build time and saves two weeks on shutdown day.

The One Deadline Brussels Didn't Move

The Digital Omnibus package pushed back the EU AI Act's heavy obligations: stand-alone high-risk systems under Annex III slip from 2026-08-02 to 2027-12-02, and those embedded in products under Annex I move to 2028-08-02. The transparency duties under Article 50 were explicitly left unmoved and have applied since 2026-08-02.[7] The law's own implementation timeline describes that day as the point where "the remainder of the AI Act starts to apply, except Article 6(1)."[8]

Provider vs. deployer: The law recognizes two roles. The provider builds the AI system — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google. The deployer puts it into use under its own name — meaning you, if there's a chatbot on your website. Picture it this way: the automaker is liable for the brakes, but whoever's behind the wheel is still responsible for the driving. In practice, that means: the fact that your provider hasn't built in a marking yet doesn't excuse you from identifying your chatbot as a machine.

Article 50 isn't a single labeling duty, it's a bundle of four strands: machine-readable marking of AI-generated output (a provider duty), disclosure at first contact that the user is communicating with a machine, information when emotion recognition or biometric categorization is used, and visible labeling of deepfakes and of AI-generated text on matters of public interest.[9] The fine range runs up to "15 million euros or 3 percent of worldwide annual turnover," whichever is higher.[9]

One exception is worth remembering because it hits everyday practice: for text, the labeling duty falls away "if the content has undergone a process of human review or editorial control and a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility."[9] So anyone who edits and takes responsibility for AI drafts is off the hook for text — not for images, audio, or a chatbot.

What I'm not claiming here Two points are too uncertain for me to state as fact. First, the widely reported transition period until 2026-12-02 for systems that were already on the market before 2026-08-02: it appears that way in an industry-blog write-up of the Omnibus compromise[13], while a German law-firm-adjacent piece from 2026-04-14 — that is, before the political agreement — explicitly does not attribute 2026-12-02 to Article 50.[9] The timing lines up; that doesn't make it confirmed. Second, the technical detail: that the practice guidelines require two-layer marking (signed, C2PA-style metadata plus an imperceptible watermark, because "metadata doesn't survive the processing pipeline") comes to me only from that same blog source.[13] Both points belong in front of a lawyer before any investment, not lifted from a newsletter. What both accounts agree on: the deployer's disclosure duty has applied since 2026-08-02, regardless of how far along providers are with their marking.

How Many German Companies This Actually Affects

The most solid figure I can primary-source today comes from Bitkom's own press release of 2026-03-11: 41% of companies with 20+ employees use AI, up from 17% the year before — another 48% are planning or discussing deployment. It's based on a representative phone survey of 604 companies fielded in calendar weeks 2–6 of 2026. The biggest hurdle there isn't the statute, it's data protection: strict data-protection requirements (77%), ahead of a skills shortage (70%), technical security requirements (61%), and a lack of market-ready solutions (43%).[11]

Methodology This too is a trade-association study: it's commissioned by Bitkom itself, and the figures are self-reported by the companies surveyed. Two independent secondary sources cite the same survey with identical 41% and the same 604 respondents, confirming the primary source.[12]

My take. The German majority still stands before adoption, not in the middle of it — and what's holding it back is data protection, not the technology. That's exactly why the Omnibus postponement is a double-edged message. For high-risk applications, some breathing room has opened up. For the everyday case — a chatbot on the website, AI images in the brochure — nothing has changed, but a great many companies will take away exactly the opposite from the headline "EU postpones AI duties." Today, that misperception is the bigger risk than the rule itself.

Two Open-Weight Models Against One Menu Item

That same week, two releases ran in the opposite direction. On 2026-08-27, Zhipu revealed that the anonymously tested model "Ox Alpha" is GLM-5.3-Flash, and released the weights — 320 billion parameters, 18 billion of them active, under an MIT license.[5] Its own positioning: it claims to beat its predecessor, GLM-5.2, "at one-tenth the price."[10] A day earlier, Alibaba had released Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, also with open weights, 125 billion parameters with 6 billion active, at $0.16 per million input tokens and $0.47 per million output tokens on the vendor's own cloud.[6]

Open weights + MIT license: "Weights" are the learned numbers that make up a model — the file that holds its capability. If they're open and carry an MIT license, you're allowed to download them, run them on your own hardware, modify them, and use them commercially without asking anyone. Picture it this way: the difference between a rental car and one you own outright. With a rental, the rental company decides when a model gets pulled from the fleet — that's exactly what happened to the DALL·E GPT today. Nobody can shut down a model you've already downloaded.

My take. The benchmark question — how good these things actually are — belongs in the ai-dev brief and gets answered there. For a deployer, something else counts, and it's written on the license line: one-tenth the price is a negotiating point, an MIT license is an insurance policy. I wouldn't advise anyone to switch providers over this right now — the tools already in use are good, and running your own infrastructure costs time you don't have when you're buying it in. But today provides the argument for why every serious AI process needs a second, vetted exit. A year ago, "just use open weights" meant giving up quality. This week, it's a price list.

Claudeforce — The Move in the Opposite Direction

On 2026-08-26, Salesforce and Anthropic announced Claudeforce: an expanded partnership in which Claude becomes the default reasoning model for Salesforce's agents. Claude powers Agentforce Vibes and Agentforce Coworker "by default," is available in Agent Builder, and, per the coverage, becomes the "intelligent backbone" of the Slack experience, Slackbot included. Alongside that comes a "Salesforce in Claude" plug-in with 37 prebuilt sales skills — meeting prep, deal health review, pipeline review. Select pilot customers are already using it; open beta is announced for September 2026.[4]

My take. For a Salesforce customer, this is good news at first glance: better agents without doing the integration yourself. The structural story is a different one. The capability that carries the process moves one layer deeper into the platform — and becomes a decision the customer no longer makes. Anyone building in Agentforce today doesn't just have a Salesforce dependency the day after tomorrow, they have a Salesforce-plus-Anthropic dependency, and they never bought into that second part. That's not a knock on Salesforce; bundling is the normal way platforms grow. But laid side by side, this week paints a clean picture: one tool vanishes via a menu item, one platform ties itself to one lab — and two labs put their weights out in the open. Anyone reading only the first two stories underestimates how cheap the counter-insurance has become.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Labeling Retrofit — Article 50 as a Change to the Publishing Workflow

ELI5 & value: Since 2026-08-02, any company running a chatbot or publishing AI-generated images and audio has to disclose that — as the deployer, regardless of what the model provider has built in.[7][9] Most firms treat this as a legal question and buy an opinion letter. But it's first and foremost a change at three points in the workflow: the chat widget's greeting, the image pipeline's export, and the log of who published what, marked how, and when. The value for the client isn't primarily the fine range of €15 million or 3% of annual turnover[9] — nobody's pulling that trigger on an 80-person business anytime soon. The value is that the question, whether it comes up in the next customer audit, a supplier questionnaire, or a cease-and-desist letter, gets answered with a piece of evidence instead of a binder.
Prototype (V0): (1) Inventory: which systems produce customer-facing output — chat widget, marketing image series, newsletter copy, voicebot. (2) For each system, map it to the applicable one of the four Article 50 strands and check the text exception (editorial responsibility excludes text, not images or a chatbot).[9] (3) Retrofit exactly one chain technically: a disclosure text at first contact in the chat, C2PA content credentials on image export — and the point almost everyone misses: a test of whether the marking actually survives scaling, compression, and the CDN.[13] (4) A proof-of-compliance log as a file, not a slide deck. Deliberately out of scope: legal advice, high-risk classification, anything beyond one chain. Building blocks: the open C2PA tool c2patool, the respective model provider's marking, the CMS's existing publish hook.
Market & gap: The German market is densely covered with advisory and practically empty on implementation. KPMG Law publishes a list of questions on the transparency duties[15], the TÜV Academy explains "what Art. 50 actually requires"[16], and consultancies and IT service providers add readiness workshops on top.[9] None of those actors touch the client's CMS publish hook. And the technical situation makes exactly that last mile hard: per the accessible write-up, the practice code requires two-layer marking because metadata doesn't survive the processing pipeline, and there's no cross-vendor standard.[13] On top of that, the role trap: the deployer's disclosure duty already applies even where the provider's marking duty is still in a transition period[13] — so the client can't just wait for their provider to finish. The gap: someone who turns a legal situation into a change to three files.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. Two to three weeks per engagement, no hosting, no uptime commitment, nothing to operate after handoff besides an optional annual re-check — scaling via one contractor per engagement, not headcount growth. Two honest limits. First: this can't be sold as legal advice; Germany's Legal Services Act (RDG) draws a hard line here. The positioning is technical implementation alongside the client's own lawyer, and that belongs in the proposal, not the fine print. Second: demand is regulation-driven and goes quiet if enforcement stays lenient. So don't offer it as a stand-alone compliance service — attach it to a project that's already underway: a website relaunch, a CMS migration, a chatbot rollout. There, it's a two-week add-on rather than a new budget line. kpmg-law.de · die-tuev-akademie.de · kileague.de · compliancehub.wiki

2 · The Second Exit — A Vetted Model Fallback for Every AI Process

ELI5 & value: Today the DALL·E GPT disappeared, o3 disappeared four days earlier, and Salesforce just locked in the reasoning model for its agents.[1][3][4] Anyone who's put a business process on exactly one vendor's surface has inherited a calendar they don't control. The offering is explicitly not "get off the cloud." It's a limited second exit: for one business-critical AI process, there's a documented, measured fallback on open weights that can also run on the client's own hardware if it ever comes to that. The value at first isn't cost savings, it's survival — the process outlives a deprecation, a pricing round, or a veto from the data protection officer. And it survives them in days, not months.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick the one process whose failure would actually hurt (proposal copy, document classification, support pre-sorting), and write down its evaluation basis: 30 to 50 real cases with the expected output. Without that file, there's no fallback, only a hope. (2) Put the model call behind one interface — OpenAI-compatible HTTP is the de facto standard, which turns a switch into swapping a base URL instead of a rebuild. (3) Run the evaluation against the current model and against an open candidate: GLM-5.3-Flash (MIT license, weights available since 08/27) or Qwen3.8-Flash-Next ($0.16 / $0.47 per million tokens) — initially through a hosted endpoint, without buying a single GPU.[5][6] (4) Only once the evaluation passes and data protection requires it, run the numbers on the on-prem variant — honestly, hardware cost included. Deliberately out of scope: replacing everything, retraining, a dedicated GPU cluster in V0. Building blocks: vLLM or Ollama for serving, an OpenAI-compatible gateway, the evaluation as plain files in Git.
Market & gap: The sovereignty end of the market is occupied — but sized for someone else. Heidelberg-based Aleph Alpha sells exactly that with PhariaAI: hybrid execution between the client's own data center and the cloud, deliverable as an appliance into the customer's building, with contracts at the federal government, Bavaria's state administration, the Bundeswehr, and DAX corporations.[17] And Aleph Alpha keeps moving further upmarket: the merger with Cohere at a roughly $20 billion valuation, with about $600 million from the Schwarz Group and operations on its STACKIT cloud, was announced on 2026-04-24.[18] Infrastructure shows the same picture: STACKIT, OVHcloud, T-Systems — all sized for corporate and government scale. Consolidation widens the gap underneath it, it doesn't close it. Below that line sits the 80-person business with one process and a data protection officer who says no — and for that business, the right answer isn't a sovereign platform, it's a vetted way out for one process. That the demand is real is confirmed by Bitkom's own survey: strict data-protection requirements are the biggest hurdle (77%), ahead of a skills shortage (70%).[11]
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium — and medium, not high, on purpose. The build itself is solo work, four to six weeks, because the evaluation and the gateway are assembly, not research. The burden comes afterward: if twiceD operates the fallback itself, that's model operations — updates, monitoring, a response-time commitment, and hardware whose depreciation someone has to carry. One person can handle that for a handful of clients, not thirty. So the honest cut is: sell the evaluation and cutover as a project, leave operations with the client or a hosting provider; only run operations with a limited commitment (business hours, not around the clock) and only if the same model serves multiple clients. If that isn't enforceable, the idea fails the filter and shouldn't be sold. technode.com · innfactory.ai · techcrunch.com · bitkom.org

The second story matters more for a German company. Brussels' Digital Omnibus package pushed back the European Union's heavy AI Act deadlines — standalone high-risk systems now have until December second, twenty twenty-seven, and embedded high-risk systems until August second, twenty twenty-eight. But one set of rules was explicitly left untouched: the transparency and labeling duties under Article fifty. Those have been in force since August second of this year.

And they land on you as the deployer, not on your AI vendor. The law splits responsibility in two: the provider builds the system, the deployer puts it to use under their own name. Think of a carmaker and a driver — the manufacturer is liable for the brakes, but whoever's behind the wheel is still responsible for the driving. In practice, a chatbot has to identify itself as a machine on first contact, deepfakes and AI images need visible labels, and fines run up to fifteen million euros or three percent of worldwide revenue. There's one practical carve-out: text is exempt if a human reviews it and takes editorial responsibility.

On where Germany actually stands, I can back a fresh number today. Bitkom surveyed six hundred four companies with twenty or more employees this past March. Forty-one percent use AI, up from seventeen percent the year before. The single biggest obstacle isn't the technology, it's data protection — seventy-seven percent cite strict data-protection requirements, seventy percent a skills shortage.

Running in the opposite direction: two AI labs opened up their models this same week. On Thursday, Zhipu revealed that its anonymously tested model was GLM-5.3-Flash, and released the weights under an MIT license — three hundred twenty billion parameters, eighteen billion of them active. A day earlier, Alibaba released Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, also open, priced at sixteen cents per million input tokens and forty-seven cents per million output tokens. Open weights mean you can download the file, run it on your own machines, and use it commercially without asking permission — nobody can pull it out from under you the way OpenAI just pulled the DALL-E tool.

Pulling the other way: on that same Wednesday, Salesforce and Anthropic announced Claudeforce. Claude is now the default reasoning model across Salesforce's Agentforce products and becomes, in Salesforce's words, the backbone of its Slack experience, with thirty-seven prebuilt sales skills built in. Open beta is planned for September. It's good news for Salesforce customers on the surface, but structurally, anyone building on Agentforce now carries not just a Salesforce dependency, but a Salesforce-plus-Anthropic one they never explicitly signed up for.

How good these new open models actually are at coding and benchmarks — that's a question for the ai-dev brief, not this one.

Two project ideas for twiceD came directly out of this material. One is a labeling retrofit: treating Article fifty not as a legal opinion to buy, but as three concrete changes to a company's publishing workflow — the chatbot's greeting, the image export pipeline, and a proof log. The other is a second exit: a vetted, measured fallback on open-weight models for one business-critical AI process, so a sudden deprecation costs days instead of months.
