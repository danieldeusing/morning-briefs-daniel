Eight Billion Against an Organizational Problem

Within nine weeks, the four biggest vendors each set up a dedicated unit whose purpose isn't to build models but to get them running at customers: Anthropic on 05/04 with a $1.5 billion joint venture with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs, aimed explicitly at mid-size companies; OpenAI on 05/11 with a Deployment Company backed by over $4 billion from 19 investors, plus the acquisition of the consulting firm Tomoro with around 150 deployment engineers; Microsoft on 07/02 with the Frontier Company, $2.5 billion and about 6,000 engineers, consultants, and specialists, first customers London Stock Exchange Group, Unilever, and Land O'Lakes; Amazon around $1 billion.[1]

The same survey supplies the reasoning: 71% of surveyed executives name organizational maturity as the top hurdle, only 11% the technology itself; 85% report fragmented or only moderately integrated data.[1]

My take: This is an admission that the model isn't the product. Anyone putting $8 billion into people who clean up processes, merge data, and talk business units into using it has stopped believing in self-service. For twiceD, that's a double-edged sword, and both edges deserve naming. The good one: the market I'm selling into is now being marked by the vendors themselves as the real market. The uncomfortable one: Anthropic's joint venture is aimed, literally, at mid-size companies — that's my segment — backed by $1.5 billion and a model maker. What's left as the real difference is the incentive: a vendor-owned deployment unit's success metric is consumption of its own model. Neutrality isn't a marketing pose here — it's the one field where $8 billion structurally can't compete.

Methodology The survey is from PYMNTS Intelligence; sample size, selection method, and survey period aren't disclosed in the write-up reviewed here. The investment figures themselves are company-disclosed with dates — the 71% is self-reported by executives about their own company. That's a different thing from a measurement.

What Comes Out When It Works — and How Rarely Anyone Bothers to Calculate It

For the Q2 2026 earnings season, 22V Research reviewed all statements from S&P 500 companies that explicitly quantified AI's contribution. Result: 25 companies did so, with an average +180 basis points of margin improvement; strip out cases where AI was mixed in with other cost-cutting programs, and +150 bp remains.[2][3]

Basis Points & Margin: A basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point. 180 bp more margin means: out of €100 in revenue, €1.80 more sticks around than before. Sounds tiny — but for a company with $20 billion in revenue, that's $360 million that didn't have to come from anywhere. That's why this metric interests investors more than any user count.

Two named cases are solid. Willis Towers Watson cites process automation as the driver behind $400 million in cost reductions. Waste Management — a waste hauler, not a tech company — runs a Smart Truck platform for route optimization; the figure of roughly $300 million in ongoing EBITDA impact that's circulating, though, I only found via an aggregator, not verbatim in either primary write-up.[4] I'm including it, but not treating it as settled. Equifax cites cost and productivity gains without a figure.[3]

My take: The interesting number isn't 180, it's 25. Five hundred of the best-measured companies in the world, each with a finance department that depreciates every coffee machine — and only one in twenty could say, in the third year of the AI boom, what the stuff actually delivers. That's not evidence against AI. It's evidence that almost nobody measures the effect. Whoever can, wins the fall budget round; whoever can't, defends a line item with an anecdote. That's exactly where the first project idea below comes from.

Twelve Agents? Thirteen? I Don't Believe Either Number

Salesforce measures how many AI agents an organization runs on average, and its own two publications arrive at two different answers. The Agentic Enterprise Index reports a rise from 5 agents per organization in February 2025 to 13 in April 2026, plus 2 → 6 capabilities per agent and 300% more employee interaction.[9] A separate report on the state of integration and AI cites 12 agents on average and states that 83% of organizations have rolled out agents in most or all teams.[10]

Why I'm not treating this as a market figure Neither publication discloses a sample size or a survey method. The population is recognizably Salesforce customers using a Salesforce agent product — that measures product usage, not the market. And the 83% doesn't square with Gartner's 17%, unless the two are counting completely different things. One of the two sources also has contradictory dates for when the report was published.

The counter-number comes from Gartner, from the first Hype Cycle for agentic AI (2026-04-02): 17% of organizations have agents in production, over 60% plan to catch up within two years; agentic AI sits on the "Peak of Inflated Expectations."[4] The same paper contains the most useful term of the year for a buyer.

Agent Washing: An existing product gets slapped with the "AI agent" label without actually acting autonomously — usually old rules-based automation with a language model bolted on front. Like "handmade" on a factory package. The practical test before signing anything: can the thing decide for itself which step comes next, and what does it do when a step fails? If the answer is a flowchart, it's not agentic — it's a workflow, which can be perfectly fine, but should be priced differently.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Impact Proof — A Number That Survives the Budget Round

ELI5 & value: A mid-size company has had AI running somewhere in the building for a year and a half and can't say what it delivers. In the fall, the managing director asks, and the answer is a feeling. This idea sells the missing number: a clean before-and-after measurement for exactly one process, in euros or hours, that holds up under scrutiny. Analogy: not the fitness tracker, but the one blood value the doctor actually trusts. The moment of value is a specific date — the budget round. Of 500 US corporations, 25 could quantify the AI effect, and got to report +180 basis points of margin for it.[2][3] Whoever can measure gets more money; whoever can't defends an anecdote.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick a process that has a documented "before" state — quote creation, initial ticket triage, invoice intake. (2) Define the metric with the controller, not with IT: hours per case or euros per case, not "satisfaction." (3) Set measurement points at the process boundaries rather than inside the tool — timestamps at intake and closure, plus a quality sample so "faster but worse" shows up. (4) Choose a comparison baseline: the prior period, or casework still running the old way in parallel. (5) A one-page report every month, generated automatically from existing data. Deliberately out of scope: company-wide attribution, "AI maturity scores," anything that can't be traced to a single case. Building blocks: the existing database plus an analysis layer — deliberately nothing new to build here.
Market & gap: Measurement today happens in two places, and both measure the wrong thing. The platform vendors count usage: Salesforce reports 5 → 13 activated agents per organization and +300% employee interaction as progress — metrics that go up as more gets consumed, regardless of whether the customer actually saves anything.[9] And the new deployment units, backed by over $8 billion in capital, are vendor-owned; their success metric is consumption of their own model, and their first customers are called London Stock Exchange Group and Unilever.[1] So the gap isn't technical, it's structural: a small, affordable, vendor-independent measurement for a 200-person company that doesn't call an $8-billion unit. Tailwind: 71% name their own organization as the hurdle — organizational problems only get addressed once they have a number attached.[1] Not checked: whether any of the big consultancies offers a comparable small-scale format; I didn't pull up a solid source on that today.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. Pure consulting and integration work, no hosting, no uptime commitment, virtually no maintenance load — after handover, the report runs at the customer's site. V0 in two to three weeks per engagement, scaling via one contractor per engagement. Two honest limits. First, this is a day-rate business and will never become a product; revenue depends on Daniel's hours. Second, the real hurdle isn't code, it's access: without the controller at the table, there's no solid comparison baseline, and an unknown vendor only gets in front of the controller through a referral. That makes it a weaker first product and a very good add-on to an ongoing engagement. marketscale.com · fool.com · pymnts.com · salesforce.com

2 · SPARK for Everyone Else — Bringing the Federal Code the Last Mile

ELI5 & value: The federal government had an AI built that pre-checks permit applications: it reads the documents, says what's missing, and maps the content to the review steps. On 2026-06-16, the ministry released this software as fully open source.[6] So it's sitting out there for free — and, two months later, still isn't in live operation within the federal government itself.[7] Analogy: the state built the engine and put it on the shelf; what's missing is someone to turn it into a car that actually drives on a specific road. The value for the customer is concrete: the most expensive part of an application is the follow-up request — weeks of waiting because one attachment was missing. That's exactly what a completeness check catches before submission.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick one application type at one customer — a county administration, a municipal utility, a planning or engineering firm, ideally from the applicant's side, where the benefit is felt immediately. (2) Collect 50 to 100 real past cases, roughly half complete and half incomplete; that's both test material and a sales argument in one. (3) From the SPARK module set, take only the formal completeness check and run it against the procedure's real list of required attachments.[8] (4) Output is a missing-items report with a reference for each point, explicitly not an approval decision — the human decides, the machine searches. (5) The case worker's corrections flow back into the check list. Deliberately out of scope: substantive and legal review, decision drafting, anything that replaces an authority's decision. Building blocks: the SPARK code, a locally or German-hosted model, no in-house development of the checking core.
Market & gap: What's unusual here is the evidence: the client itself confirms that the technology exists and still isn't running. The BMDS describes a module set running from information extraction through formal and substantive completeness checks to legal review, and puts „halbiert Genehmigungszeiten" in the headline;[6] Behörden Spiegel reports projected processing times 50 to 80 percent shorter — right next to: „Ein Wirkbetrieb findet derzeit noch nicht statt."[7] So the gap isn't the model, it's the fit: a specific business process, a data-protection sign-off, training, a clear liability arrangement. And it's structurally protected: a county with eight people in its building department doesn't call a deployment unit whose first customers are named Unilever and the London Stock Exchange Group.[1] Open-sourcing flips the usual pitch on its head — not "we have the better AI," but "the federal government already paid for this AI, I'll get it running for you." Honest gap in my research: I didn't check today whether the established public-sector IT vendors already carry SPARK as an offering. That would need clearing up before the first sales conversation, since it determines the price.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium to high — and the caveat matters more than the grade. Building it is solo-feasible because the checking core is free: V0 in four to six weeks once the past cases are in hand. Operations is where it gets serious. If the check runs on-premise at the customer — which is the only sellable option for application data anyway — the maintenance load stays manageable and twiceD sells the setup, check-list upkeep, and an annual refresh. If twiceD hosts it, uptime, data-processing agreements, and record retention all get added on; a solo shop can only carry that for very few customers. Clear recommendation: ship on-premise, leave operations at the customer. Two risks remain — the public-sector sales cycle is long and formalized, and an AI system that contributes to an official benefits/permit decision quickly slides into the EU AI Act's high-risk category. That's exactly why V0 is deliberately limited to the completeness check: finding missing items isn't a ruling. bmds.bund.de · gitlab.opencode.de · behoerden-spiegel.de · pymnts.com
