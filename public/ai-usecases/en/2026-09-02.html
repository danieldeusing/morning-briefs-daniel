Google Builds In an Emergency Stop

On 2026-08-26, Google added usage-based billing alongside the existing per-seat subscription for Gemini Enterprise: no upfront commitment, no base fee — you're billed for compute time and token consumption at standard API rates. More interesting than the pricing model is what got built in alongside it. Projects get a monthly limit, and once it's hit, "agent API calls [are] temporarily paused" — the rest of the customer's production environment keeps running, only the agent stops. The administrator can manually restart it or knowingly allow the overage. There are also savings plans with a 10% discount for a one-year commitment and 20% for three years; tasks that can be deferred into off-peak windows get cheaper by "as much as half."[1][2]

Usage-based billing for agents: Until now, you paid per employee per month — predictable, like a monthly transit pass. But an agent keeps working unsupervised: it reads, thinks, calls tools, tries again. The bill is no longer tied to headcount, but to how often the machine thinks. That's the shift from a monthly pass to a taxi with the meter running — and nobody knows in advance how many loops the taxi will drive.

My take: A pricing page is a confession. Switching from seats to consumption shifts the volume risk onto the customer. And building a cap right next to it that stops a running agent mid-task means someone has seen how that plays out in the field. For any agent offering, that means two things: a ceiling belongs in the contract, not the ops documentation — the first invoice decides whether a project keeps going. And "stops when the money runs out" is a real design requirement: an agent that can halt at any moment has to be in a clean state at any moment. Half-finished bookings are more expensive than a budget that's too small.

The trigger making the rounds is Uber, said to have burned through its 2026 AI budget in four months after a coding tool went out to roughly 5,000 engineers.[2] Reported only — the page wasn't loading, and the tooling angle belongs in the ai-dev brief.

Two Continents, One Word: Reversible

On 2026-08-25, the IT services firm Aziro released the platform Aziron. The strongest line comes from deputy CTO Anand JK Jain: "The enterprise problem is not getting another answer. It is turning an answer into a governed action that finishes in the system of record."[8] It ships self-hosted, for either the customer's own server room or their own cloud, explicitly supporting local models too.[9] Two of the five modules are developer tools and belong in the ai-dev brief; what's interesting is the rest: execution, model routing with cost control, governance.

System of record: The system where something is officially true — the accounting ledger, the bank account, the personnel file. Picture the difference between a sticky note on the fridge and an entry in the land registry. An agent that only writes suggestions is touching the sticky note; one that posts transactions is touching the land registry. All the overhead of approvals, logs, and unwinding only shows up in the second case.

Almost simultaneously, the Brazilian fintech Conta Simples describes its offering "Faça Seu Agente" in the same terms: the people responsible describe, in plain language, what the agent should do, when it may act, and within what limits — and every action stays "registrada, auditável e reversível." My take: Two vendors, two continents, two weeks apart, the exact same sentence structure. The differentiator is no longer "the model answers well," it's "the action is permitted, logged, and reversible." That's not an AI feature, that's audit-proofing — limits, approvals, an event log, a reversal entry. And that's the good news for a small vendor: this part is boring software, not model training. Brussels Pushes Back — But Not the Part That Affects You

The "Digital Omnibus" has been in the Official Journal as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 since 2026-07-24 and has been in force since 07/27. It pushes back the AI Act's high-risk obligations: stand-alone Annex III systems from 2026-08-02 to 2027-12-02, and Annex I systems embedded in regulated products to 2028-08-02.[12]

Annex III versus Annex I: Annex III is stand-alone AI in sensitive areas — hiring decisions, creditworthiness, education, critical infrastructure. Annex I is AI embedded in a product that's already subject to inspection — machinery, elevators, medical devices. Analogy: a new tool on the shop floor versus a component in a device that has to go through an inspection anyway. That's why the second one gets more time: it rides along with an existing certification process.

Unchanged, on the other hand, is everything already in force or that started on 2026-08-02: the bans under Article 5 (since 2025-02-02), the general-purpose model obligations (since 2025-08-02), and the transparency duties under Article 50. Systems that were on the market before that date get a four-month grace period for labeling synthetic content — it ends on 2026-12-02.[12][13]

My take: "The AI Act got pushed back" will be the summary that costs the most money this year. What got pushed back is the expensive part, for a comparatively small number of companies. What stayed is the cheap part, for almost everyone: if a system talks to people or generates text, images, or voice, it has to disclose that and mark its output in a machine-readable way — and legacy systems now have just thirteen weeks left, starting today, to do it. The side effect is a market gap: the delay strips urgency from exactly the consulting offerings aimed at high-risk, while the obligation that hits the mid-sized business with a website chatbot is still due in December, unchanged. That's where the second project idea below comes from.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Approval Layer — One Process an Agent Is Actually Allowed to Execute

ELI5 & value: Almost every agent project at a mid-sized company dies on a single half-sentence: "…and then it posts the transaction." Not because the model is too dumb, but because nobody wants to put their name on it. This idea sells exactly that signature: for one process, a thin layer between the agent and the target system that enforces hard limits, routes anything above a threshold to a human for approval, logs every action in a tamper-proof way, and can undo any action it takes. The value is the sentence the client gets to say differently afterward: from "we can't let it do that" to "up to €500 it can, above that I sign off." Analogy: not a better driver, but the driving-school car with a second brake pedal.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick a process where every action is a reversible operation in exactly one system — posting and reversal, credit note, dunning stage, payment reconciliation. (2) Write the guardrails as a rules file, not as prompt text: amount limits per action and per day, permitted offsetting accounts, permitted operation types. (3) The agent proposes, the layer executes — the model never gets the write credentials. (4) Every action goes into an append-only log with a reason, and the reversal is defined in advance for each one. (5) Approval happens in the channel the client already reads, with a four-eyes threshold. Deliberately out of scope: multiple target systems, anything without a defined rollback, payouts to third parties. Building blocks: an existing ERP/bank interface, a model hosted locally or in the EU, the log as an append-only table.
Market & gap: The pattern is already being sold, in the same words. Aziro's Aziron (2026-08-25) explicitly promises "a governed action that finishes in the system of record" and ships self-hosted, local models included.[8][9] In Brazil, Conta Simples describes agents whose actions stay "registrada, auditável e reversível."[10] On the German sovereignty end of the market, the platform tier is already occupied: Langdock, based in Berlin, advertises "Fully compliant and hosted in Europe," ISO 27001 and SOC 2 Type II, quotes a Merck executive citing "more than 33,000 monthly active users," and lists BASF, Würth, Trumpf, and Der Spiegel as customers. But that's a platform for company-wide AI use — not a promise that one specific transaction gets executed correctly and reversibly. So the gap isn't "Germany needs one more platform," it's this: these offerings assume a customer with their own team. A 150-person company doesn't run a platform, it needs one signed-off transaction — and what wins the deal isn't the model, it's the most audit-proof log and the reversal entry. Not checked: whether the ERP vendors (SAP, DATEV, Sage) already ship an approval layer like this. That's the first question before the first client conversation, because it determines the price.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High — with one condition. The layer is deliberately boring software: rule checks, approval, logging, reversal calls. No model training, no GPU, no research risk. V0 in four to six weeks solo per engagement, scaling via one contractor per engagement. The condition is operations: as long as it ships on-premise and twiceD never holds the write credentials to the client's ERP, the maintenance load stays small. If twiceD runs it itself, availability, data processing agreements, and liability all come with it — a solo shop can only carry that for a very small number of clients. Recommendation: ship it, don't operate it. One risk remains: as soon as the process touches people, say in HR, it drifts toward Annex III.[12] That's why the V0 targets money and document processes, not decisions about people. itbrief.com.au · cioandleader.com · startups.com.br · langdock.com

2 · The December 2 Roadmap — While Everyone Thinks the AI Act Got Pushed Back

ELI5 & value: Ever since the Digital Omnibus landed in the Official Journal on 2026-07-24, everyone's been saying "the AI Act got pushed back." For the expensive high-risk part, that's true: 2027-12-02 and 2028-08-02, respectively.[12] For the part that touches almost every company, it isn't: anyone running a system that talks to people or generates text, images, audio, or video has to disclose that — and systems that were already on the market before have to meet Article 50(2) by 2026-12-02.[13] The value is a small, dated, tightly scoped deliverable that clears a legal risk ahead of a fixed date — thirteen weeks from today. Analogy: everyone's celebrating that the big vehicle inspection got pushed back two years, and missing that the sticker expires in December.
Prototype (V0): The product isn't software, it's a two-week inventory at a fixed price. (1) Inventory: every spot in the company where a model generates or answers something a human ends up seeing — website chat, email templates, marketing images, phone prompts, generated product copy. The list comes from invoices and installed extensions, not from IT's own self-reporting. (2) Map each entry against the three regimes currently in force — prohibited practices (Art. 5), transparency (Art. 50), general-purpose model obligations — and set aside anything that's Annex III or I, with its new date attached. (3) One concrete measure per entry: a disclosure text, machine-readable labeling, an addition to the terms of use. (4) Result: a dated two-page memo per system, plus a summary table for sign-off. (5) Optionally, a small maintenance contract for system changes. Deliberately out of scope: conformity assessment for high-risk, CE-adjacent work, anything involving a notified body.
Market & gap: The market exists and has names attached. TÜV SÜD offers a free AI Act Risk Navigator for risk classification, plus voluntary conformity certificates, training, and gap analyses; TÜV Rheinland Consulting sells advisory work on general-purpose models under Article 53. That tier targets manufacturers and high-risk systems — exactly the part that just slipped back by sixteen to twenty-four months and lost its urgency argument as a result. What didn't get pushed back is the boring transparency layer on the deployer side: the 200-person company with a website chatbot and generated product images. Nobody's selling them a small, dated engagement — too small for the certification houses, too legal for the ad agency. Source honesty: the TÜV SÜD press release returned a 403 today; the description rests on search-result summaries and the reachable TÜV Rheinland page. So "the offering exists," not verified in detail.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. Pure advisory work: no hosting, no uptime commitment, zero maintenance load after handoff. Three to five person-days per engagement, and the tedious part in particular — going through invoices, tool lists, and website integrations — can be handled heavily AI-assisted. Scaling via a template plus one contractor. Two honest limits: it's day-rate business and will never become a product, and the line to legal advice has to be respected — twiceD delivers the inventory and the measure, not the legal assessment; a lawyer needs to be involved for the wording. There's also a window: through 2026-12-02 it sells on the deadline, after that only on diligence. gibsondunn.com · artificialintelligenceact.eu · tuvsud.com · consulting.tuv.com

If you run a system that talks to people or generates content, you have to disclose it, and existing systems have until December second of this year to comply. That's thirteen weeks away. The sentence that'll cost the most money this year is going to be "the AI Act got delayed." Brazil is the mirror image. Banco do Brasil is rolling out a voice-assistant app, per a single regional source, since August twenty-fourth — Pix transfers, card blocking, limit changes. The data-protection authority published the first part of its regulatory sandbox report in July; there's still no dedicated AI law. In Europe the rules exist and the rollouts are missing. In Brazil, things ship and the rules are missing. Neither one holds you back — and neither one protects you either.

Two project ideas to close, both straight out of this material. The first is the approval layer: for exactly one process, a thin layer between the agent and the target system that enforces hard dollar limits, routes anything above a threshold to a human, logs everything in a tamper-proof way, and can reverse any single action. The model never gets write access — it only proposes. The second is the December second roadmap: a two-week, fixed-price inventory that lists every place in a company where a model generates something a human ends up seeing, with one concrete labeling fix per item. Both are solo-viable if you ship the work instead of running it.
