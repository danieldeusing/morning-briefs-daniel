Berlin Builds a Testing Bench [1][2]

Germany's Federal Ministry for Digital Affairs announced the founding of AISI Deutschland, the German AI Safety Institute, on 2026-08-31; work begins in Berlin on 09/01. It's backed jointly by the BMDS and the Federal Ministry of the Interior (BMI), based on a June 2026 decision by the National Security Council. At its core are two existing agencies with a shared mandate: the BSI covers the cybersecurity dimension, the Bundesnetzagentur the technical-safety side. Per the ministry, its tasks are: assessing the opportunities and risks of advanced models, organizing knowledge transfer across government, industry, and civil society, providing technical advice — and coordinating with France, the UK, and the EU AI Office.[1]

AI Safety Institute: A government body that tests AI models itself instead of taking a vendor's word for it — can the model be manipulated under attack, does it leak dangerous knowledge, does it break under load. Analogy: a materials-testing lab. It doesn't build bridges and it doesn't ban them either — it pulls on the steel until it snaps and writes down at what load. Important: an institute like this isn't a regulator — it can test and advise, not prohibit.

My take: The interesting part is the construction. Germany isn't founding a new agency, it's wiring together two existing ones. That's fast and cheap — and it reveals the intent: what's wanted is testing capability, not another jurisdiction. For a mid-sized vendor, that's more relevant than it sounds. Once the state has its own test procedures, they seep into tenders: first an agency asks for evidence of a model's robustness, then that agency's own customer asks for it, then it ends up in the spec sheet. That's exactly how BSI Grundschutz played out. What I didn't find: a single number. No budget, no headcount, no timeline for first publications. Without those, AISI is, for now, an org chart. The thing to watch: when does the first published test report land — and does it examine a real model, or just a procedure?

The Price of Listening Falls Below the Radar [3][4][5]

On 2026-09-01, Meta released Muse Voice Transcribe: a model that transcribes in real time, separates speakers — rated for 20+ voices — and detects on its own when a sentence has ended. Trained on more than 70 languages, of which 25 are, by its own account, extensively verified. Meta puts the error rate at 3.1% for the final transcript, delivered 0.16 seconds after the last spoken word.[3] The real break is in the price list: $3 per 1,000 audio-minutes — $0.18 per hour.[4] On the Artificial Analysis leaderboard for streaming speech recognition, the model currently sits in first place, ahead of Cartesia, ElevenLabs, OpenAI, and Google.[5]

Diarization and word error rate: Diarization means "who spoke when" — not just what was said, but assigning it to voices without knowing anyone's name. Analogy: a note-taker who doesn't know the participants but faithfully writes down "Speaker A," "Speaker B." Word error rate counts how many words out of a hundred are wrong, missing, or extra; 3% means three errors per hundred words — roughly the level of a skilled human transcriber with clear audio, noticeably worse with noise and accents.

My take: Run the numbers once and the whole way of thinking flips. Twenty people in phone support, six hours of calls a day, twenty-one working days: 2,520 hours a month, roughly $454 at list price — my own calculation off the published price, not a vendor claim. The same twenty people with an Otter Business seat cost $600 a month.[15] Full transcription of every customer call is now cheaper than the meeting-notes subscription for the same team. Quality assurance in call centers has run on sampling for thirty years because listening was expensive. That justification is gone. What's left is the second hurdle, and it isn't technical: works-council sign-off, caller consent, retention periods. The core point: the model itself is now a commodity; the value sits behind the transcript — evaluation, attribution, consequence — and in front of it, in permission. That's exactly where the first project idea below comes from. It's also true that the same model powers Meta's dictation feature and a coding tool, but that's a matter for the ai-dev brief.[4]

Cisco Delivers the Number Nobody Else Does [6][7]

On 2026-08-27, Cisco described what's happening internally on its own corporate blog: MyAgent is rolling out to 90,000 employees, and agentic interactions have grown "nearly 350% quarter over quarter."[6] Trade outlet PYMNTS independently confirmed the scale and date the same day.[7]

My take: The value of this story isn't the percentage, it's who's saying it. The default in AI marketing is a vendor's success story about an unnamed customer. Here, the company is publishing numbers about its own operation, under its own name, with a scale that can be checked later. That's the gold standard I keep demanding in this brief — and that's why the story runs here, even though it's a week old.

Two caveats: "rolled out to" isn't the same as usage — 90,000 activated accounts say nothing about how many people are actually touching the tool on a Tuesday afternoon. And +350% is a growth figure with no base number; going from 100 to 450 produces the same percentage as going from 100,000 to 450,000, and no starting figure is given. There's also a detailed technical breakdown of the MyAgent architecture circulating that reads like a Cisco number. It comes from a single post on X, appears in neither the Cisco blog nor PYMNTS — so it doesn't appear here either, not even as a quote.

Whoever Builds It Themselves Stops Buying [8][9]

McKinsey's annual survey "The State of AI" (published 2026-08-25, fieldwork 2026-05-04–2026-06-08, 1,719 respondents in 97 countries) yields a figure that wasn't public until yesterday and is the most important one this month for any software firm: 32% of organizations have declined to buy at least one software product or feature and built it themselves instead using agentic coding tools. Among the so-called "High Performers" — the 6% who attribute at least 5% of their operating profit to AI — it's nearly half, versus 31% among everyone else.[8] Then there's the size gap: firms with over $1 billion in revenue are now scaling agents in at least one function at a 40% rate (up from 27% last year), while smaller firms remain flat at 22%.[8]

Methodology & number caution Self-reported by executives about their own company, collected by a firm that sells AI transformation. "Declined to buy and built it themselves" is a statement of intent, not a bookkeeping entry — nobody verified whether the in-house project ever shipped, or whether the license got bought after all a year later. The original McKinsey page still wasn't loading today either (timed out); every figure here comes via a secondary write-up.[8][9]

My take: If a third of companies are canceling a software purchase because an agent can rebuild the feature in two weeks, that's bad news for standard SaaS and very good news for a small service provider. The reason is pricing structure: a SaaS subscription costs per user, per month, forever; a build-it-yourself project costs once, then maintenance. As build costs fall, the threshold at which building it yourself pays off shifts — and it shifts toward smaller projects. But that's exactly where the second figure gets in the way: large firms have moved from 27 to 40, small ones haven't budged. The tool got cheaper; the skill didn't. A mid-sized company can afford to build something in-house today — it just can't build it itself. That's not a market gap mentioned in passing, that's the gap, and it's where the second project idea below comes from.

Genesys: Foundation Now, Product in 2027 [10][11]

At its Xperience 2026 user conference, Genesys unveiled four building blocks for Genesys Cloud on 09/02: Navigator, Orchestrator, Contextual Intelligence, and an AI Control Plane. The virtual agent gets Scaled Cognition's large action model APT-2, new voice capabilities, and connections to "more than 500 enterprise integrations."[10] Availability: Contextual Intelligence and the AI Control Plane ship immediately; Navigator is slated for the fourth quarter of the current fiscal year (Nov 2026–Jan 2027), Orchestrator for the first quarter of the next one — Feb–Apr 2027.[11]

Orchestration in customer service: Not "a bot answers a question," but this: an issue moves across chat, phone, and a human agent, and the context never gets lost. Analogy: not the individual teller at the counter, but the case file that travels along with the issue — including a note on what's already been tried.

My take: Only one quarter separates the two dates — Genesys' fiscal year starts February 1, so Navigator falls into the last quarter of the current one and Orchestrator into the first quarter of the next. That's a tight roadmap, and that's the real story: the vendor is confident it can get the two pieces working together within eight months. What's being sold today is still the narrative; what ships is the foundation — the two parts available immediately are context storage and a control layer, exactly the pieces that do nothing without the other two. What's useful here isn't the product, it's confirmation of the pattern: competition in customer service no longer runs on answer quality, it runs on context retention and control. And the date sets a deadline: whoever builds a small-scale version of this for a mid-sized company should be standing in front of the customer before spring 2027, not after.

The Pesquisa Febraban de Tecnologia Bancária 2026, conducted by Deloitte and published on 2026-08-25, puts Brazilian banks' planned 2026 investment in AI, analytics, and big data at "cerca de R$ 3 bilhões" — roughly R$3 billion, up 8% from 2025. That lines up with McKinsey's finding: felt speed is everywhere, bottom-line impact is rare. Still, for a European vendor with a Brazilian address, the reading is favorable: a market that explicitly buys process acceleration is buying small, tightly scoped automations — not the big platform.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Full Call Capture — Every Customer Call Instead of Every Fiftieth

ELI5 & value: Almost every business with a phone-support team checks quality by sampling: a team lead listens to a handful of calls per week and fills out a form. The reason was never conviction, it was cost — listening was expensive. Since 09/01, an hour of audio transcribed by machine costs $0.18.[4] Twenty people in phone support with six hours of calls a day add up to roughly 2,520 hours a month, or about $454 for full transcription — my own calculation, not a vendor promise. The value for the customer isn't the transcript, it's what becomes answerable for the first time: which objection is costing us deals? What promise are our people making that isn't in the contract? Which three questions drive half of all callbacks? Analogy: not a better magnifying glass, but turning the light on.
Prototype (V0): (1) One business, one phone system, four weeks of recordings — settle the legal question beforehand, not after. (2) Transcription via the model API, with speaker separation, filed after each call; the audio gets deleted after transcription, only the text is kept. (3) A single analysis, defined by the customer — not ten metrics, but the one question the operations manager already asks every week. (4) A weekly summary with supporting evidence, every claim backed by a quote and timestamp. (5) Only once that holds up: a search field across all calls. Deliberately out of scope: evaluating individual employees, a real-time prompter during the call, CRM integration. Building blocks: the existing phone system, a transcription API (Meta's Muse Voice Transcribe, or for the on-prem variant an open model on a single GPU), a text database, a weekly report.
Market & gap: Two tiers are occupied, the middle one isn't. Above are the customer-service platforms: Genesys unveiled four building blocks aimed at exactly this on 09/02 — but the two decisive ones won't reach general availability until between November 2026 and April 2027, that is, within the next eight months.[10][11] Below are the meeting note-takers: Otter.ai charges $30 per user per month for its Business tier ($19.99 on an annual plan), with Fireflies.ai and tl;dv playing in the same space. But those are built for meetings: an attendee list, a calendar entry, minutes — not a thousand customer calls a month that nobody reads individually. And they charge per user, which is exactly backwards: the cost should scale with call minutes, not headcount. The market is growing at a solid clip, but it's no gold rush — one market-research forecast puts AI speech-recognition tools at $3.87 billion in 2026, growing roughly 17% a year.[16] The gap for twiceD lies neither in the model nor in the analysis, but in what comes attached to it in Germany: works-council co-determination, caller consent, deletion deadlines, purpose limitation — and the option to run the whole thing in-house. A US subscription with cloud storage isn't hard to sell to a German mid-sized company because it's bad.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium — and the caveat matters. The build itself is small: transcription, storage, one analysis, one report. Four to six weeks solo to the first solid weekly report. The real load sits elsewhere. First, legally: as soon as calls involving employees get recorded, the works council is at the table, and that's not a form to fill out, it's a negotiation — twiceD delivers the technology and the deletion concept, the client and their lawyer handle the works agreement. Second, operationally: anyone processing audio on an ongoing basis is carrying an uptime commitment. The recommendation, as before: ship and set up, don't run it long-term — on-premise at the client's site, twiceD maintains it for a flat fee. Scale via one contractor per engagement. One honest risk: if the price keeps falling, eventually the phone system itself will build this in. The window is good, but not unlimited. research.meta.ai · 9to5mac.com · otter.ai · fireflies.ai · precedenceresearch.com · itbrief.com.au

2 · The License Buyout — Cancel a Subscription, the Budget Is the Saved License

ELI5 & value: Today's McKinsey finding: 32% of companies have canceled at least one software purchase and built it themselves instead.[8] So far, almost only the large ones can do that — small firms have been stuck at 22% agent adoption for a year.[8] This idea sells the mid-sized company exactly that capability as a service, with an offer that needs no sales pitch: we find a subscription your company is using poorly, replace it with a small in-house tool, and cap the fee at whatever the license costs over eighteen months. Not a promise about the future, just one fewer line on the credit-card statement. Analogy: not selling a new car, but getting rid of the second one that just sits in the garage.
Prototype (V0): (1) Inventory built from the invoices, not the IT list — credit-card statements and recurring charges from the last twelve months. (2) Three numbers per subscription: paid seats, active seats, functionality actually used. (3) Pick one candidate that meets three conditions: narrow feature scope, no regulatory baggage, clean data migration. (4) Rebuild it in two to three weeks using agentic development, on the customer's own infrastructure. (5) Run in parallel for a month, then cancel on the cutoff date. Deliberately out of scope: accounting, payroll, anything with a retention requirement, anything with more than two integrations. Building blocks: an app platform for the interface (Retool, Appsmith, Budibase), the existing database, agentic coding tools for the build.
Market & gap: The waste is well documented, and it comes from the industry that's trying to measure it: Zylo's 2026 SaaS Management Index (2026-01-29) finds that organizations leave an average of 36% of their software licenses unused, and puts the spending growth for AI-native applications at +108% year over year — +393% at companies with over 10,000 employees.[17] So there are more subscriptions, not fewer. The catch: Zylo, Torii, and Vertice find the unused license and send a cancellation recommendation — they don't rebuild anything, and they target companies that already have their own license management. On the other side, the app-building platforms sell their own subscription: Retool's Business tier charges $50 per developer and $15 per internal user, per month[18] — so you're trading one subscription for another, plus the work nobody does. The gap is the 80-to-300-person company with no in-house development team: it has the subscriptions, it has the waste, but it has nobody to do the rebuild and stand behind it afterward. Not checked: whether DATEV, SAP, or the big systems integrators already offer something comparable. That's the first question to answer before the first client conversation.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium to high, depending on scope. The inventory phase is pure consulting, two to three person-days, zero maintenance load, and can be done heavily AI-assisted — it sells itself right away and funds the rest. The rebuild is the honest catch: afterward, the customer owns a tool that someone has to maintain, and that someone will initially be twiceD. That's manageable as long as one rule holds: one tool per customer, run within that customer's own environment, maintenance billed as a flat fee with a clear cap — and the source code plus documentation belong to the customer, so they can switch providers. Anyone maintaining ten self-built tools across ten customers at once doesn't have a product, they have a junkyard. Hence: at most one active rebuild per quarter, the rest is inventory work. finance.yahoo.com · zylo.com · retool.com

It's Thursday, September third. There's no fresh story from the last twenty-four hours — the real news happened on Tuesday. That day, two things happened that nobody's telling as one story, even though they belong together. In Berlin, a government AI-testing institute opened its doors. And Meta released a speech-recognition model that transcribes an hour of audio for eighteen cents. One is about building testing capacity. The other is about to make the volume of things worth testing explode.

Start with Berlin. Germany's Federal Ministry for Digital Affairs announced the founding of AISI Deutschland, the country's AI Safety Institute, on August thirty-first; work began in Berlin on September first. It's backed jointly by the digital ministry and the interior ministry, following a June decision from the National Security Council. At its core sit two existing agencies splitting the job: the Federal Office for Information Security covers cybersecurity, the Bundesnetzagentur covers technical safety. The institute coordinates with France, the United Kingdom, and the European Union's AI Office.

What an institute like this actually does is test models itself instead of taking a vendor's word for it — can the model be manipulated under attack, does it leak dangerous knowledge, does it break under load. Think of a materials-testing lab: it doesn't build bridges and it doesn't ban them, it pulls on the steel until it snaps and writes down at what load. My take: the interesting part is the construction. Germany isn't creating a new agency, it's wiring two existing ones together. That's fast and cheap, and it tells you what's actually wanted — testing capability, not another jurisdiction. That matters more to a mid-sized vendor than it sounds, because procedures like this tend to leak into government tenders: first an agency asks for proof a model is robust, then that agency's own customers start asking, and eventually it's just in the spec sheet. What I couldn't find anywhere was a single number — no budget, no headcount, no timeline. For now, AISI is an org chart.

The second story from that same Tuesday is the one that actually changes your business. Meta released Muse Voice Transcribe: it transcribes audio live, tells more than twenty different speakers apart, and knows on its own when a sentence has ended. It's trained on more than seventy languages, twenty-five of them thoroughly checked by Meta's own account. Meta puts its error rate at three point one percent for the final transcript — a vendor claim, not an independent test. The real break is the price: three dollars per thousand minutes of audio, or eighteen cents for a full hour of listening.

Run the math and it flips how you think about the whole category. Twenty people in phone support, six hours of calls a day, twenty-one working days a month — that's two thousand five hundred twenty hours, or roughly four hundred fifty-four dollars at list price to transcribe every single one of them. That's my own back-of-envelope math, not a vendor promise. The same twenty people paying for an Otter Business plan cost six hundred dollars a month. Transcribing every customer call is now cheaper than the meeting-notes subscription for the same team. Quality control in call centers has run on spot-checks for thirty years, simply because listening to everything was too expensive. That excuse just disappeared. What's left isn't a technology problem, it's a permissions problem — works councils, caller consent, how long you're allowed to keep a recording. The model itself just became a commodity. The value now sits behind the transcript, in what you do with it, and in front of it, in whether you're allowed to listen at all.

On August twenty-seventh, Cisco wrote on its own blog that its internal agent, MyAgent, is rolling out to ninety thousand employees, and that agentic interactions are up nearly three hundred fifty percent quarter over quarter. What makes this valuable isn't the percentage, it's who's saying it — a company publishing real numbers about its own operations, under its own name, instead of the usual vendor case study about an unnamed customer. Two caveats stand, though. Rolled out to isn't the same as used by — ninety thousand activated accounts say nothing about how many people are actually touching the tool on a given afternoon. And three hundred fifty percent growth with no base number can mean almost anything: the same percentage covers going from a hundred to four hundred fifty, or from a hundred thousand to four hundred fifty thousand. A detailed technical breakdown of the system now circulating as a Cisco number actually comes from a single post on social media and isn't confirmed anywhere else — so it doesn't appear in this brief, not even as a quote.

Then there's the number that matters most this month if you sell software. McKinsey's annual AI survey, published August twenty-fifth, one thousand seven hundred nineteen respondents in ninety-seven countries, found that thirty-two percent of organizations skipped buying a piece of software and built it themselves instead, using agentic coding tools. Among the companies that actually trace a bottom-line effect to AI, it's nearly half. There's a size gap hiding underneath, too: large companies — those over a billion dollars in revenue — have gone from scaling agents in one function twenty-seven percent of the time to forty percent within a year. Smaller companies haven't moved off twenty-two percent. Worth remembering: this is self-reported by executives about their own companies, collected by a firm that sells AI transformation, and "didn't buy it, built it instead" is a statement of intent, not a bookkeeping entry. My take: if a third of companies are canceling a software purchase because an agent can rebuild the feature in two weeks, that's terrible news for standard software and very good news for a small services shop. But the second number gets in the way — the tool got cheaper, the skill didn't. A mid-sized company can afford to build something in-house today. It just can't build it itself.

Three shorter customer-service stories, all pointing the same direction. Genesys unveiled four new building blocks for its cloud platform at its user conference on September second. Two ship immediately — a context store and a control layer. The two interesting ones come later: one between this November and next January, the other between February and April of twenty twenty-seven. Only one quarter separates the two dates — the roadmap is tight, but everything decisive still lies beyond this year. Two days earlier, in Korea, the telecom company KT began rewiring Woori Bank's AI services so its chatbot, advisory bot, and AI banker all share one conversation thread instead of starting from zero every time. KT already runs the AI call centers for four of Korea's five biggest banks. And in Brazil, a survey of the country's banks — conducted by Deloitte, published August twenty-fifth — found they're planning to spend roughly three billion reais on AI, analytics, and big data this year, up eight percent from last year. Ninety-two percent of them name the same main benefit: not new products, not better lending decisions, just faster routine work.

Two project ideas to close, both straight out of this material. The first is full call capture: transcribe every customer call instead of every fiftieth one, delete the audio right after, and track exactly one question the client already asks every week. The competition sits in two tiers that don't overlap with this — customer-service platforms with delivery dates stretching out to twenty twenty-seven, and meeting-note tools built for calendars and attendee lists, charging per head instead of per minute, and not built for a thousand untouched customer calls a month. The German-shaped gap in the middle is works councils, caller consent, deletion rules, and running the whole thing in-house. The second idea is the license buyout: find a subscription a company barely uses, replace it with a small custom tool, and cap the fee at eighteen months of that license's cost. One industry study found organizations leave an average of thirty-six percent of their software licenses unused — but the tools that find that waste don't rebuild anything, and the tools that let you rebuild it just sell you another subscription. The gap is the mid-sized company with no developers of its own; the hard rule here is at most one rebuild per quarter, or you don't own a product, you own a junkyard.
