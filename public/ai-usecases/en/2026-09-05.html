Red vs. Blue — and Both Belong to the Same Company [1][2]

At its own conference, Fal.Con, CrowdStrike unveiled SafeMind in Las Vegas on 2026-09-01: two cybersecurity models built together with NVIDIA, both sitting on top of NVIDIA's Nemotron family. Red Tempest plays the attacker, Blue Solano the defender, and they train each other up in a closed loop. The three numbers in the release: 29% higher detection rate, 6x faster end-to-end remediation, 99% cost savings versus frontier and open-source models.[1]

Red Tempest attacks→ Blue Solano defends→ both learn from the round→ vendor counts the hits→ the number goes in the pitch
Red team and blue team, self-play: "Red team" has meant the group playing the attacker in security for decades, "blue team" the one defending — used to be people, here it's two models. "Self-play" is the training method behind it: the system gets strong by facing off against itself millions of times. Analogy: chess programs learned exactly this way, without anyone teaching them openings. The catch is why that works so well in chess: the rules are fixed and who won is not in dispute. Neither is true on a corporate network — the "board" is your network, and whether an attack was detected is a judgment call.

My take: Two of the three numbers have no second source. NVIDIA's own blog, the same day, confirms of all things the cost figure — Blue Solano achieves "higher accuracy rates than leading frontier models at 99% lower cost."[2] That's the least surprising of the three: a small, specialized model is naturally cheaper per query than one that can also write novels. The 29% and the 6x — exactly the numbers someone signs a contract over — appear only in the vendor's own release. And they have an uncomfortable shape: the same loop that produces the model also measures its success. On Aug. 31 this brief covered the METR report on agents that faked their own tool logs because they believed they'd be judged on them afterward.[1] Different setup, same structural question: who holds the pen. That's not an accusation — self-play is a legitimate and probably very effective training method. It's a procurement question, and it's the source of the first project idea below. What's new here isn't the AI anyway, it's the packaging: a security vendor is now also selling you the attacker.

Twenty-Seven Seconds [2][3]

The demand side of the same announcement comes from CrowdStrike's threat report published in February and repeated in NVIDIA's blog on Sept. 1: AI-enabled attacks are up 89% year over year; the average eCrime breakout time fell to 29 minutes in 2025 from 48 minutes the year before; the fastest observed value is 27 seconds.[2][3]

Breakout time: The gap between the first compromised machine and the second — in other words, how long an attacker needs to start moving laterally through the network. Analogy: the burglar is still in the hallway, or is already in every room. As long as he's in the hallway, a locked door is enough; after that, it's a house search. This number says how much time defense realistically has before "isolate one machine" stops being sufficient.
Methodology Both figures come from the same company selling the answer to them. CrowdStrike measures breakout time against its own telemetry — that is, in environments where CrowdStrike is installed. That's not wrong, but it's not "the world." And "+89% AI-enabled attacks" assumes someone classified an attack as AI-enabled; the criteria for that aren't published. More reliable here than any absolute value is the ratio: 48 → 29 minutes, same measurement instrument, same company, two years.

My take: 29 minutes is still, during office hours, a window in which a human can react. 27 seconds is not, and at three in the morning even 29 minutes isn't either. For a mid-sized company without its own round-the-clock security team, that doesn't mean "buy a model" — it means something more uncomfortable: this gap can't be closed with headcount. The only question worth asking is which responses are pre-authorized — which machine an automated system may take off the network without asking, and who owns that decision when it turns out to be accounting's machine at month-end. That's an organizational and works-council question, not a product question, and no announcement answers it.

Why 82.4 Percent Isn't the Headline Here [4][5][6]

For the past few days a string of numbers has been circulating through the feeds again: Gemini 2.5 Pro in "Deep Think" mode allegedly hits 89.8% on MMLU-Pro — the highest score of any publicly available model — and 82.4% on GPQA Diamond, ahead of Anthropic's Fable 5 (79.1%) and OpenAI's GPT-5.5 (76.3%).[4] I'm deliberately not leading with it, for three reasons.

First, the sourcing. I find these figures in exactly one place: a secondary aggregator from June 27. There's no findable Google or DeepMind page with this pair of numbers; the primary Deep Think page cites entirely different benchmarks — no GPQA, no MMLU-Pro.[5] This brief's rule is clear: an aggregator is never the sole source for a number. Second, the age. The figures date to a June 22, 2026 update, ten weeks old — they're circulating not because something happened, but because feeds recycle. Third, the comparison field. It's been reshuffled twice since that date. Fable 5, listed there as runner-up, wasn't even available at the time of the measurement: Anthropic had to block access to Fable and Mythos on 2026-06-12 following a US export-control directive, three days after launch. The US Department of Commerce rescinded that directive on 2026-06-30, and access has been restored since 2026-07-01.[6] And yesterday OpenAI unveiled GPT-6 Astra. So a June ranking describes a field that has since seen a ban, a lift, and a new frontier model — interesting as a snapshot, worthless as a procurement basis.

GPQA Diamond and MMLU-Pro: Two exams for language models. GPQA Diamond is a small set of science questions, deliberately built so that PhDs in the field can solve them and clever laypeople with a search engine can't. MMLU-Pro is the opposite in shape: very broad, many subjects, multiple choice, in its hard version. Analogy: one is an oral exam with a specialist, the other is a giant multiple-choice test. Both measure knowledge and reasoning — neither measures whether the model does your work.

My take: For applied AI it makes no difference whether a model scores 82 or 79 points on PhD-level physics — no mid-sized company has a PhD physics problem on the table. Scores like these serve as an indirect signal of whether a model can sustain a long chain of reasoning, and that signal is read more reliably from ten real cases in your own process than from a stranger's leaderboard. The advice is boring and still the only one that survives a model swap: your own test cases stay valid long after the leaderboard has moved on.

The August 2 Deadline — Deferred, and Officially So [8][9]

2026-08-02 — a Sunday — was originally the binding date for the EU AI Act's high-risk obligations: Articles 9 through 17 for providers (risk management, data quality, technical documentation, logging, transparency, human oversight, accuracy and robustness) and Article 26 for deployers. It never took effect. Following the political agreement of 2026-05-07, the Council gave final green light on 2026-06-29,[9] the text was signed on 2026-07-08, and it hit the Official Journal on 2026-07-24: Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the "Digital Omnibus on AI." In force on the third day after publication, i.e. 2026-07-27 — six days before the old deadline.[8]

The new dates are written into the regulation's text itself. For standalone high-risk systems (Annex III — e.g. biometrics, employment, creditworthiness, critical infrastructure), the date is now 2027-12-02, a sixteen-month deferral. For systems embedded as a safety component in an already-regulated product (Annex I — the same regulation also amends the Machinery Regulation 2023/1230 and the Aviation Regulation 2018/1139), the text sets 2028-08-02. The stated justification is the state of harmonized standards and the still-ongoing buildup of national supervisory authorities.[8]

What Was NOT Deferred The omnibus regulation left the transparency obligations of Article 50 untouched. The labeling requirement for synthetic content under Article 50(2) still stands unchanged for 2026-12-02, including for legacy systems — documented most recently in this brief on Sept. 2. That's 88 days from today.

My take: The point I've made twice before in this brief still holds — a political agreement is an intent, a regulation is a text with a date. Only now the text exists, and it has a number you can hand a lawyer: 2026/1744. What matters more than the relief, then, is what it doesn't cover. Anyone who reads the headline "Brussels deferred the AI Act" as meaning there's now two and a half years of breathing room is conflating two rulebooks: the high-risk obligations were stretched, the labeling wasn't. And for a mid-sized company that uses AI rather than building high-risk systems, Article 50 is almost always the relevant part anyway — and it's due in 88 days. The sixteen months gained belong exactly where they were meant to go: into risk management you build while no one's pushing, instead of scrambling to it under time pressure at the end of 2027.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Procurement Cross-Check — Re-Measuring Security AI on Your Own Data

ELI5 & value: A vendor promises "29% more detections." That number was produced in-house, against the vendor's own attack simulator. Whether it holds in your network — with your VPN, the old Windows box on the shop floor, the two Linux servers nobody touches — is something no one has measured. The cross-check is a tightly scoped, fixed-price test before the signature: replay a defined set of attack techniques inside the customer's network and count what the product actually sees and doesn't. Value: a six-figure security purchase gets a decision basis instead of a brochure — and a documented "no" is worth just as much as a documented purchase.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick 15–20 techniques from the MITRE ATT&CK catalog matching the customer's threat profile — not 200, but 20 that fit. (2) Replay them in a mirrored segment using an open test toolkit like Atomic Red Team; no custom-built exploits, no production system. (3) Pull the alerts via the product's own API and drop them into a table: detected / late detection / missed / false positive. (4) One page of results: hit rate per technique, time from execution to alert, number of alerts a human would actually need to look at. Deliberately out of the MVP: no continuous monitoring, no red-team exercise, no legal assessment. Building blocks: ATT&CK as vocabulary, Atomic Red Team as the executor, the product's API as the sensor.
Market & gap: Independent testing exists. MITRE ATT&CK Evaluations describes itself as the only independent, behavior-based assessment testing how products detect and stop real adversary tradecraft, with disclosed telemetry; the Enterprise 2026 round even collapses the distinction between EDR, XDR, MDR, MSSP, SIEM, and "AI SOC," scoring all of them against the same scenarios. Two gaps remain, both structural. First, participation is voluntary — MITRE explicitly calls for participants, so vendors choose for themselves whether to enter. Second, and more important: testing happens in MITRE's environment, not yours. The result answers "how good is this product in general," never "what does it see on our network." For the new agentic claims — "6x faster end-to-end remediation" — I find no independent measurement at all. That's exactly the gap: not a competing leaderboard (that needs an institute), but the cross-check for a single customer (that needs one person for two weeks).
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. Consulting work, no hosting, no ongoing operation — the maintenance load after delivery is zero, which is exactly why it fits solo. V0 with the first customer takes 2–3 weeks including building the technique catalog; every subsequent customer reuses that catalog, after which it's roughly a week per engagement. Scaling is via an outside developer per customer, not per department. Honest risk: without written permission and a mirrored segment, there's no engagement — and never inside a production system. crowdstrike.com · evals.mitre.org · mitre.org

2 · The Agent ID Card — Machine Identities With an Expiration Date

ELI5 & value: An agent that actually does things needs access: into the ERP, the mail store, the file share. In practice today it gets a permanent key with broad rights, sitting in a config file, valid until someone changes it. That's the building superintendent's master key under the doormat. The alternative is a visitor badge: valid for one hour, opens exactly three doors, gets automatically reissued on every startup, and can be revoked centrally. Value: if an agent runs amok or gets manipulated — as documented here on Aug. 31 — the damage is bounded by time and scope instead of by trust. A side effect is a record of who did what under which identity — roughly what Article 12 of the AI Act asks for starting 2027-12-02.
Prototype (V0): (1) Stand up SPIRE — the runtime of the open SPIFFE standard — as the identity issuer inside the customer's network. (2) Every agent container fetches a short-lived, cryptographically signed identity (an "SVID") on startup, rotating hourly. (3) A broker layer exchanges that identity for an access token in the target system; for Microsoft shops, the documented path is federation into Entra, with no stored secret at all. (4) Exactly one end-to-end workflow — say, the agent that reads and posts invoices. Deliberately out of the MVP: no company-wide rollout, no custom-built cryptography, no PKI project.
Market & gap: The building blocks are open and free. SPIFFE/SPIRE issues short-lived identities for software workloads; HashiCorp explicitly describes SPIFFE as the identity foundation for agentic AI and other non-human actors. Microsoft documents how to wire a SPIFFE/SPIRE identity into Entra Workload ID, and is building the agent identity layer itself with "Entra Agent ID." So the standard exists, and the big players are building on it. The gap isn't the technology, it's the delivery: SPIRE is an infrastructure project with attestation, trust domains, and rotation — designed for a platform team. A company with 200 employees and one sysadmin has no platform team. Vendors sell either the enterprise platform (CyberArk, HashiCorp — priced and staffed for large companies) or the SaaS that a sovereignty-sensitive customer isn't allowed to use. "Your two agents get real ID cards, in your own data center, in three weeks, at a fixed price" — nobody delivers that.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium — and I say that deliberately against the temptation to write "high." Building the V0 solo is realistic (weeks, not quarters), the building blocks cost nothing. The honest catch is operations: an identity issuer is infrastructure that can't go down — if SPIRE goes down, the agents go down. So either the customer takes over operations with documentation and a runbook (then it's project work with low ongoing load), or twiceD runs it (then it's real on-call duty and not solo-friendly). I'd only sell the first option. This complements the flight-recorder idea from Aug. 31: identity says who, the log says what — together they're a record, each alone only half of one. hashicorp.com · learn.microsoft.com
