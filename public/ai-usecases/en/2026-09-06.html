The Regulator Sees Three Systems From the Inside [1]

On 2026-09-02, Lucas Anjos, superintendent for technological innovation at Brazil's data protection authority ANPD, reported on the status of the first AI regulatory sandbox during the panel "Fale com a ANPD" at the 17th Seminar on Privacy and Data Protection: "O projeto está em fase final de testes" — the pilot is in the final phase of testing. Full results of this first stage are expected to be published by the end of October. Three organizations are involved, from medical diagnostics, transportation, and financial-sector security. The subject is a single provision: Article 20 of the LGPD, and with it "transparência, observabilidade e explicabilidade de decisões automatizadas."[1]

Regulatory sandbox and Article 20: A "sandbox" here isn't a test server, it's an arrangement: a regulator lets companies run a real system under supervision and watches over its shoulder while they do — both sides learn, and no one gets punished right away. Analogy: the driving instructor rides along in the car, instead of reading the accident report afterward. Article 20 of the Brazilian LGPD is the provision that gives people a right when a machine decides about them — credit, a rate, a suspension, a job application: they can demand a review and learn what criteria the decision was based on. "Explainability" means exactly that and nothing bigger: can the company say why the no was a no.
three real systems→ regulator sees the inner workings→ criteria emerge from practice→ report in late October→ template for later rules

It doesn't stop there. A second round is set to be issued still in late 2026 and carried out in 2027, this time on the requirements of the ECA Digital, i.e. child protection in digital environments. And the agency is building an AI center in-house, housed at the innovation lab of the same superintendency — the stated goal is to develop supervisory capacity "tanto em infraestrutura quanto na formação e especialização de pessoal," that is, in both technology and people.[1]

Sourcing This is a single source: a Brazilian trade outlet reporting on a panel statement. The official gov.br page on the topic returned HTTP 401 today, and I found no ANPD publication of its own with this status. Date, speaker, occasion, and the quotes are from the loaded article; the numbers behind them — how many applications there were, how selection worked — aren't in it. This brief documented the first interim report from 2026-07-02 on Sept. 2.

My take: The sequence is the real story here. Europe wrote the law first and then had to stretch the high-risk deadlines by sixteen months, partly because the national supervisory authorities are still being built up. Brazil has no AI law — and its regulator is already practicing anyway, on three live systems, with a submission deadline. To me that's the superior approach, for an unromantic reason: an agency that has seen three explainability implementations from the inside will later write different rules than one that has only read hearing transcripts. It will know what a fraud model in the financial sector can actually produce and what it can't. Anyone building something in Brazil that decides about people shouldn't dismiss the October report as bureaucratic prose: it will be the first document in which a regulator writes down what form of justification it considers sufficient in operation — and that, a year before any law requires it, is practically a free blueprint.

The Clock That Keeps Running Anyway [2][3]

For context, briefly and without a rehash — the full version ran here on Sept. 5: the omnibus regulation stretched the EU AI Act's high-risk obligations to 2027-12-02 for standalone systems under Annex III and to 2028-08-02 for those embedded as a safety component in an already-regulated product.[2][3] Left untouched is 2026-12-02. On that date, providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, image, video, or text content must comply with Article 50(2) — explicitly even if the system was already on the market before 2026-08-02. The same date brings two new prohibitions under Article 5 that the omnibus added: non-consensual intimate images and depictions of child sexual abuse.[2][3]

My take: That's 87 days. What's notable is the direction: the same regulation sold as a "simplification" expanded the list of prohibitions. Anyone who read the headline "Brussels delays the AI Act" and filed it away got both things wrong — the deadline that probably affects them still stands, and the list of what isn't allowed in the first place got longer.

Twenty-Seven of Seventy-Five [4][5]

The Enterprise AI Benchmark Report from PYMNTS Intelligence doesn't count companies, it counts tasks: 75 distinct AI-supported work steps across eight business functions, surveyed among 60 senior technology executives — 20 each from financial services, healthcare, and media and advertising, all US companies with at least $1 billion in annual revenue, fieldwork in March 2026. Result: in financial services, 27 of these 75 tasks reach high usage, in media and advertising 16, in healthcare 10.[4]

Tasks with high AI usage, out of 75 surveyed — PYMNTS Intelligence, fieldwork March 2026, n = 60

Two more findings from the same survey. 80 to 85% of respondents across all three industries say the technology will augment human decisions rather than replace them. And the funding logic differs sharply: healthcare funds pilots "without formal return on investment (ROI) requirements" — meaning no one has to calculate the payoff in advance — while the financial industry ties its spending to productivity gains and competitive position.[4]

Methodology n = 60 here means n = 20 per industry. A single person answering differently shifts an industry figure by five percentage points. On top of that: US only, only companies above a billion dollars in revenue, self-reported by the technology leadership about its own department. For a machine-tool maker with 200 employees in Baden-Württemberg, this survey says nothing — it says something about sixty large US companies. The firm behind it funds itself through sponsored content; the task list itself ("75 tasks, eight functions") is nonetheless the most useful structure I've seen on this topic, because it measures usage where the work actually happens.

And now the comparison that makes this instructive. A completely different set of figures from the same publisher is running through the feeds: 95% / 84% / 81% of financial, healthcare, and media firms reportedly signaled "broad or embedded" AI usage. The page originally cited no longer loads (404), but the same figures appear verbatim on two related, still-live PYMNTS pages from a different survey wave (June 2026, the same 60-firm panel) — a different wave than the task benchmark above, not a repeat of the same data.[5] The same companion study is also the source for the claim that at least half the companies in every industry put full payback at five to six years.

My take: Both numbers can be true at once, and that's exactly the point. Ask a company "do you use AI broadly?" and 95% say yes. Count how many of its actual work steps that really carries in, and it's 27 of 75 — 36%, in the industry that's furthest ahead. In healthcare it's 13%. The first number answers an image question, the second answers a work question, and only the second one helps with a decision. For twiceD, that adds up to an awkwardly good piece of news: the market isn't saturated, it's just fashionable. Two-thirds of the tasks are still wide open, even at the companies that consider themselves done.

Four Hundred Fifty. Or a Thousand. Depending What's Counted. [6][7][8]

Presentations and blog posts have said for months that JPMorgan has "over 450 AI use cases in production." I find this figure in three variants — 400, 450, 500 — and exclusively in secondary sources; I found no primary publication from the bank with this claim.[7] What's dated and documented sounds different: at the earnings call on 2026-07-14, the bank said it had "nearly 1,000 AI use cases under development, with roughly 50 representing the bank's largest efforts" — nearly a thousand in development, about fifty large ones.[6] That's not a refinement of the 450, it's a different metric.

For comparison, a number that comes with a definition. IBM publishes a containment rate of 94% for its own HR assistant AskHR, a 75% drop in incoming support tickets since 2016, and 11.5 million employee interactions in 2024 alone.[8] That's the vendor reporting on its own product in-house, so not an independent audit. But every one of these numbers has a denominator: requests, tickets, interactions. You can dispute it, because you know what's being argued about.

Containment rate: The share of requests an assistant handles to completion without a human taking over. Analogy: how many callers hang up satisfied before saying "speak to an agent." The number is only as honest as its definition — does a dropped call count as resolved just because no one transferred it? Does a wrong answer nobody objected to count as a success? So the only question worth asking about any percentage of this kind is: percent of what, and who counted the denominator?

My take: A use-case counter is a metric for the budget, not for impact. It grows when you slice things smaller, and no one publishes the flip side — how many were shut down again. "Nearly a thousand in development," for that matter, is a remarkably defensive phrasing once you take it seriously: something nobody has touched in a year still counts as "in development." In practice, that means twiceD's offering should do the opposite of what corporations model: don't cite the number of initiatives, cite a single number with a definition, a denominator, and a measurement date. That's exactly what idea 2 below aims at — containment rate as a contract term instead of a brochure line.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Reasoned Denial — Article 20 as a Deliverable Artifact

ELI5 & value: When a system turns someone down — denies credit, blocks a card, screens out an application, raises a rate — Article 20 of Brazil's data protection law gives them the right to demand a review and learn the criteria. In practice, they get a boilerplate sentence that says nothing ("due to internal policy"). This idea sells the missing piece: every denial automatically generates a readable justification from the actual decision inputs, plus a documented appeal path — both filed in an audit-proof way. Value: the customer can answer the three words the ANPD is currently testing against three live systems — transparency, observability, explainability — before anyone asks. Side benefit: the same artifact is what European documentation requirements will want to see anyway, later.
Prototype (V0): (1) One decision type at one customer — say, the automatic fraud block or the credit limit. (2) Record inputs, model output, and threshold at the moment of decision; that's the part almost nobody has, and without it every justification is invented after the fact. (3) Generate the justification text from a template, filled with the factors that actually drove the outcome — directly computable for scoring and tree models, via SHAP otherwise. The language model only phrases it, it doesn't justify: it may only voice what the deterministic layer already calculated. (4) Appeal path: a button, a case with a deadline, a human decision, logged. Deliberately out of the MVP: no model monitoring, no bias analysis, no second decision type.
Market & gap: Governance platforms for exactly this field exist in abundance and they're good: IBM watsonx.governance, Credo AI, Holistic AI, Fiddler, OneTrust are named consistently as the field in 2026 comparison roundups.[12] What stands out is what they do: an inventory of AI assets, a policy layer, risk dashboards, drift and bias monitoring, audit-ready evidence — all facing inward, for a governance team managing a portfolio. None of these platforms produces the document the rejected person receives. On top of that comes price: the same roundups cite six-figure annual sums for the enterprise platforms and per-monitored-model pricing for the mid-tier vendors — a secondary claim, not a price sheet, but the order of magnitude fits the target audience.[12] Gap for twiceD: the Brazilian mid-market — credit cooperatives, regional insurers, fifty-person fintechs — that needs to cleanly explain one decision type and has no governance team to manage a portfolio. And a timing advantage: the ANPD's October report will supply the language the artifact can align itself with.[1]
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. Project-based work with a clear boundary: recording, justification generation, appeal flow, then handover. No hosting requirement — the layer runs at the customer's site, because the decision inputs are personal data and the company won't hand them over anyway. V0 is realistic in four to six weeks solo; every additional decision type after that takes days, since the template and the filing system are already in place. Maintenance load is low as long as the rule holds: one decision type per engagement. Honest risk: instrumenting existing models turns up decisions that don't justify well. That's uncomfortable for the customer — and the real reason someone should buy this before a regulator asks the same question. convergenciadigital.com.br · atlan.com · cloudzero.com

2 · Containment as a Contract Term — the Internal Help-Desk Agent With a Target Rate

ELI5 & value: In every company, employees keep asking HR and IT the same fifty questions: remaining vacation days, sick leave, expense rates, the VPN isn't working, how do I apply for X. IBM answers such questions in-house without a human 94% of the time and reports 75% fewer tickets since 2016.[8] A mid-sized company with 300 employees has the same questions, the same overloaded inbox — and none of this. The difference between this idea and any chatbot offering: what's sold isn't the tool, it's a measured rate on the fifty most common question types, with a denominator and a measurement period written into the contract. Value: HR gets back the hours currently draining away on repeat questions — and, for the first time, gets numbers on what's actually being asked.
Prototype (V0): (1) Analyze four weeks of real inquiry traffic (inbox, ticket system, chat) and bundle it into the 50 most common question types — that alone is already a result the customer keeps, even if the project ends there. (2) Answer only these 50, grounded in the company's own documents (works agreements, handbooks, intranet) via document retrieval; hand everything else straight to a human, with the conversation history attached. (3) From day one, measure per question type: handled without a human / handed off / answered incorrectly. (4) At most two write actions, each with confirmation — say, filing a vacation request, resetting a password. Deliberately out of the MVP: no open-ended conversation, no write access into the HR system beyond that, no voice telephony.
Market & gap: The segment exists and has just been consolidated: ServiceNow announced the acquisition of Moveworks — the best-known independent employee assistant — on 2025-03-10 for $2.85 billion, explicitly aiming to bring agentic AI to "every employee" via its own platform.[13] Alongside it sit Leena AI, Espressive, ServiceNow's own assistant, and IBM's watsonx Orchestrate. The common denominator: these offerings assume the platform underneath — a ticketing system, an HR system, a knowledge base in organized form — bill per user, and ship in English first. A German company with 300 employees has a shared inbox, a wiki, and a works agreement; it has no ITSM platform, and it doesn't want its people's HR questions sitting in a US SaaS product. Gap: fifty question types, on the company's own documents, on-premises or EU-hosted, at a project price instead of per head per month.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium to high — with two honest catches. A solo V0 in four to six weeks is realistic; the building blocks (document retrieval, a model, a handoff rule) are standard, the work is in the question-type analysis and the fit. Catch one: the answers are only as good as the documents, and documents go stale — so a quarterly content review belongs in the contract, not round-the-clock operation. Catch two isn't technical: a system that reads along with employee questions needs a works agreement in Germany. Plan four to six weeks for that in parallel — that's not a risk to success, but it is one to the timeline. Scaling runs through an outside developer per customer; the question-type craft stays with twiceD, because that's where the difference is made. ibm.com · newsroom.servicenow.com
