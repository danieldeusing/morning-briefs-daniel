Two Companies Are Now Selling to Agents, Not Screens [1][2]

Frigade, a founder-led company out of San Francisco, unveiled its Assist API on 2026-09-08. The mechanism is captured in a single sentence of the release: „A company registers Frigade as a single tool in the agent it has already built." And further: „Every request routes through the company's own agent, so the company keeps control of its interface and users never see a second assistant." Retell AI, an infrastructure provider for voice AI, is named as the first adopter.[1]

Registering a tool inside an agent: An AI assistant doesn't just talk — it can also look things up and trigger actions. What it's allowed to do sits on a list of "tools" handed to it at startup — much like telling a new colleague which three phone numbers they're allowed to call. Adding a tool means writing one more number onto that list. The colleague is still the same person; they just now know one more thing to do.

CEO Eric Brownrout is candid that it comes down to competition for the chat window: „More and more teams are building their own agents now, and they don't want a second one competing with it."[1] That's exactly the point. Frigade is no longer selling the guidance interface — only what sits behind it: knowledge about the customer's own product.

Nine days earlier, on 2026-09-01, Yodeck made the same move in a completely different industry. Yodeck runs digital signage — the screens in stores, cafeterias, factory floors — for, by its own account, over 65,000 customers worldwide. Its new AI agent will now manage those screens from inside third-party programs: ChatGPT, Claude, Cursor, VS Code, Windsurf, and the Claude Code CLI, built technically on the open Model Context Protocol.[2]

Vendor claim The line „Tasks that used to take 10 minutes can now take 10 seconds" comes straight from the company's own release and is nowhere independently verified. A sixtyfold speedup isn't a number you pass along unchecked. The protocol side of this story — what MCP actually is and what a server for it looks like — belongs in the ai-dev brief; here, only the business meaning counts.
Customer runs its own assistant→Vendor registers as a tool→Vendor's interface disappears→Knowledge and write access remain the product

My take. For twenty years the interface was the moat: once someone got used to a program, they didn't switch. Both companies are giving up that moat voluntarily, and both are right to — if the customer is already running an assistant, a second chat box isn't a feature, it's an imposition. What remains as an asset are two things: domain expertise, and the right to change something inside the customer's system. Both are harder to copy than a menu structure. For a German Mittelstand software house, that's the uncomfortable question of the year, and it isn't a technical one: if your customer is running its own assistant in 2027, is your interface still worth what's on the invoice? The flip side is the opportunity — whoever lets themselves be called as a tool ends up inside workflows they were never invited to bid for as a vendor.

Everything Is Moving Except the Money [3]

From McKinsey's State of AI 2026, as covered by The Register on 2026-08-25: 88% of respondents report regular AI use in at least one business function, up from 78% a year earlier. Among companies above $1 billion in revenue, the share scaling agentic AI in production has risen from 27% to 40%. And then the two numbers that haven't moved: 37% attribute "at least some" EBIT contribution to AI — exactly as in 2025. Top performers, defined as attributing more than 5% of EBIT to AI, stand at 6% — also exactly as in 2025.[3]

EBIT: What's left of revenue after all operating costs are paid, but before interest and taxes — operating profit. It's the most honest place to check whether AI has actually paid off: minutes saved only show up here once they've turned into either lower costs or more orders.
Methodology Survey period 2026-05-04 through 2026-06-08, 1,719 participants from 97 countries, 36% from organizations above $1 billion in revenue, results GDP-weighted. This is executives self-reporting on their own success — the direction of the bias is known, and it points upward. The publisher is a firm that sells AI transformation projects. McKinsey's own page timed out twice when accessed on 2026-09-10; the figures here rest on The Register as a secondary source plus a search-index excerpt of McKinsey's own methodology line. The Register itself notes that the phrase "at least some EBIT contribution" is doing „heavy lifting".[3]

My take. "At least some contribution" isn't a measurement, it's a feeling with no floor — someone who guesses 0.1% ticks the same box as someone who calculated 4%. The only number with a hard floor is the 6%: more than 5% of operating profit attributed to AI. And that one has stood still for twelve months while spending has gone up. Read strictly, the study says: one company in sixteen can put a number on the effect — exactly as last year. The rest have tools.

Side note for the record: 39% expect AI-driven headcount reductions, up from 32% the year before — though The Register notes in the same piece that last year's reduction expectations fell well short of what actually happened.[3]

The €47 Million Nobody Actually Imposed [5][6]

For a few days now, a story has been circulating: the EU has issued its first fines under the AI Act, totaling €47 million across three named cases — €18 million against a hiring-AI vendor, €14 million against a credit-scoring firm, €15 million over emotion recognition in retail. It has the shape of a news story: a sum, a date, three cases.

It couldn't be verified as of 2026-09-10. The text appears word-for-word on several compliance and SEO blogs that are copying each other. The EU Commission's official enforcement page describes the framework — who can sanction whom, from when, up to what ceiling — and names not a single fine actually imposed.[5] Reuters, the FT, and AP don't carry it either. Until an authority or a wire service backs it up, it isn't a news story.

What actually is confirmed and dated: since 2026-08-02, the AI Office and national authorities have had enforcement powers. The ceilings: up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover for prohibited practices, up to €15 million or 3% for other violations — „whichever is higher".[5] The next hard deadline is Wednesday, 2026-12-02, 83 days from now: systems that generate synthetic image, audio, video, or text content and were already on the market before that date must then meet the labeling requirement; at the same time, the bans on AI systems that generate non-consensual intimate imagery or abuse material become applicable.[6]

My take. Second day running, second widely shared enforcement story that falls apart on contact — yesterday a "wave of inspections" in September, today a €47 million fine. I'm not suggesting anyone's acting in bad faith, but the incentive is plain: compliance-adjacent sites need urgency, and a fine already imposed sells better than a deadline in December. The practical rule that follows is unglamorous and saves a lot of time: for the AI Act, what counts is the Commission's page and the regulation's text. If an enforcement figure doesn't appear on either, it doesn't exist yet.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Product Knowledge as a Tool Inside the Customer's Agent

ELI5 & value: A machine builder or a specialist software house has its product knowledge sitting in manuals, in ten years of resolved support tickets, in customization documents, and in the heads of two people in support. Its customers are just starting to run their own assistants. The idea: package that knowledge so the customer's own assistant can query it — a registered tool, not a second chat window, no interface anyone has to learn. Value for the customer: support requests that never arise in the first place, and onboarding without a training session. Value for the vendor: staying inside the workflow, even once the interface no longer belongs to them.
Prototype (V0): (1) One customer, one product, one question class — for instance "how do I configure X," drawn from the manual plus resolved tickets from the last twelve months. (2) Load these documents into a search index and make them discoverable by similarity. (3) Offer exactly one tool endpoint, tight contract: question in, answer plus source citation out — as an MCP server or a plain HTTP endpoint the customer registers in its own agent. (4) A refusal path: no cited source → no answer. (5) Log every question; as a side effect, that's the best product research the customer has ever had. Deliberately out of scope: no write access, no dedicated interface, no multi-tenancy. Building blocks: vector search in the customer's already-existing Postgres database (pgvector), the MCP reference SDK, and, where sovereignty matters, an open model instead of an API.
Market & gap: The category that owns this space today is called Digital Adoption Platform — WalkMe, Whatfix, Pendo, Chameleon, Appcues. WalkMe has been part of the SAP stack since SAP's acquisition (September 2024, $1.5 billion) and is aimed at SuccessFactors, Ariba, Concur, and S/4HANA; market size is fuzzy — one market researcher cites roughly $1.24 billion (2025) and $1.59 billion (2026), others disagree, so take the direction, not the number. Both figures come from secondary sources (competitor-comparison pages, paid market research), neither is primary-sourced here. What these vendors leave out is structural: they're overlays on web interfaces — they walk a human through a screen. For the case where there's no screen, because it's the customer's own agent asking, none of them is built for it. Frigade is built for exactly that, but it's a US SaaS with no on-prem story.[1] The gap for twiceD: German-language product documentation, a German contract, EU hosting or on-prem where the manual is the crown jewel — and hand-fitting into one product line rather than a self-service platform.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. The hard part — the model and the search mechanism — gets bought, not built. V0 solo in three to five weeks. The maintenance load stays limited as long as scope means "answer, don't write": re-indexing when documents change, keeping an eye on the refusal rate, roughly half a day per week per customer. Scaling runs through one external developer per additional customer, not through a department. On-prem variant: open model plus local index — that costs some answer quality, but it's exactly the argument that would make these customers buy it. prnewswire.com — Frigade Assist API · whatfix.com — WalkMe alternatives (competitor's view) · chameleon.io — post-SAP field overview

2 · The 12/02 Labeling Deadline — Measure First, Then Build

ELI5 & value: As of 2026-12-02, systems that generate synthetic images, audio, video, or text and were already on the market before that must label their output as machine-readable and artificially generated. That hits a lot of companies that don't think of themselves as AI vendors at all: agencies that generate product images, publishers, online retailers with AI-generated product copy. Value: a dated, bounded obligation with €15 million or 3% of global turnover as the ceiling behind it — and almost none of the affected companies can say today whether their image and text pipeline labels anything at all.
Prototype (V0): (1) Inventory: which content leaves the building, which system generated it, was it in use before 2026-08-02. (2) Measure — pull twenty real files from the live production pipeline and check whether the provenance metadata survives the journey: through the CMS, the image resizer, the delivery network. This measurement is the product; almost nobody has it. (3) A results sheet per content class: labeled / lost in transit / never labeled. (4) Only then the build: set labeling at the point of generation and shut down the steps that strip it. Deliberately out of scope: no legal advice, no in-house signing service — the assessment is the product, the build is the follow-on engagement.
Market & gap: What Article 50(2) literally requires: output must be „marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated", and the technical solutions must be „effective, interoperable, robust and reliable as far as this is technically feasible" (Article 50(2), linked below). The de facto standard the industry is pointing to is C2PA / Content Credentials; Adobe, Truepic, and Digimarc are named as vendors in the provenance and watermarking space. Honest sourcing note: the numbers circulating for C2PA members and signatories of the code of conduct appear exclusively on SEO blogs copying each other — the same pattern as the "€47 million" story above. That's why I'm naming the participants, not the figures. The gap is mechanical, and exactly for that reason it's a real engagement: metadata isn't robust across a normal web pipeline. One resize, one re-encode, one screenshot, one upload to a platform that strips EXIF data — and the label is gone. The big audit firms sell the paper, the standards vendors sell the standard; whether the label survives this one specific customer pipeline is something nobody is measuring.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High, and the better of the two ideas on maintenance load: an assessment carries practically none. V0 needs no infrastructure — a test set of real files, a tool to read out provenance manifests, a checklist. The clock works in its favor: 83 days until 2026-12-02. Honestly named risk: demand hinges on whether German Mittelstand companies feel addressed by this at all — most don't consider themselves "providers" in the sense the article means. So the first sale is education, which makes it consulting work, not a product. Scaling runs through external developers per customer; if it works, the same assessment turns into a recurring annual check. artificialintelligenceact.eu — Article 50 · artificialintelligenceact.eu — Deadline schedule · digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu — Enforcement

It's Thursday, September tenth. The most interesting thing happening this week isn't a new model release — it's a quiet move by two unrelated companies that are doing the exact same thing: both are giving up their own user interface and registering themselves instead as a tool inside the assistant their customer is already running.

First up is Frigade, out of San Francisco. On September eighth, the company rolled out what it calls its Assist API. The customer registers Frigade as a single tool inside its own assistant, every request routes through that assistant, and the end user never sees a second chat box pop up. Retell AI is named as the first company using it. Frigade's CEO is candid about why: more and more teams are building their own agents, and they don't want a competitor showing up inside them.

Nine days before that, Yodeck did the same thing in a completely different industry. Yodeck runs the digital screens you see in store chains and factory floors, serving, by its own count, more than sixty-five thousand customers. Those screens can now be managed from inside other companies' software — ChatGPT, Claude, various coding tools. Yodeck claims tasks that used to take ten minutes now take ten seconds. That's a vendor claim nobody has independently verified, and a sixty-times speedup isn't something you repeat without a grain of salt.

Here's my take. For twenty years, the interface was a software company's moat. Both of these companies are giving that moat up voluntarily, and both are right to. What's left as the real asset is domain expertise and the right to actually change something inside the customer's own system. For a German Mittelstand software company, that's the uncomfortable question of the year: if your customer is running its own assistant next year, is your interface still worth what's on the invoice?

The second story is a number that refuses to budge. McKinsey surveyed one thousand seven hundred nineteen executives across ninety-seven countries between May fourth and June eighth. Eighty-eight percent now use AI regularly in at least one part of the business, up from seventy-eight percent a year ago. Among large enterprises, the share scaling agentic AI in production has jumped from twenty-seven to forty percent. But the share that sees a contribution to operating profit is stuck at thirty-seven percent, exactly where it was last year, and top performers are stuck at six percent, also exactly where they were last year.

Read strictly, that means one company in sixteen can actually put a number on the payoff, same as last year. Everyone else has tools. And the phrase doing all the work here is "at least some contribution" — that's not a measurement, it's a feeling with no floor. A number out of Brazil fits right alongside it: in KPMG's survey, one hundred percent of Brazilian companies say they've adopted automation and AI. A hundred percent in a survey isn't an adoption rate, it's a definition problem. The useful number is right next to it: forty-five percent report running multiple AI projects that don't talk to each other.

Third item is a non-story, and it's the second one in two days. There's a claim going around that the European Union has issued its first fines under the AI Act, totaling forty-seven million euros across three cases. That claim appears word for word on several compliance blogs and on zero official government pages. The Commission's actual enforcement page just describes the framework and names not a single fine that's been imposed. Yesterday it was a supposed wave of inspections in September; today it's forty-seven million euros. The incentive is obvious: an already-imposed fine sells better than a deadline in December.

What's actually true: since August second, the AI Office and national authorities have had the power to sanction companies, with ceilings of thirty-five million euros or seven percent of global revenue for prohibited practices, and fifteen million or three percent for other violations. The next hard deadline is Wednesday, December second — eighty-three days from now: anyone generating synthetic content who was already on the market has to start labeling that content as machine-readable.

That's the seed for today's second twiceD idea: measure first, then build. Pull twenty real files from a customer's live production pipeline and check whether the label actually survives the CMS, the image resizer, and the delivery network. Almost nobody has done this measurement — it takes two days and produces a to-do list that only makes sense once you've actually measured. The first idea is the Frigade move, adapted for the German Mittelstand: package product knowledge so the customer's own assistant can query it, in German, under a German contract, and optionally running in their own server room.
