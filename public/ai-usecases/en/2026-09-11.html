Shopping Agents Get Papers [1]

Ant International, Mastercard, and Visa announced a joint framework called Know Your Agent (KYA) on 2026-09-09. It's meant to, in the framework's own words, "streamline artificial intelligence agent onboarding and identification across networks" and "reduce integration complexity and duplicative agent identity verification efforts while maintaining robust trust and risk controls".[1] In plain terms: an agent verified with one of the three networks counts as verified with the other two.

KYC — and now KYA: When you open a bank account, you have to show ID. Banks call this "Know Your Customer" — they need to know who you are before they move your money. With a shopping agent, the bank faces the same question, just without a face: Who are you, who are you acting for, and did your human actually authorize this? "Know Your Agent" is the ID for the software. The difference from a human ID: it also has to say on whose behalf the purchase is happening right now.

The catch so far wasn't that there was no solution — it was that there were three. Visa runs the Trusted Agent Protocol, Mastercard runs Verifiable Intent, Ant International runs the Agentic Mobile Protocol.[1] For a merchant, that meant integrating three times, verifying three times, and bearing liability three times. KYA is the attempt to turn three IDs into mutual recognition.

Agent gets verified with one network → Verification result applies across networks → Merchant integrates once instead of three times

The justifying figure in the paper is a forecast, not a measurement: $3 trillion to $5 trillion of worldwide consumer commerce is projected to flow through agents by 2030.[1] Numbers like that are standard inventory in payment-network announcements, and should be treated accordingly.

My take. The forecast is the least interesting part of this announcement. What's interesting is that three direct competitors — two American card networks and Alibaba's payments arm — are agreeing on exactly the identity layer, and nothing else. That's not idealism; it's the realization that an ID is worthless if only a third of the world accepts it. That's the exact same logic that produced the card standards thirty years ago.

For anyone running an online store, the question becomes very concrete: does your own storefront recognize a legitimate agent at the door, or does your bot defense throw it out along with the price scrapers? That's where the first twiceD idea below comes from.

Eighty Percent Feel It, Six Percent Can Put a Number On It [2][3]

Stanford HAI's AI Index 2026 puts organizational adoption at 88%.[2] The McKinsey 2026 survey describes the same finding from the other side: 80% of respondents report personal productivity gains, but only 37% report a positive contribution to operating profit, and only 6% attribute more than 5% of their EBIT to AI.[3]

Methodology Both are self-reported figures from executives, not audited books, and "productivity gain" is undefined in either survey. The 37% and the 6% already appeared in yesterday's edition from a second write-up of the same survey; the only new line here is the 80% figure.

My take. Eighty versus thirty-seven is the most revealing gap of numbers all year, and it isn't a contradiction — both figures can be true. Time saved only turns into money if someone actually captures it: fewer positions, more throughput, shorter turnaround. If that doesn't happen, the gain just spreads out over the day and disappears. The practical consequence for anyone writing a proposal: "saves two hours a week" only becomes a line item on the P&L once the customer can say what happens instead during those two hours.

Five Models in Ten Days — the Story Is the Price [4][5][6]

Between 2026-09-01 and 2026-09-03, five flagship models shipped. Read them as a leaderboard and you see rankings; read them as a buyer and, for the first time, you see a cleanly staggered price ladder for practically the same class of work.

Output tokens per 1M, US$ — list prices as of 2026-09-11

The figures come from each vendor directly. Per OpenAI's own pricing page, GPT-6 Astra costs $10.00 per million input tokens and $50.00 per million output tokens; with long context, $20.00 and $75.00.[4] Gemini 3.8 Flash sits at $0.75 / $3.75 — a launch price that expires on 2026-12-31 and doubles to $1.50 / $7.50 on 2027-01-01.[5] Qwen3.8-Max stands at $2.00 / $6.00 with a 1 million token context window.[6] And K2 Horizon costs nothing per token, because you run it yourself — instead, you pay for hardware and operations.[9]

Substantively, GPT-6 Astra is the break: the model operates screens instead of just text — Fortune describes it as able to "zip through spreadsheets, fill out forms, and navigate across web pages often at superhuman speed". Greg Brockman added: "It's not unreasonable to feel that we are now in the AGI era … I think it's reasonable."[4] That second statement can safely be taken for what it is — a co-founder's marketing on launch day.

My take. The factor of twenty between $3.75 and $75.00 is the practically most important number this week, more important than any benchmark point. It means: "which model is the best?" is now the wrong question for a business to ask. The right one is which price tier a specific workflow actually needs. Anyone who built a workflow on 2025's one usable model is likely paying twenty times over today for a result that a tier down would have passed just as well — and nobody re-measures, because re-measuring is work and the monthly bill looks small.

On top of that, there's a date worth writing down: on 2027-01-01, the Flash price doubles. Anyone budgeting on today's launch price is budgeting on a number with an expiration date. That's the hook for the second twiceD idea below.

The coding-benchmark side of these launches — Terminal-Bench, agent harnesses, the Agents API beta — belongs in the ai-dev brief; what counts here is what a business pays for it.

A Model You Can't Buy [5]

Alongside the regular Gemini 3.8 Flash, Google launched a second variant on 2026-09-02: 3.8 Flash Cyber, built for finding and closing security vulnerabilities. The figures are vendor claims, but they're unusually specific: a vulnerability-finding success rate above 70% on an internal benchmark spanning 20 programming languages, and the Chrome security team reports 2.6 times as many correct patches as "the best commercial models that are much larger".[5]

What's notable isn't the performance, it's the distribution channel. The model isn't sold, it's allocated through a new program called Fairwind — "prioritized access" for "trusted government authorities, as well as critical infrastructure operators and software maintainers".[5] Whoever can find vulnerabilities can also exploit them; Google draws the conclusion that access to this model is a matter of trust, not price.

My take. Access policy has itself become a product feature, and that's actually the new category this week: a model you can rent (Flash), one you can own (K2 Horizon), one you can't get yet (Atlas) — and one you have to qualify for. For a European Mittelstand company, that last tier is the most uncomfortable: it can't be reached with money, and the criteria are defined by an American vendor. Who ultimately ends up on the list of "trusted defenders" is an industrial-policy question currently being decided without any European involvement.

The uncomfortable part remains unanswered: an access barrier slows attackers down, it doesn't stop them. The 70% success rate describes a capability that now exists.

The High-Risk Deadlines Now Stand at 2027 and 2028 [7]

The EU AI Act's deadline schedule, checked on 2026-09-11, no longer shows the high-risk obligations landing this year. For Annex III systems — hiring, creditworthiness, education, critical infrastructure — the application of Chapter III begins on 2027-12-02. For high-risk systems under Article 6(1) and Annex I, i.e., products with their own product-safety legislation, not until 2028-08-02.[7]

Unchanged is the deadline on 2026-12-02, 82 days out: providers of systems that generate synthetic image, audio, video, or text content must meet Article 50(2) by then — machine-readable labeling — and on the same date, the bans on AI systems that generate non-consensual intimate imagery or abuse material become applicable.[7]

Sourcing That this delay traces back to the Digital Omnibus appears in a compliance tracker (as of 2026-08-25), not on the deadline portal itself — which lists the dates without explanation. So it's the deadline schedule that's solid, not its backstory. Anyone basing planning on this should treat the regulation's text and the Commission's page as authoritative.

My take. Sixteen months' delay on the high-risk obligations reads like an all-clear, and it's the opposite. What got pushed back is the obligation with the biggest build effort — risk management, data quality, technical documentation, human oversight. What did not get pushed back is the obligation that kicks in in 82 days and that almost nobody claims applies to them, because they don't consider themselves an AI provider: an agency generating product images, a retailer with AI-generated product copy. The delay pushes the expensive work further out and leaves the cheap, near-term deadline exactly where it was. For 2026 planning, it changes practically nothing.

Mix-up risk K2 Horizon has nothing to do with Moonshot AI's Kimi K2. Two independent labs picked the same mountain name. Whoever writes "K2" in a procurement document may mean something different from what the reader thinks.
Open weights ≠ open source: The "weights" are the finished, trained numbers inside the model — the result of training. If someone releases only those, you get the finished cake without the recipe: you can eat the cake and resell it, but you can't rebake it or find out what went into it. That's exactly how the K2 Horizon fleet is staggered — for 3.7B and 7B, weights, training data, and code are fully open; for the large 36B and 375B, so far only the weights are out, with data and code announced as "forthcoming".[9]

Notably honest is the handling of its own benchmark: the flagship model's initially reported score was revised down from 70.2% to 66.9% after a review.[9] A downward correction after the fact is rare enough in this industry to be worth mentioning — no reliable comparison figure against a closed frontier model could be found to size the remaining gap, though. The 32B tier is worth a second look for a different reason: unlike the 3.7B/7B models (complete artefacts) or the 36B/375B models (weights out, training data/code "forthcoming"), the vendor published the 32B variant only as an incomplete Stage 1 checkpoint — for sovereignty use cases on in-house hardware, that makes it more a candidate to watch than to deploy today.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Measuring Agent Traffic Before It Pays

ELI5 & value: An online retailer today has exactly two kinds of automated traffic: price scrapers it wants to get rid of, and — as of recently — shopping agents that want to buy on behalf of real customers. Its bot defense can't tell the two apart, because it filters on addresses and fingerprints, not on IDs. So it either blocks both or lets both through. The first costs orders, the second costs control. With the KYA framework, legitimate agents now actually carry papers — and the question becomes answerable. Value for the customer: a measurement instead of a guess about what's currently happening at their door.
Prototype (V0): (1) Analyze thirty days of a shop's real server logs: how much traffic already identifies itself as an agent, how much presents itself as a browser. (2) Test bench: send a real checkout path through the existing bot and firewall configuration as a signed agent, and log at which step it fails — login, CAPTCHA, payment authorization. (3) A results sheet per path: let through / blocked / silently degraded. (4) A rule proposal: which agent identities get recognized, which get throttled. Deliberately out of scope: no payment integration, no agent of its own, no legal advice. Building blocks: Cloudflare's Web Bot Auth — Ed25519 signatures, a public key directory under /.well-known/http-message-signatures-directory, and the Signature-Input, Signature, Signature-Agent headers —, the customer's existing firewall logs, and the KYA protocols as the next thing to recognize.
Market & gap: The infrastructure layer exists and is properly documented: Cloudflare's Web Bot Auth describes a mechanism that lets operators cryptographically "verify that a request comes from an automated bot" instead of guessing from addresses and fingerprints, including transitive trust for cases where "the operator and the end user are not the same party". The ID layer comes from the payment networks (KYA, Trusted Agent Protocol, Verifiable Intent).[1] Both are platforms — they deliver mechanics, not an answer to what's happening in this particular shop. And that's exactly what's missing: a Mittelstand merchant running Shopware or Shopify operates its bot rules on default settings, never reads its logs, and never finds out about a blocked agent order, because an order that never happened doesn't generate a ticket. Honest note: there's plenty of competition in bot management; what I cite here is only what I read directly at the vendor, and I'm skipping market-size figures I have no solid source for.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. It's a measurement, not an operation — maintenance load after delivery is practically zero, and the setup is the same for each additional customer. V0 solo in two to three weeks, repeat per shop in a few days; scaling runs through one external developer per customer, not a department. Honestly named risk: demand depends on agent traffic actually showing up. If the $3 trillion to $5 trillion stays a forecast, this assessment sells once and not again. A fallback with real substance: the measurement already shows today what the bot defense is throwing out in legitimate traffic — that finding holds up even without an agent wave. developers.cloudflare.com — Web Bot Auth · pymnts.com — Know Your Agent

2 · Model Diet: One Workflow, Three Price Tiers, One Euro Figure

ELI5 & value: Many businesses built a workflow in 2025 — proposal copy, ticket summaries, document review — on whatever was the one usable model at the time. Since then, three price tiers have emerged, and nobody has re-measured whether the expensive tier is still needed. The gap today is documented and large: $3.75 versus $75.00 per million output tokens, a factor of twenty for roughly the same class of work.[4][5] Value for the customer: not a new tool, but a number — how many euros a month the same workflow costs in the next-cheapest tier, and what quality gets lost along the way. The savings either pay for the project or they don't, and you find out either way in two weeks.
Prototype (V0): (1) One workflow, 200 to 500 real requests from the production logs as a pseudonymized test set. (2) The customer writes the evaluation criteria themselves — what "good enough" means for this exact task. That's the hard and the genuinely valuable step; I facilitate it, I don't invent it. (3) Run the same test set through a price ladder: self-hosted open model in the 32B tier → Flash tier → flagship model. (4) Result: success rate per tier, euros per month at current volume, plus a routing rule — easy cases cheap, hard cases escalate. Deliberately out of scope: no hosting, no dedicated evaluation platform, no rebuild of the workflow. Building blocks: the customer's logs, an open model from the K2 Horizon class or comparable under Apache 2.0[9], a plain repeat script, the vendors' published price lists.
Market & gap: Two categories occupy this field. Routing services like OpenRouter offer dozens of models behind one interface and make switching technically trivial — but they don't answer whether you should switch. And evaluation platforms sell self-service tools, which is exactly the work a Mittelstand company can't do itself: there's nobody there to pull the logs, work out the criteria with the business unit, and land on a euro figure at the end. Honest note: I'm not citing market sizes for this segment — the figures circulating come from paid market research I couldn't access. There's also a German-specific lever here: routing production traffic through three vendors requires a data processing agreement, and for the sensitive tier, an in-house variant. That's exactly what the bottom rung of the ladder is for — here, the open model isn't the budget option, it's the only permissible one.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High on building, medium on selling. V0 solo in two to three weeks; no maintenance falls due between engagements, because nothing runs continuously. The measurement, however, expires with each new model generation — that's simultaneously the recurring business and the risk, since a customer who's watched it once can try the repeat themselves. Pre-qualification is mandatory: under roughly 10M tokens a month, the savings don't carry the project — decline honestly at that point. Working in its favor is a fixed date, 2027-01-01, when the Flash launch price doubles: a dated occasion to reopen the calculation. developers.openai.com — API pricing page · blog.google — Gemini 3.8 Flash · openrouter.ai — model listing
