A thousand engineers, sitting with the customer [1][2][3]

Accenture and Google Cloud announced the formation of the Accenture Gemini Enterprise Business Group on 2026-09-08. The core of the unit isn't technology, it's people: a 1,000-strong force of forward deployed engineers, trained by Google Cloud, who build customized applications on the Gemini Enterprise platform on-site at the customer — backed by roughly 50,000 Accenture staff with Google Cloud qualifications and a company with about $70 billion in annual revenue.[1]

Forward deployed engineer: A developer who doesn't work on a product from their own office, but sits inside the customer's operation for weeks or months and gets the software running there. Like a fitter who doesn't build the machine but sets it up on the customer's factory floor — and discovers along the way that the ceiling is two centimeters too low. The role was long seen as a step backward in the software industry: expensive, poorly scalable, not "product." That the four biggest vendors are now buying it by the thousand is therefore a statement about the state of enterprise AI, not about hiring policy.

The one named customer case in the announcement is YouTube: a Gemini Enterprise agent in customer service, set up with Accenture and Google Cloud during the demand spike around NFL Sunday Ticket, delivered 11% more positive customer sentiment and 37% less average handle time, according to the announcement.[1]

Average handle time (AHT): The standard metric for every service hotline — how long an agent spends on a customer contact, on average, from pickup to wrap-up. Minus 37% means: ten minutes becomes a good six. With 40 people on the phones, that's the difference between hiring and not hiring — which is why this one number is the hardest currency there is in service organizations.

My take: The metric pairing is exactly right — one efficiency number and one quality number, so no one can ask whether this is just faster, worse service. Two things temper it nonetheless. First, the source is the press release of the two parties involved, not an independent remeasurement. Second — and this is the bigger one — Google's flagship customer for Gemini Enterprise is a Google company. YouTube already has the platform, the data, the telemetry, and above all the measurement infrastructure in-house. What this case shows is that the technology works when everything around it is already in place. What it doesn't show is transferability to a 300-person machine builder whose service desk runs on Zammad and who doesn't know its current handle time.

Models roughly equal → Bottleneck = delivery → Big vendors buy delivery capacity → Mid-market next in line

This chain isn't a guess, it's paid for. From July — older context, but here's the scale that explains today — four line items sit side by side: Microsoft $2.5 billion for its Frontier Company with roughly 6,000 engineers, OpenAI raising over $4 billion for its Deployment Company plus the acquisition of consultancy Tomoro with 150 deployment engineers, Anthropic a $1.5 billion joint venture with Blackstone, Hellman & Friedman, and Goldman Sachs explicitly targeting mid-sized companies, and Amazon $1 billion via AWS. Add the jobs-page angle: postings for forward deployed engineers rose more than 800% between January and September 2025.[2]

Methodology The accompanying figures from the same source — 71% of executives at companies with over $1 billion in revenue name organizational maturity as the main obstacle, only 11% cite the technology, 85% call their data fragmented — come from an in-house survey for which the article names no fieldwork period, sample size, or population. Directional, not robust. And: the piece is from 2026-07-06, so a good two months old.[2]

The German reference figure for this has run here multiple times over the past two weeks and gets no rerun today: 51.2% of mid-sized German companies are using or testing AI, 16.6% deploy agents — but fieldwork was November 2025, published 2026-03-09, n ≈ 700, collected by Salesforce together with the Deutscher Mittelstands-Bund, i.e. by a vendor with a stake in the result.[3] I'm keeping it as a benchmark nonetheless, because it quantifies the target group: that 16.6% is exactly the address the newly bought delivery capacity is knocking on next.

160,000 accelerators built only to answer [4][5]

According to reports citing Bloomberg sources, DeepSeek has ordered at least 160,000 Huawei accelerators of the Ascend 950DT type — each with 144 GB of memory at 4 TB/s bandwidth — for a gigawatt-scale data center in Inner Mongolia, whose power demand is compared to that of roughly 750,000 households.[4][5] The crucial sentence in this is a small one: the facility is planned for inference only. Training still runs on Nvidia.[4]

Training vs. inference: Training is the one-time, very expensive process of teaching a model. Inference is the day-to-day answering that follows. Like the difference between writing a cookbook and cooking ten thousand portions from it: you write the book once, but you cook the portions every day — and that's where the cost of a plate gets decided. For anyone building AI into a product, only the second number is the operating math.
Unconfirmed As of 2026-09-09, neither DeepSeek nor Huawei had publicly confirmed the plan; the figures trace back to anonymous sources.[4] I haven't read the original Bloomberg report myself, only two independent write-ups citing the same figure. So: a report, not a fact — though I consider the direction more solid than the number.

Why this belongs in this brief and not just in the chip section of the business press: whoever can build answer capacity at domestic cost sets the price floor for every AI feature running on top of it. The split — domestic hardware good enough for the part that scales with user count, not yet for the part that scales with ambition — is the most honest status report on China's chip industry I've seen this week. And it has a practical consequence for European buyers that has nothing to do with geopolitics: "answering gets cheaper and stays diverse" is now a hardware fact with a location and a power budget, not a hope anymore. Anyone who nails a workflow permanently to one model today forgoes that price movement. That becomes idea 2 below.

Worth fifty billion — and a no in the clinic [5][6]

Moonshot AI, the lab behind Kimi, has filed a confidential A1 application with the Hong Kong Stock Exchange, according to the same weekly roundup: a target raise of roughly $3 billion at a pre-IPO valuation of roughly $50 billion, with a possible listing in the fourth quarter of 2026.[5] The flagship Kimi K3 has 2.8 trillion parameters, of which only 104 billion compute per request — a panel of experts where only the two relevant ones ever speak up — and a context window of one million tokens.[5][6]

And now the other side of the same model. In regulated healthcare and pharma workflows, K3 is not deployable — and not for technical reasons:

PHI and BAA: Protected Health Information is patient data. Anyone processing it in the US may only hand it to vendors who co-sign a special contract — the Business Associate Agreement. Think of it as the key to the filing cabinet: no signature, no key, no matter how well you can read. Moonshot has no published BAA program. That makes the hosted K3 API off-limits, under current enforcement practice, for any workflow touching patient data — the best technology in the world is legally a no.[6]
The 1025-FLOP threshold: The EU AI Act draws a line for general-purpose models based on the compute that went into training. Above it, stricter obligations apply — like a driver's-license class that depends on engine power. K3's size plausibly crosses that line, but Moonshot has never disclosed its training compute. So the buyer can't even determine which class they're driving.[6]

The escape route would be self-hosting. Moonshot's own recommendation calls for 64 or more accelerators for that — a ballpark figure the analysis classifies as "outside the reach of all but the largest pharmaceutical manufacturers".[6]

My take: The two stories line up, and that's the real news of the day. Capital markets value the model, regulators value the workflow. A lab can be worth $50 billion and still stay a no in exactly the three industries where document-heavy work is most expensive — healthcare, pharma, finance. For anyone selling AI into a regulated German Mittelstand, the question "which models am I even allowed to deploy here" is therefore worth more than any benchmark table. It's the question idea 2 below makes answerable.

Reg thread: the deadlines, verified today [7][8]

Because the data on this circulates messily in German trade blogs, I checked the timeline against the source myself today. What holds: the Chapter III requirements apply to high-risk systems under Annex III (Art. 6(2)) from 2027-12-02, and under Annex I (Art. 6(1)) from 2028-08-02. In addition — and this is the deadline that actually counts soon — providers of systems generating synthetic audio, image, video, or text content that were placed on the market before 2026-08-02 must have taken the steps to comply with Art. 50(2) by 2026-12-02.[7] From today: 81 days.

Confirmed The small mid-cap category — under 750 employees, revenue of at most €150 million (or ≤€129 million balance sheet), simplified documentation/quality-management/sandbox-access requirements — is real: it's part of the AI Act's "Digital Omnibus," published in the EU's Official Journal and in force since July 2026. The high-risk timeline in source [7] only omits it because that page tracks applicability deadlines, not documentation tiers.[8] If your client has under 750 people: this now belongs in compliance planning.

Project ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The baseline measurement — what the service desk can do before the agent arrives

ELI5 & value: Accenture can say "minus 37 percent handle time" for YouTube because YouTube measured beforehand. A 250-person company running a Zammad or Freshdesk desk can't: it doesn't know how long a ticket currently lives on average, which category eats the time, or how many cases get reopened. Result: after the agent project, there's no result, only a feeling — and with it, no budget defense next year and no negotiating position with the vendor. The offer is a baseline measurement: three weeks, before the project, vendor-neutral. The deliverable is a signed baseline with a cutoff date, plus the script to repeat it identically six months later. The customer buys the ability to answer "did it work?" with numbers — even, and especially, when someone else builds the agent.
Prototype (V0): (1) Export twelve months of tickets and call logs via the API of the existing system — Zammad, OTRS, Freshdesk, Zendesk, Jira Service Management. (2) Compute the hard metrics with zero AI: handle time per category, first-contact resolution rate, reopen rate, follow-up-question loops. Pandas, no model. (3) Exactly one AI component: a sentiment score over the closing messages, calibrated against 200 hand-labeled cases — so the measurement's margin of error is spelled out in the report instead of implied. (4) A one-page baseline plus the repeat script in the customer's repository. Deliberately out of scope: no agent, no live dashboard, no forecast, no recommendation of which agent to use.
Market & gap: The four biggest vendors in the industry are currently buying delivery capacity, not measurement capacity — Microsoft $2.5 billion with roughly 6,000 engineers, OpenAI over $4 billion plus Tomoro with 150 deployment engineers, Anthropic $1.5 billion in a joint venture explicitly targeting mid-sized firms, Amazon $1 billion via AWS.[2] Accenture adds 1,000 FDEs on top.[1] All of them sell the agent. None of them sells the evaluation of the agent — and none could do so credibly, since they're an interested party. That the gap is real is stated in the same source: 71% name organizational maturity as the main obstacle, only 11% cite the technology, 85% consider their data fragmented.[2] In plain words, that's "we can't measure it." On the tooling side, quality and sentiment analytics already exist, but as a component of the big contact-center platforms — Observe.AI with automated quality assurance and real-time assist, plus Genesys and Zendesk's quality modules — licensed per seat and built for shops that already run those platforms. A mid-sized company on Zammad simply doesn't have that layer, and it won't buy it just for a one-off measurement. Honest note: I've only seen the positioning and feature scope of these tools through comparison articles in the search index, not on the vendor sites themselves — which is why I name them as a category and print no prices.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. It's a measurement, not an operation: maintenance load after delivery is practically zero, the setup repeats per customer, and the expensive part — cutting categories cleanly, calibrating the sentiment score — is head work, exactly the kind AI assists well with. V0 solo in two to three weeks, each repeat afterward in three to five days; scaling runs through one external developer per engagement, not a department. Honestly named risk: this is project business — one engagement per customer plus a follow-up measurement, no return from repetition. Anyone who wants recurring revenue from this has to lock the follow-up measurement into the first contract, not hope for it. newsroom.accenture.com — YouTube metrics · pymnts.com — delivery capacity, 71/11/85

2 · The swap switch — the model as an interchangeable part, with an acceptance test

ELI5 & value: Today's two China stories say the same thing about models, from two different angles. They're getting cheaper — DeepSeek is said to have ordered 160,000 accelerators that do nothing but answer.[4] And they can become unusable overnight — Kimi K3 is technically top-tier and still a no in regulated US healthcare operations, because a signature is missing.[6] Anyone who has nailed a workflow permanently to one vendor's SDK carries both risks with no way out: they can't capture the price drop, and they can't absorb the outage. The swap switch is the countermeasure — a thin abstraction layer plus an acceptance test built from the customer's real cases, so a model switch becomes a one-day decision instead of a project. Value: negotiable prices, and a plan B that's already written by the time it's needed.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick one live workflow that costs money — quote summarization, complaint triage, inbound-mail sorting. (2) Put an OpenAI-compatible router in front of it, LiteLLM or 200 lines of your own; the vendor SDK disappears from the application code. (3) Freeze 150 to 300 real production cases as a golden set, with a pass/fail rule the business unit signs off on — not a model acting as sole judge. (4) Run that same set against three candidates, one of them self-hosted, and deliver a switching-cost table: euros per 1,000 cases, quality delta, and a column for legally deployable — data processing agreement in place, BAA or equivalent, hosting region, training compute disclosed yes/no. Deliberately out of scope: no GPU operations, no fine-tuning, no legal advice — the column collects evidence, it doesn't render a verdict.
Market & gap: The plumbing layer is solved and free: LiteLLM and OpenRouter have been routing across vendors for years. The evaluation layer also exists as an off-the-shelf product — LangSmith, Braintrust, Galileo, all three with free entry tiers, Galileo with over twenty built-in metrics from hallucination detection to context faithfulness. But both are self-service tools for teams that already have an AI staff: they deliver the mechanics for measuring, not the decision — and certainly not the golden set, since that has to be cut from this specific customer's cases, and that's exactly the work a mid-sized company never does. None of these tools has a legally deployable column, even though the Moonshot analysis spells out precisely what blocks deployment: missing BAA program, undisclosed training compute, a self-hosting floor of 64 accelerators.[6] Honest note: the details on the evaluation tools come from comparison articles via the search index, not from the vendor sites; I'm therefore not naming prices.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium — and honestly medium, not high. The build is solo-doable, two to four weeks per workflow, router and test run are standard work. But the output spoils: a golden set ages with the business, and the legal column ages with every vendor announcement. That means real maintenance, one to two days per quarter per customer. Solo-viable up to roughly ten engagements, after which an external developer runs the quarterly passes. Sovereignty variant as a lever: the same test run with an open model on rented EU hardware as a fourth candidate. In German conversations, that's often the reason anyone listens at all — and unlike a promise in a contract, it's a measurement. winbuzzer.com — inference build-out · intuitionlabs.ai — deployability blockers · pydantic.dev — evaluation tools overview
