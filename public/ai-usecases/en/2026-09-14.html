OpenAI Puts an Analyst Inside the Business Unit [1]

On 2026-09-10, OpenAI launched a Data agent in ChatGPT Work. It connects to a company's approved data sources — named sources include Amazon Redshift, Google BigQuery, Snowflake, Databricks, MongoDB, ClickHouse, and Datadog, plus files from Google Drive and SharePoint — answers questions in plain language, and builds dashboards directly in the tools already running in-house: Tableau, Power BI, Sigma, Omni, Oracle BI, ThoughtSpot.[1] OpenAI's own phrasing is the entire sales pitch in one sentence: users direct and refine the analysis "in a single conversation, without writing queries or learning a separate analytics tool."

Data Warehouse & BI: A data warehouse is the central repository where a company copies data from all its individual systems — inventory management, point of sale, web shop, accounting — so it can all be analyzed together. Like a storage room where every department drops off its boxes. BI tools like Tableau or Power BI are the windows into that room: they turn the data into charts and tables. Until now, every new question needed someone fluent in the query language SQL — that's the person the Data agent is meant to replace.

As evidence, OpenAI cites NTT DATA among others: there, "many non-engineers, particularly in sales and corporate functions, have been able to build and update their own dashboards using plain language," according to Yuji Shono.[1] A customer quote, not a measurement — no before-and-after, no figure, no time savings.

My take. Writing the query was never the expensive part. The expensive part is the definition. If the business unit asks "What was revenue in August?", the answer depends on whether refunds are subtracted, whether freight is included, and whether it's counted by invoice date or delivery date. A controller knows these rules and produces a number that can be defended. An agent only knows them if someone has written them down — and otherwise answers anyway, in complete sentences and with full confidence. A wrong number that sounds convincing and ends up in a board presentation costs more than a slow, correct one.

What's notable is where OpenAI doesn't attack: the Data agent doesn't replace Tableau and Power BI, it writes into them. That avoids a head-on fight with the established licenses — and, incidentally, is the only existing safeguard, since these tools hold the semantic models with the definitions. Anyone without one just hooks the agent up to raw tables. That's where the first project idea below comes from.

Five customer figures from one release — five different denominators

Side by side, the numbers look comparable, and they aren't. Hibbett's 90% means covered shopping journeys, not completed purchases. Perk's 60% means pipeline built — that is, meetings and opportunities, not revenue. Engine's 50% means fully resolved chat inquiries, which requires someone to define when something counts as "fully resolved." And Asana's fourfold conversation volume with Piper isn't a result at all, just activity: four times as many conversations can be four times as many leads or four times as much noise.[2]

Methodology All the figures are vendor claims about their own customers, with no baseline, no measurement period, and no third-party verification. Anthropic's phrasing is the most honest of the bunch: "79% of conversations that Fin sees are resolved autonomously" — so the denominator is only the conversations that even get routed to the agent, not all contacts. For Marshall, the release names no figure at all. That's no disaster, but it says something about which roles already have references and which don't.

That customers rename their instances — at Engine, the help agent is called Eva, at Hibbett it's simply Hibbett AI[2] — gives away the mechanism: the seven first names aren't products you buy, they're role templates you fill.

My take. Rebranding "platform feature" as "role with a first name" is a pricing move, not a technology move. As long as an agent is sold as software, procurement compares it to other software and pushes the price down. Sold as a role, the business unit compares it to a headcount — reference point: a five-figure annual salary. That's why the metrics here are uniformly completion rates; that's the language of workforce planning, not license comparison. So the only useful counter-question in procurement isn't "How high is the rate?" but "Percent of what, measured against what, and what was the value before?" The most useful number in the release, by contrast, looks like nothing at all: Hibbett went live in six weeks.[2] For a mid-sized company, project duration decides whether an initiative fits into a quarter or disappears into a steering committee.

The Counter-Math: Under Ten Percent [3][4]

Against these launch announcements stands the stocktaking. In McKinsey's State of AI 2025 (n = 1,993 companies), fewer than 10% report scaling AI agents in any single function. 23% say they're scaling an agentic system somewhere across the enterprise, another 39% are still experimenting. In product development, 73% aren't using agents at all.[3] The gap between "somewhere across the enterprise" and "carried through in one function" is the same gap this brief has been tracking for weeks.

Sourcing & methodology mckinsey.com was unreachable for us (403 or timeout, multiple attempts on two URLs). The figures come from two independent secondary sources that quote the same survey verbatim and agree. As a result, we couldn't confirm the exact publication date of the original report. As with all consultancy surveys, it also holds: self-reported by respondents, not an audited operational finding.

And the number currently making the rounds again in every feed is old. "More than 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027" comes from a Gartner press release dated 2025-06-25 — fifteen months old. It's currently being cited as if it were new. The basis, moreover, isn't project tracking but a webinar poll of 3,412 attendees from January 2025: 19% reported significant investment, 42% conservative investment, 8% none, 31% were undecided.[4] The harder observation from that same release: of the "thousands" of vendors calling themselves agentic, Gartner considers only about 130 genuine.[4]

Agent-washing: An existing product — a chatbot, a rule engine, an automation pipeline — gets a new label and is now called an agent, without any change in behavior. Like "handmade" on a factory-line package. The test question is always the same: which work step does the thing decide on its own, and what happens when it gets it wrong?

My take. Today's two launch announcements and these counter-figures don't contradict each other. Salesforce and OpenAI are selling directly into the gap McKinsey measures — and both do it with the same trick: they scope things so narrowly that delivery takes weeks instead of quarters. Six weeks at Hibbett, one role instead of a platform, one conversation instead of an analytics tool. That's not marketing finesse, it's the only known countermeasure against the 40% cancellation rate Gartner has forecast.

Reg thread, just today's tie-in. Article 50 of the AI Act has been enforced since 2026-08-02: anyone interacting with a system must be told it's a machine. That hits exactly the class introduced today — Casey on the phone, Carter in the shop, Fin in chat. Fines run up to €15 million or 3% of global annual revenue.[5] For legacy systems that generate synthetic content, the labeling deadline runs to 2026-12-02 — 79 days from today. Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Metrics Canon — Before the Question-Answering Agent Gets Database Access

ELI5 & value: Someone in sales asks the new data agent: "What was revenue in August?" Depending on which table it touches, three different numbers come out — with or without refunds, with or without freight, by invoice date or by delivery date. Until now, nobody noticed, because two controllers wrote every report and held the rules in their heads. A question-answering agent turns that into a hundred questions a day from people who don't know the rules, and it answers every single time in complete, convincing sentences. The metrics canon is the written-down, machine-readable definition of the twenty to thirty metrics that actually matter in the business — plus a test that proves they're correct. Value: a wrong number in a board or bank presentation costs more than the entire project; and without this canon, a data agent can't even be signed off, because there's no "correct" to check it against.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pull the twenty to thirty metrics from the reports actually sent out over the last twelve months, not from management's wish list. (2) For each metric, a definition as code — source table, filters, time reference, exclusions, currency conversion — inside an existing semantic layer; dbt Semantic Layer or Cube, not built from scratch. (3) Regression test: thirty questions with known answers from old reports, the layer has to reproduce them down to the cent — that's the acceptance artifact. (4) The agent gets access exclusively through this layer (MCP or API), never directly to raw tables. Deliberately not in the MVP: a new data warehouse, migrations, real time, write access.
Market & gap: The tooling side is crowded and mature. The dbt Semantic Layer sells exactly this promise — teams are supposed to work "with the same secure definitions across every report, embedded app, and AI workflow," delivered among other ways via its own MCP server.[6] Cube positions itself even more directly as the "agentic analytics platform built on a semantic layer" and delivers "governed, reliable business data through MCP and APIs."[7] And OpenAI's Data agent deliberately writes into Tableau, Power BI, and Sigma instead of replacing them — that's where the semantic models live.[1] The gap isn't a product gap, it's a work gap: the decisive task is sitting down with the controller and writing down what "revenue" means here — and nobody sells that, because it can't be billed as a subscription. In mid-sized companies, there's an added problem: there's no analytics engineer to operate these tools. The urgency is quantified — under 10% scale agents in any function at all.[3]
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. Access is read-only, operations stay with the customer, there's no on-call duty. V0 in four to six weeks solo; the heavy tooling gets bought, what gets built is definitions and tests. The maintenance load is limited and predictable: the canon changes when the business changes — that's an annual mandate, not ongoing operations. Scaling runs through an external developer per additional source system. Honest catch: this assumes the customer already has a data warehouse, or at least a usable reporting database. Anyone with only an ERP and a mailbox needs a different, bigger project first — better to decline than to bundle it in. getdbt.com · cube.dev · openai.com

2 · The Platform-Free Role Agent — Automating One Job Without Buying a Suite

ELI5 & value: Since Friday, Salesforce has been selling agents like jobs: a first name, a role, a quota. That's a good product — but only purchasable if you already have Salesforce. The mid-sized company running inventory software, a mailbox, and WhatsApp has neither that nor the expensive tier of Microsoft 365. The platform-free role agent carries over the same framing: one clearly scoped role gets automated — say, delivery-status inquiries, which today eat up half a position in inside sales — with a written job description instead of a feature list. Value: a recurring, well-measured piece of work disappears, without buying a platform the business wouldn't otherwise need.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick a role that fits in one sentence and whose answers come from at most two systems. (2) Write the job description — what the agent may do, what it explicitly may not, when it escalates and to whom, and the one metric it gets measured against. That's a contract appendix, not a wiki document. (3) Tools instead of free text: one read query into the ERP, at most one write action, and only with human approval. (4) One channel first — email or WhatsApp — not four. (5) Disclosure that a machine is answering, built in from the start, because Article 50 requires it in the EU anyway.[5] Building blocks: a model API, an existing agent framework, the existing ERP access. Not included: a voice channel, multilingual support, CRM synchronization.
Market & gap: The big vendors deliver the framing, but only on their own turf. Salesforce named seven roles on 2026-09-11, six of them available immediately — Casey, Paige, Carter, Hunter, Marshall, Piper, Fin.[2] Microsoft's Copilot Studio is the second route there, costing $30 per user per month in the Microsoft 365 Copilot plan when paid annually, while the consumption-based variants explicitly require an Azure subscription and the Copilot variant requires "a qualifying Microsoft 365 plan."[8] Both, then, assume the customer's data already sits in their world. The gap is the last mile for everyone else: the business whose truth lives in an ERP database and an inbox, that doesn't want to sign a suite contract, and for which nobody builds the escalation path. That this exact last mile is the problem shows up in the numbers: under 10% scale agents in any function[3], and Gartner expects over 40% of projects canceled by the end of 2027 — with only about 130 out of thousands of vendors earning the name.[4] The only documented counterweight is speed: six weeks to go-live at Hibbett.[2]
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium — and here honesty matters more than enthusiasm. One person can build it: V0 in four to six weeks, since the model and framework are bought and only tools, guardrails, and escalation get built. Operations are the burden: an agent answering customers runs around the clock, needs monitoring, incurs cost per message, and a wrong answer is reputational damage at the customer's own customer. Realistically three to five hours per week per active agent; the ceiling for one person sits at four to six parallel installations, beyond that it needs staff — at which point the idea has failed the feasibility filter and has to switch to build-and-hand-off. Scaling runs through an external developer per customer. Where data sovereignty is the lever, there's a variant: a small open model on-premises for classification and lookup, with only the final phrasing going to a hosted model — or not even that, if the customer wants it that way. salesforce.com · microsoft.com · ec.europa.eu
