An inspection wave for a duty that doesn't apply until 2027

A story has been making the rounds in compliance circles for a few days: the EU AI Office supposedly began on-site inspections of high-risk systems at companies on 2026-08-30 — CV screening, credit scoring, medical triage. The story is well told, precise on the details, and even names the lead authorities. It has just two problems: there's no official source for it, and the obligations it claims are being inspected don't apply, per the official timeline, until 2027-12-02. Alongside that, today's second number: a health benchmark that rose by 2.9 points and is now being sold as medical progress. Both are the same mistake: the figure keeps traveling, its source gets left behind.

The inspection wave no agency confirms [1][2][3]

The story goes like this: "On August 30, 2026, the European AI Office began its first wave of high-risk on-site audits under the AI Act, focusing on technical documentation for automated resume-screening, credit-assessment and healthcare-triage systems." And on the agencies: the AI Office is supposedly working with twenty-four national market surveillance authorities, "with France's CNIL, Germany's BfDI and Spain's AESIA leading the initial requests."[1] That's concrete enough to land in a board memo — and just concrete enough to check.

High-risk system & market surveillance: the AI Act sorts software by potential for harm. "High-risk" is the second-highest tier and covers systems that decide about people — who gets invited to a job interview, who gets a loan, who gets seen first in the ER. For this tier, the law requires a thick technical file: what the system can do, what it was trained on, how it's monitored. The market surveillance authority is the body allowed to see that file — like the weights-and-measures inspector who checks the scale at the supermarket: it doesn't build scales, but it can open one up anytime and issue fines.

Check one: the calendar. The official implementation timeline for the Act says, for exactly this class of system: Chapter III, Sections 1, 2, and 3 apply to high-risk systems under Article 6(2) and Annex III starting December 2, 2027; for Annex I systems, starting August 2, 2028.[2] Annex III is the list that covers employment, creditworthiness, and healthcare. It's hard for an agency to check compliance with a documentation duty that doesn't start applying for another fifteen months.

Check two: jurisdiction. In Germany, the Act on Market Surveillance and Innovation Promotion for Artificial Intelligence (KI-MIG) has been in force since 2026-07-29. It makes the Bundesnetzagentur the "market surveillance authority, single point of contact, and complaints office" for the AI Act and sets up a central coordination and competence center there.[3] BfDI is Germany's federal data protection commissioner — a different agency operating under a different law. That it would be the one requesting German AI files would need some explaining.

Check three: who's telling the story. Research for this brief found no release from the Commission, the AI Office, CNIL, or BfDI dated August 30 — only two independent commentary blogs that are near word-for-word identical on the date, sectors, and agency names.[1] Two secondary sources saying the same thing isn't a cross-check; they may just have copied the same original claim.

Assessment I'm not printing the inspection wave as fact. Something real could still be behind it — informal information requests to general-purpose models, for instance, have been underway for a while, and the Article 50 transparency duties have been enforced since 2026-08-02. But "the AI Office has been inspecting high-risk files on-site since August 30" is not, in this form, backed by any reachable primary source, and it doesn't match the published deadline schedule. Anyone who commissions a compliance project off the back of it is buying based on a blog line.

My take. What's interesting isn't whether the piece is true, it's why it works so well. The high-risk tier's deadline moved; plenty of minds and plenty of procurement guides still carry a date from summer 2026. When an expected deadline passes and nothing happens, a gap opens up — and stories that fill that gap spread faster than the timeline that debunks them. For Daniel's clients, the practical takeaway is: don't act on a rumor, produce two sheets of paper instead — what's running in-house, and who's actually responsible for it. That's exactly the first project idea below, and it's cheaper than any panic project.

Two-point-nine points is not a diagnosis [4]

OpenAI unveiled GPT-6 Astra on 2026-09-03. The coding numbers belong in the ai-dev brief; what belongs here is the figure the model is currently being marketed with in healthcare: 63.4 on the length-adjusted HealthBench Professional, versus 60.5 for its predecessor, GPT-5.6 Sol.[4] That's 2.9 points on a scale to 100.

HealthBench Professional (length-adjusted), scale 0–100 — the entire gain in two bars
HealthBench & "length-adjusted": a benchmark is a test for models. HealthBench's test consists of everyday medical conversations, graded against a checklist that doctors wrote in advance — "asked about allergies," "advised calling emergency services." The Professional variant phrases the questions the way clinicians would, not the way laypeople would. Length-adjusted means there's no credit for writing more. Without that correction, the model that lists everything out of caution wins — like a student who writes down the entire syllabus and hopes the right answer is in there somewhere.

The specialist article the number comes from attaches its own editorial warning — not OpenAI itself, but the author, a physician: the score shouldn't be described as "diagnostic accuracy, prospective clinical validation, regulatory approval by any authority, or evidence that Astra can replace clinicians."[4] That reads unusually official, but it's editorial framing, not OpenAI's own wording — and still exactly the caveat that tends to get dropped first on the way from the article to the sales deck.

My take. 2.9 points on a rubric-graded exam is not medical progress, it's measurement noise with marketing value. The number from the same release that actually changes something is further down: roughly 1.05 million tokens of context.[4] That's, roughly speaking, a few thousand pages in a single pass — a complete patient chart, a complete claims file, a complete tender folder. That shifts the failure mode: until now, a system like this failed by not knowing the case. Going forward, it fails by missing something on page 380 — and nobody notices, as long as the answer sounds fluent. The expensive part shifts from the model to checkability: every claim needs its citation, or the tool isn't sign-off-able.

Incidentally, that's the bridge back up top: a system that decides a triage outcome is the high-risk case from section one, with obligations starting 2027-12-02.[2] A reading tool that supplies citations, with a human deciding, is something else. That's why the second project idea below is cut so narrowly — this isn't legal advice, but it's the distinction a proposal hinges on.

Methodology These figures appear in coverage of a product launch that one of the participating consultancies profits from directly. Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with a market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · AI inventory with a jurisdiction radar — against both panic and neglect

ELI5 & value: a mid-sized company is already running five to fifteen AI systems: the chat widget in the webshop, application pre-sorting in the HR tool, demand forecasting in the ERP, an assistant bundled with the office license, an image generator in marketing. Nobody has a list. That produces exactly two expensive mistakes, and both were on display in this very brief today: panic — a blog line claims an inspection wave, and a compliance project gets commissioned that addresses no actual obligation — and neglect: the labeling duty for legacy systems really does expire on 2026-12-02, in 78 days, and it hits the marketing image generator.[2] The product is unglamorous: a dated list. What's running, in what role (provider or deployer), which obligation applies and from when, and which agency is actually responsible. Value: a fine framework of up to €15 million against a project that takes a few days — and the document ends the debate in-house instead of relitigating it every quarter.
Prototype (V0): (1) A two-hour inventory workshop, pulling the system list from invoices and SaaS contracts, not from IT's wish list — what's billed is what's actually running. (2) Four questions per system: your own role, purpose, whether it generates synthetic content or talks directly to people (then Article 50 applies), whether it decides about people in employment, credit, or health (then the Annex III logic applies, dated 2027-12-02).[2] (3) A calendar sheet built from that, containing only the deadlines relevant to this particular client — the rest of the regulation is explicitly left out. (4) One page of evidence per system: screenshot of the disclosure, the vendor's conformity statement, the data-processing agreement. (5) One sheet on "who's responsible": in Germany, the Bundesnetzagentur as the point of contact, and the relevant sector regulator for specialized areas.[3] Deliberately not included: no platform, no continuous monitoring, no legal advice — where a legal judgment call is needed, a lawyer signs off.
Market & gap: the tooling side is well served. The leading governance platforms cited across the board are Credo AI, Holistic AI, OneTrust AI Governance, and IBM watsonx.governance, plus the GRC add-ons ServiceNow AI Control Tower and Collibra, and specialists Modulos, Saidot, and trail; Credo AI pairs a use-case registry with assessments mapped to the EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF, and ISO 42001.[8] How young this category still is shows in the market estimate itself: for the same year, 2026, figures circulate ranging roughly from $0.42 billion to $0.80 billion, depending on the research house — a gap of nearly two.[8] Two gaps remain. First, fit: these platforms are built for enterprises with hundreds of models and a dedicated governance team; a company with nine systems and nobody in charge doesn't need software, it needs an outcome. Second, localization: that the Bundesnetzagentur has been Germany's point of contact since 2026-07-29 appears in no US product catalog[3] — and that's exactly the first question the managing director asks.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. This is consulting with an artifact, not an operation: no hosting, no on-call, no model costs. The template is buildable solo in four to six weeks and then filled in per client in two to four days; maintenance means keeping the deadline sheet current when the timeline changes — half a day per change, shared across all clients. Scaling happens through an annual review appointment per client, not through headcount. Honest catch: revenue is per-project, not recurring — the idea works as a door-opener for the build work that follows, not as a business model on its own. And it only works with a lawyer on call for cases where the classification is disputed. artificialintelligenceact.eu · bundesnetzagentur.de · mordorintelligence.com · precedenceresearch.com

2 · The document reader with mandatory citations — long context windows, made checkable

ELI5 & value: a warranty file in construction, a claims file at an insurance broker, a tender folder in plant engineering: four hundred pages, and just to answer one question — "Was the defect mentioned in the handover protocol?" — someone spends three hours reading. Long-context models can now ingest a file like that in one pass; for Astra, that's roughly 1.05 million tokens.[4] The summary isn't the product here; a fluent summary you still have to double-check saves nothing. The product is mandatory citation: every sentence of the answer carries the page and paragraph it came from. Then the human checks it in thirty seconds instead of three hours. Value: it's not reading time that's saved, it's review time — and only review time can be defended against a liability risk.
Prototype (V0): (1) With the client's subject-matter expert, collect twenty real files and forty questions with known answers — that's both the test set and the later sign-off artifact. (2) Ingest with page anchors preserved; an existing layout parser, nothing custom-built. (3) One long-context pass answers the question. (4) A second pass discards any claim whose page reference can't be found in the text — that's the whole trick, and the reason the output becomes sign-off-able. (5) Output: answer plus citations; a human decides, nothing gets written back into any system. Deliberately not included: no decision-making, no legal assessment, no automatic drafting of correspondence. Building blocks: a long-context model API, an open model on-premises for sovereignty-sensitive cases, a PDF layout parser.
Market & gap: in the legal vertical, the money is already there: Legora raised $550 million at a $5.55 billion valuation in April 2026 and is explicitly going head-to-head with Harvey.[9] In August 2026, reporting said Legora was targeting over $10 billion at $150 million ARR, with Harvey in talks around $15 billion.[10] Luminance owns document analysis and due diligence.[10] Horizontally, Microsoft 365 Copilot and NotebookLM push in the same direction. The gap isn't the technology, it's the combination of industry and burden of proof: these products are cut for law-firm budgets and law-firm workflows, or they're generic office tools with no citation requirement. The construction contractor with warranty files, the industrial insurance broker, the plant builder with tender folders has neither. And the decisive artifact — the test set built from the client's own files — can't be sold as a subscription. That's exactly why nobody builds it.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium. Building it is solo-feasible: V0 in four to six weeks, since the model and parser are bought and only citation-checking, the test set, and the interface need building. The maintenance load is real but bounded and predictable: every model change requires rerunning the test set — an afternoon per change, and that afternoon is itself the sales pitch, since it goes out as a report. Ongoing operations otherwise stay light, since nothing answers around the clock; realistically two to three hours per week per client. Scaling happens through an external developer per client. Sovereignty variant: an open long-context model on owned hardware, noticeably weaker, but no file leaves the building — in insurance and construction, that's often the deciding factor over price. Honest exit point: the moment the client wants not a reading aid but a decision, the risk class changes, and with it liability and price — decline or renegotiate at that point. iatrox.com · techcrunch.com · lawyer-monthly.com

It's Tuesday, September fifteenth. The most useful thing this brief can do today is flag a story that sounds very convincing right now.

For a few days, compliance circles online have been passing around a report that the European AI Office started on-site inspections of high-risk systems at companies on August thirtieth. The sectors named: automated resume screening, credit scoring, medical triage. Even the agencies supposedly leading the charge get named — the French data-protection authority, Germany's federal data-protection commissioner, and Spain's AI oversight body. It's a well-told story. It doesn't hold up to a closer look.

First, the calendar. The obligations in question — the thick technical file for systems that decide about people — don't apply, under the official timeline, until December second, twenty twenty-seven. For the product annexes, not until August twenty twenty-eight. An agency can hardly inspect compliance with a rule that isn't in force yet. Second, jurisdiction. In Germany, since July twenty-ninth, the Bundesnetzagentur — the federal network agency — has been the market-surveillance authority, point of contact, and complaints office for the AI Act, with its own coordination center. The federal data-protection commissioner is not. And third, the sourcing. There's no release from the Commission, none from any of the named agencies — just two commentary blogs that read almost word for word alike.

So I'm not printing the inspection wave as fact. What is dated and real: the labeling requirement for legacy systems that generate synthetic content expires on December second of this year. That's seventy-eight days from today, and it hits perfectly ordinary things, like the image generator sitting in a marketing department.
