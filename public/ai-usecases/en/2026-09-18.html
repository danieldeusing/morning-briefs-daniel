The biggest AI launch of the year — with a waitlist

Apple shipped iOS 27 on 2026-09-14, and with it the newly built Siri AI. The interesting part is how cautiously it's rolling out: the feature isn't simply there, it's waitlist-gated. You sign up under Settings → Siri → "Try Siri AI (beta)" and get unlocked once Apple allows it — requirements: a compatible iPhone (iPhone 15 Pro or later) and the device language set to English.[1][2] Technically it's a hybrid: part of it runs on the device, part in Apple's Private Cloud Compute.

Private Cloud Compute: if a request is too big for the phone, it goes to Apple's own servers — built so the data isn't stored there and no one at Apple can look at it. Like a copy shop that processes your document but keeps no copy and isn't allowed to look at it. Apple's own words: personal data is "neither stored nor made accessible to Apple or anyone else."[2]

My take. A waitlist plus a language filter isn't a marketing trick, it's an admission: an assistant that reads messages, changes appointments, and reaches into apps is a class of risk you don't push to a billion devices at the flip of a switch. The lesson for the deployment business is uncomfortably simple — even the manufacturer with the greatest distribution power in the world rolls out staged, single-language, and revocable. Anyone in the mid-market planning an assistant "for everyone" is planning more carelessly than Apple.

Why the EU iPhone isn't getting the new Siri [3]

On iPhone, iPad, and Apple Watch in the EU, Siri AI isn't available — and, contrary to the reflexive assumption, not because of the AI Act. Apple points to the Digital Markets Act: regulators demanded that competing assistants get practically the same access to the device as Siri, including autonomous action — reading messages, triggering purchases, controlling apps.

DMA interoperability: the Digital Markets Act obliges the big platforms not to connect their own services any more tightly than their competitors'. Like a landlord who has to hand every delivery service the same house key his own courier has. For an assistant, though, "the same key" means access to messages, calendar, and payments.

Apple's counterproposal was an intermediary layer called the Trusted System Agent, through which outside assistants could have used the same capabilities, plus a phased rollout over 18 months. Apple's account of the outcome is terse: "The European Commission said no. In fact, the European Commission did not agree to any of Apple's proposals." No date exists. Siri AI does run on macOS 27 and visionOS 27 in Europe too; the Watch is excluded along with the phone, since it depends on it.

My take. Both positions are defensible: the DMA wants to stop the gatekeeper from widening its own gate; Apple doesn't want to hand a master key to third parties who aren't subject to any security review. But the account is one-sided — this is Apple's press release, not the Commission's minutes, and "unlimited access" is Apple's own phrasing for a requirement whose exact wording we don't know. What stays practically relevant is one thing: in Europe, the assistant layer on the phone remains unoccupied for the foreseeable future. Anyone building a product a customer is meant to "just talk to" needs their own channel — app, web, WhatsApp — and can't wait for the operating system to handle it for them.

The obligation that's been running for 47 days — and the guide to meeting it [5][6][7]

While Apple and the DMA grab the headlines, Article 50 of the AI Act has been quietly in force since 2026-08-02: "On 2 August 2026, new rules on the transparency of AI systems take effect." Enforcement sits with the national market surveillance authorities, the European AI Office, and the European Data Protection Supervisor; the fine range runs up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover. In practice that means two things: anyone talking to an AI system has to be able to recognize it — and AI-generated or -manipulated content has to be marked in a machine-readable way. For the second part, the Commission published the final Code of Practice on 2026-06-10. It's voluntary — the obligation underneath it isn't.

Marked in a machine-readable format: not the small "AI-generated" caption under the image, but a feature in the image itself or its metadata that a program can read out — a watermark plus a provenance record. Like the security thread in a banknote: the cashier only sees it under light, the machine checks it every time. The Commission frames the goal as: outputs should be "marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated."

Germany-specific on top of that: since 2026-07-29, the AI Implementation Act has been in force, which puts BaFin in charge of market surveillance for AI systems used "in direct connection with regulated financial activities." For high-risk systems, the deadline remains 2027-12-02 — 440 days out.

My take. This is the cheapest compliance hour a mid-sized company can invest right now, and almost nobody is spending it. The reason is mundane: the labeling obligation sounds like law and is actually engineering. A lawyer can write down that the marking has to be there; whether it survives the trip from generator through CMS, image resizing, CDN, and social upload is something only measurement can tell you. That becomes the first twiceD idea below.

Two figures deliberately left out First, the report that 24 national authorities began a September inspection wave on hiring, credit, and triage AI: findable only at one low-quality aggregator, confirmed neither by the Commission nor by artificialintelligenceact.eu — which lists the deadline, not an inspection calendar. Second, adoption figures (60.4% in manufacturing, 49% in retail) whose primary source returned a 403 and whose underlying study isn't even named. Both remain unconfirmed.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Labeling pipeline for Article 50 — the technical half

ELI5 & value: for 47 days now, a company deploying AI has had to do two things: make it recognizable to people that they're talking to a machine, and mark AI-generated images, videos, audio, and certain texts in a machine-readable way.[5] Fine range: €15 million or 3% of global turnover. The problem isn't a legal one: a marketing department generates an image, the CMS resizes it, converts it to WebP, the CDN recompresses it, LinkedIn does the same on upload — somewhere along that path the marking quietly dies, and nobody in the company knows where. Value: half a week of work against a liability risk that's already live today, plus a checkable record instead of a statement of intent.
Prototype (V0): (1) Inventory: which generator feeds which channel — CMS, newsletter, shop, social? Two hours of conversation, no software. (2) Marking at the source: C2PA Content Credentials plus the vendor's own watermark, written in at CMS ingest. (3) The survival test — the actual product: send a marked asset through the real chain (resizing, format conversion, CDN, upload to LinkedIn and Instagram) and measure at which stage the marking disappears. That's the finding nobody has today. (4) Chat widget: one disclosure sentence, checked in the embedded widget too. (5) A two-page file: which systems, which marking, which known losses. Deliberately left out: no risk classification, no AI management system, no detector building. Building blocks: the open C2PA spec, the generators' APIs, the Code of Practice as the yardstick.[6]
Market & gap: the Article 50 market is densely occupied — but only on the paper side. TÜV SÜD sells voluntary conformity certificates for AI systems plus gap assessments and training;[9] TÜV Rheinland Consulting sells compliance consulting, the TÜV association sells a compliance check via TÜV AI.Lab, appliedAI trains risk classification; on top of that, law firms and data-protection houses like Proliance, ADVISORI, or Grant Thornton. What these vendors deliver is a document. None of them wires the marking into the client's actual image pipeline, and none of them measures whether it survives that pipeline. That's exactly where the obligation fails in practice, invisibly: the audit report is green, the image on LinkedIn has no metadata left. Gap for twiceD: the Code of Practice says what has to be true; making it true takes someone who can actually read the CMS.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. Consulting plus a small pipeline intervention — no hosting, no on-call duty, no model costs. V0 solo in three to four weeks, then five to eight days per client. Maintenance near zero, with one honest recurring catch: the survival test has to rerun whenever the CMS, CDN, or generator changes — half a day, so a small maintenance contract instead of an invented subscription. Catch: this is not a certificate. Anyone who wants a stamp goes on to TÜV; twiceD is the hand that makes the marking real. And whether a text is "of public interest" is a lawyer's call, not something my offer decides. digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu · tuvsud.com · consulting.tuv.com

2 · The assistant that stays in-house — for BaFin's 51-day-old oversight

ELI5 & value: two facts from today, one conclusion. First: the assistant from the largest device maker doesn't reach EU iPhones, with no date set.[3] Second: since 2026-07-29, BaFin has been overseeing AI systems used in direct connection with regulated financial services.[7] A German financial or insurance provider therefore can neither wait for the platform assistant nor casually type customer data into a US consumer tool. The product: a deliberately small internal assistant on open model weights, operated in Germany or in-house, for exactly one document collection — complete with the data-flow documentation the regulator asks for. Value: an answer in seconds instead of ten minutes in the archive, and a paper trail instead of a shrug.
Prototype (V0): (1) One collection, one team — say, contract and policy documents for claims processing. (2) Open weights on European infrastructure: IONOS AI Model Hub, STACKIT, Deutsche Telekom's Industrial AI Cloud, OVHcloud, or Scaleway — or in-house hardware where data may not leave the building at all.[10] (3) Search over the collection with a citation requirement: no answer without a document and a page number. (4) A fixed acceptance set of 50 real questions with the business unit, pass rate documented. (5) Data-flow documentation plus Article 50 disclosure. Deliberately left out: no company-wide assistant, no agent that writes into systems, no fine-tuning.
Market & gap: the sovereign platform tier exists and is respectable: Aleph Alpha's PhariaAI runs on-premise or in German data centers, but targets critical infrastructure, defense, and public administration; alongside it, IONOS AI Model Hub, STACKIT, and Deutsche Telekom's Industrial AI Cloud — EU-based operation is now a standard offering, not an exception.[10] Two gaps. First, access: prices on request, no self-service tier, no sandbox without a sales call — a 150-person company doesn't get through that door. Second, and sharper: the flag has moved. Cohere and Aleph Alpha announced a merger on 2026-04-24 and signed the definitive agreement on 2026-09-16 — pending regulatory approval, with closing expected later in 2026; the Cohere side would hold about 90% of the combined company, operating worldwide as Cohere.[11] On the agreed terms, Germany's sovereign AI champion is majority Canadian — that just hasn't closed yet. Sober conclusion: sovereignty as a vendor label is worth less than it was a year ago; what matters is where the data actually sits — an integration decision, which is exactly twiceD's layer.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium — and the "medium" is the honest part. Building is easy, operating costs money: patching, watching the GPU bill, rerunning the acceptance set whenever the model changes. Solo-manageable at two to four clients as long as the scope stays at one collection and read-only; beyond that it needs an external developer on call, and that has to be in the price. Cheapest path: hosted EU inference instead of your own GPUs — the sovereignty argument survives (data stays in the EU, documented), the operational load doesn't. Real on-premise only where data genuinely can't leave the building, and then with an operating contract instead of good intentions. schwarz-digits.de · techcrunch.com · bafin.de
