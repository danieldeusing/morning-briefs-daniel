Two numbers from the same survey, five days old, and together they set the business premise for the next two years: 57% of German companies use AI — the majority for the first time, nearly a tripling in 24 months. And not a single one says it's tapping the full potential; 59% say "not at all." The Majority Is In — and No One's Really In [1]

Bitkom Research surveyed 603 companies with 20 or more employees by phone for the digital association, in calendar weeks 28 through 33 of this year; the result was published on 2026-09-14. The finding that made headlines: "For the first time, the majority of companies — 57 percent — are using AI. A year ago it was just 36 percent, two years ago only 20 percent." Association president Ralf Wintergerst sells that as arrival at scale — "Artificial intelligence is not a matter for a handful of pioneers."

AI use among German companies with 20+ employees — Bitkom release dated 2026-09-14, prior-year figures as stated there

Two paragraphs further down sits the number nobody put in a headline: not a single company with AI in use says it's making full use of its potential. 3 percent say "fairly strongly," 28 percent "a little," 59 percent "not at all." At the same time, the money keeps rising: 45 percent of all companies are spending more on AI in 2026 than the year before (13 percent significantly more, 32 percent somewhat more), and half are keeping spending unchanged.

My take: Budget up, utilization practically zero — that's not a technology problem. That's a purchased license sitting on the shelf. The bottleneck has moved from access to use in 24 months, and the sellable value has moved with it: "We're adopting AI" is done as a pitch, because everyone has already done it. What's missing is proof that what got bought actually does something.

Methodology Phone survey with self-assessment: "tapping the potential" isn't a measurement, it's a feeling. A company with three cleanly running processes might honestly tick "a little" because it senses how much more is possible — and another might tick "fairly strongly" because it doesn't know what it's missing. What's reliable is the distribution, not any single value. The 20/36/57 series comes from three consecutive waves of the same Bitkom series, as stated in the 09/14 release itself.

The Cross-Check: In Four Out of Ten Cases, Nobody Knows If It Pays Off [2]

Gartner evaluated 432 AI use cases in customer service. Result: 25 percent deliver a positive return, 25 percent a negative one, 11 percent break even — and for 42 percent, the return is simply unclear. In parallel, service teams run just under five AI initiatives at once on average and tie up about 13 percent of their budget doing it; more than three-quarters still plan to spend more in 2026.

My take: Bitkom's "59 percent not at all" and Gartner's "42 percent unclear" are the same finding seen from two sides. Companies can't say what their AI is doing. That's not a model problem — the models are better than the processes they get plugged into. It's a measurement problem. And a measurement problem is something you can sell as a service without building a model yourself.

Methodology The evaluation carries the Gartner label, but the available coverage states neither the sample construction nor the funding; only the population — "432 use cases" — is documented. It covers one function — customer service — not the whole company. Useful as a direction, not as a precise figure. Per the report, the survey was conducted roughly a month before its 2026-08-17 publication.
  1. customerexperiencedive.com — "Only one quarter of AI customer service use cases produce ROI," 2026-08-17. Trade secondary source on a Gartner evaluation (primary study not freely accessible). Verbatim: "25% of AI customer service use cases produce positive ROI … 25% deliver negative returns … 42% have unclear ROI"; also "customer service teams pursue an average of nearly five AI initiatives while allocating roughly 13% of their budget". Flag: Gartner-branded, but without disclosed sample or funding details beyond the "432 use cases" figure; customer service only, not cross-industry.

Dreamforce: The Sales Assistant Becomes a Colleague [3][4]

At Dreamforce in San Francisco (2026-09-15 through 2026-09-17), Salesforce unveiled AIforce — described as a new "AI interface layer" over the platform. Underneath sit three things with their own names: Agentforce Coworker, a cross-device AI colleague; Slackforce, which makes the CRM operable from within Slack; and Claudeforce, the expansion of the Anthropic partnership with 37 prebuilt sales skills. The AgentExchange marketplace now lists partner agents from AWS, Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, Lovable Labs, Vercel, and DocuSign.

Agent marketplace: Like an app store, except the apps are workers. Instead of programming an assistant yourself, you install a ready-made one that takes over a task in the CRM — drafting quotes, answering service requests, sending contracts out for signature. The catch is the same as with an app store: you only find out what the app is really worth once it's running, and every vendor only tests its own.

As the customer proof point, Hotel Engine gets named, handling "more than 800,000 customer inquiries a year" through the agent stack. That's a volume figure, not a result: it's missing the before value, the handling time, the resolution rate, and the cost. Numbers like that read like proof and aren't — the counter-example would be "from two to five days down to 20 to 30 minutes," i.e., two measured points.

Salesforce's own Agentic Enterprise Index supplies the matching self-measurement: the average organization ran five activated agents in February 2025, already 13 by April 2026; build time per agent fell 53 percent to 1.9 days. That's an adoption index the vendor runs on its own product — it counts agents built and build time, not their value. That an agent can be stood up in under two days, combined with Gartner's 42-percent unclear figure, isn't good news — it's an unsettling one.

The developer part of the keynote — 40 skills for the Claude Code plugin — doesn't belong here: details in the ai-dev brief.

Brussels Asks Questions — of More Than 30 Model Providers [5][6]

On 2026-09-01, the EU Commission's AI Office sent its first formal requests for information: to more than 30 providers of general-purpose AI models. They run along two tracks. The first concerns safety of the most capable models — how they're defended against attacks, whether independent experts have reviewed them, how they're monitored after launch. The second concerns copyright and training-data transparency and, per the report, targets in particular companies that haven't taken part in the AI Office's informal compliance dialogues. Anyone who answers incompletely, incorrectly, or misleadingly risks up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual revenue.

General-purpose AI model (GPAI): A model that wasn't built for one task, but for many — chatting, summarizing, translating, coding. Like a pocketknife instead of a bread knife. Important for context: these obligations hit the knife maker — OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Mistral, and the like — not the mid-sized company slicing bread with it. Their obligation is the transparency rule under Article 50 (a chatbot has to identify itself as AI, deepfakes have to be labeled), in force since 2026-08-02.

My take: Enforcement is starting where it hurts least and generates the most signal — with a manageable number of large providers, using questionnaires instead of raids. For Daniel's client base, nothing changes legally today. What changes is the expectation: whoever buys a model will, within twelve months, be asked by their own procurement department what answers their provider sent to Brussels. Vendor documentation becomes a procurement requirement before it becomes a legal obligation.

Unconfirmed Since late August, a second, far more dramatic version has been circulating: that the AI Office launched sector-specific inspections — applicant screening in HR, credit scoring in retail banking, triage in private clinics — led by CNIL, BfDI, and Spain's AESIA, dated either 08/30 or "sometime in September." The text appears nearly word-for-word on a number of small SEO sites and can't be found at the Commission, Agence Europe, Euractiv, Politico, or Reuters. So it's not being stated as fact here. Anyone currently planning compliance work around it: don't, until a regulator writes it themselves.

Sovereign Agents for Government — Delivery Date: Early 2027 [7]

On 2026-09-16, OpenText and Cohere announced a partnership: an agent stack for governments and regulated industries that connects OpenText's document and information holdings with Cohere's models. Deployable on-premises, or in a private, public, or sovereign cloud. OpenText brings, by its own account, more than 120,000 enterprise customers in 180 countries; Cohere brings roughly $1.6 billion in raised capital. The whole thing is set to reach customers "in early 2027."

Sovereign cloud: Servers in a data center that sits legally and physically within your own territory and is run by an operator subject to that territory's law — so no foreign authority can order the data handed over. Like a safe-deposit box at the branch around the corner instead of a vault abroad: same function, different jurisdiction.

My take: What's interesting isn't the partnership, it's the date. Demand for sovereignty is there today — at municipal utilities, health funds, law firms, energy providers. The product arrives "early 2027." In between lie twelve to fifteen months in which those customers either buy something else or do nothing at all. For a small vendor, that's exactly the window — not to beat the platform, but to get a single process running sovereignly beforehand. On the side: Cohere is the same company that's merging with Aleph Alpha. Europe's sovereignty story keeps running, conspicuously often, through a single vendor — that's not sovereignty, that's a new lump in the throat.

That puts Brazil's banking sector, Gartner's customer service, and the German Mittelstand on the same level: the deployment is real, the proof is missing everywhere.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Utilization Audit — Measuring What the AI You Bought Actually Does

ELI5 & value: 57% of German companies use AI, and not a single one says it's tapping it.[1] In practice that means: licenses are bought — Copilot seats, a CRM assistant, some chat window — and nobody in the building can say who's using them or what they deliver. The audit is a fixed-price engagement over two to three weeks that answers exactly this question: three real workflows, measured before and after, in euros per transaction. The value for the customer is concrete in both directions — either a canceled license line item or a documented saved hour. Both are numbers a management team can act on; "we're AI-capable now" isn't.
Prototype (V0): (1) One client, read access to the usage reports of the suites they're already paying for. (2) Pin down three candidate workflows with the department heads — no more. (3) Pull 30–50 historical transactions per workflow and reconstruct manual handling time from existing ticket/ERP timestamps. (4) Run the same transactions through the already-licensed tool, same metric. (5) One report: euros per transaction, ranked, plus one workflow that actually ends up running productively. Deliberately left out: no model training, no new platform, no agent building. Existing building blocks: the admin/usage reports of the existing suites, the client's ticket history, a spreadsheet. No software gets built for V0 — that's the point.
Market & gap: The measurement exists, but only at the top end. Deloitte writes the technology study for the Brazilian banking association[8] and consults for the same size class; Gartner sells the ROI evaluation as research[2]; Salesforce, in its own index, measures activated agents and build time — explicitly not their value.[4] Bitkom documents the gap year after year and doesn't close it.[1] What's missing is a fixed-price measurement for 20 to 250 employees: the Mittelstand doesn't buy a Big Four engagement, and the vendor will never tell the client their licenses are sitting idle. The gap isn't technology, it's a price bracket.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. A consulting service with no ongoing operations: nothing to host, no on-call duty, no maintenance after sign-off. Two to three weeks solo per engagement, scaling via one external developer per parallel engagement. The risk sits in sales, not delivery — and the door-opener is a number straight out of the press release from the very association the client belongs to. bitkom.org · customerexperiencedive.com · salesforce.com

2 · Agent Acceptance Testing — Regression Tests for Purchased AI Agents

ELI5 & value: Anyone buying software tests it before go-live and again after every update. Nobody does that with purchased AI agents. They get switched on, and the next month the vendor swaps out the model underneath — answer quality slips, and nobody notices until a customer complains. "Agent acceptance testing" is a small test rig: 100 to 300 real transactions from the client's own history, whose correct outcome is known, run against the agent overnight; if the hit rate drops, an email goes out. Value: the 25% of use cases with negative return that Gartner counts are exactly the ones nobody's watching.[2]
Prototype (V0): (1) Export 150 historical transactions, along with the outcome a human produced at the time. (2) Feed them to the purchased agent through its official interface. (3) Two cheap checks: a hard field comparison (correct account number, correct amount) plus an evaluator model for free text. (4) A nightly run, an email if the hit rate drops past a threshold. (5) A one-pager per month. Deliberately left out: no intervention in live operations, no automatic rollback, read-only. Existing building blocks: promptfoo for deterministic test suites or Langfuse for evaluation and observability — both open source and self-hostable.[10] The test rig doesn't get built, it gets assembled.
Market & gap: Testing tools exist — but only per vendor, and only for whoever builds the agent. Salesforce runs its own test environment, the Agentforce Testing Center, where agents run against sample dialogues before rollout[9] — inside Salesforce. promptfoo and Langfuse are excellent and open source, but they're aimed at development teams with a CI pipeline.[10] A business that buys agents has neither. Then there's the portfolio question: per Salesforce's own index, the average organization runs 13 activated agents[4] — spread across vendors, so no single vendor tool covers the whole thing. And Gartner's 42% "unclear ROI"[2] is exactly what a hit rate turns into a number.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium to high. The test rig is open source; twiceD builds the case set, the scoring rules, and the operation — that's the actual work, and it's work for one person. V0 in about four weeks solo. Honest about the load: a nightly run per client is real operational surface, but a limited one — a cron job and an email send, no hosted platform, and if a run fails, nothing stops at the client's end. Scaling via a maintenance contract per client plus one external developer per parallel rollout. On-prem variant: both test rigs run self-hosted, the evaluator model can be a local open model — the case data then never leaves the building. That's the lever with regulated clients, for whom the OpenText/Cohere sovereignty stack doesn't arrive until 2027.[7] salesforce.com · langfuse.com · promptfoo.dev

Documents the existence and purpose of the tool: lifecycle management and testing of autonomous AI agents before go-live, within the Salesforce environment. Flag: the release is nearly two years old; the detailed description of regression suites and multi-turn dialogue replays comes from partner and consulting blogs (including Gearset, Copado), not from the vendor release — used here only as "test environment before rollout" for that reason.
