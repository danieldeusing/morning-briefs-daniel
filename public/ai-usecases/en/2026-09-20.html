An Evaluator With a Company Badge [1][2]

On September 18, 2026, Anthropic introduced Accenture as its first embedded evaluator. Accenture people will now work inside Anthropic and, per the announcement, do exactly three things there: evaluate and red-team models, conduct alignment assessments, and test safeguards. The access is the point: the evaluators are meant to "watch models take shape in training, follow the decisions that govern how those models are built and deployed, and speak directly to employees" — employee-level rights, not visitor rights.

Red-Teaming & Alignment Testing: Red-teaming means hiring someone specifically to break your own system — like a burglar the bank itself pays to test every door before opening. Alignment testing asks the question one level earlier: does the model actually want what we asked it to do — or did it just learn during training to look like it does?

The money is unusually specific: "Anthropic and Accenture each expect to invest at least $1 billion in building capacity in this area over the next five years." A billion each, five years — roughly $400 million a year combined. That's not building a department, that's building an evaluation firm. Anthropic itself frames the purpose carefully: "independent embedded evaluators do not reduce our accountability, but help to make it more verifiable." TechCrunch frames it as a step toward fulfilling Dario Amodei's pledge to have the strongest models reviewed by independent observers; per the report, Anthropic is in parallel talks with METR and other nonprofit evaluators.

My take: Two things are true at once. First, this is more visibility than any regulator has today — the EU AI Office sends information requests, these people sit at the next desk over. Second, "independent" and "embedded" pull in opposite directions: the evaluator gets paid out of a relationship both sides want to continue, and the announcement says nothing about a duty to publish. So the real story here isn't a safety story, it's a market story: a billable service category — AI evaluation — is taking shape before any regulator defines it. Whoever writes the methodology ends up owning the standard.

What's not in there The announcement names no headcount, no start date, no duration, and no rule on whether or how findings get published. Only the investment pledge is solid so far. Everything beyond that — impact, independence, depth of review — is a statement of intent so far, not evidence.

The Other Half: 1,000 Engineers in the Customer's Office [3][4]

Ten days before the evaluator announcement, on September 8, 2026, the same company set up the Accenture Gemini Enterprise Business Group with Google Cloud. Core of it: "establish a 1,000 forward deployed engineer (FDE) workforce to accelerate AI value," built on "Accenture's nearly 50,000 Google Cloud-skilled professionals." Google takes on training these people so they can build custom applications on Gemini Enterprise.

Forward-Deployed Engineer (FDE): A developer who doesn't sit at the vendor's office but at the customer's — working with their real data, in their real workflow, and responsible for making the thing run in production, not just in a demo. Like a furniture store that realizes the cabinet will never get assembled and sends its own carpenter along. Palantir invented the model roughly two decades ago; in 2026, half the industry is copying it.

And there's a named customer figure instead of the usual survey: "YouTube partnered with Accenture and Google Cloud to deploy a Gemini Enterprise agent during NFL Sunday Ticket surge demand, boosting customer sentiment by 11% and slashing average handle time by 37%." A concrete load case, two metrics, a named customer — that's the format that matters more in this brief than any adoption percentage.

TechCrunch supplied the context that makes the announcement interesting, the same day: Google is training "up to 1,000 of the consultancy firm's FDEs" — and is therefore late to it. Microsoft set up its FDE practice in March 2026, Amazon has its own unit, OpenAI runs it under its own name as "The Deployment Co.," and Anthropic also places engineers in customer environments. The reasoning is the same everywhere: "enterprises have simply lacked the expertise to intelligently integrate AI tools and services into their workflows."

License purchased→ Process stays as it was→ no measurable result→ Vendor sends its own people

My take: When five vendors independently arrive at "we have to send our own people," that's not a service upgrade, it's a verdict on the product. Self-service doesn't work. For twiceD, that's the best news of the week and the most uncomfortable one at once: the bottleneck is exactly the work a small shop can sell — but Accenture's minimum deal size will never fit a 200-person business in the Sauerland. The gap isn't technical. It's a price bracket.

Kurian's Numbers — and the One I'm Leaving Out [5]

Thomas Kurian spoke at the Goldman Sachs Communacopia conference on that same September 8; Google Cloud published the recap on September 11, 2026 on its own blog. The numbers: "We have 17 product lines with more than $1 billion in revenues," "more than 300 customers each with $100 million-plus contractual commitments," "more than 2x quarter-over-quarter and year-over-year growth in the number and value of $100 million to $1 billion deals" — and a "2-year AI server payback period." The one that matters most to us is tucked in almost as an aside: "Google Cloud customers that use our AI products use 1.8 times as many products as those who do not."

Methodology This is an investor appearance, recapped on the vendor's own blog — not an audited statement. The 1.8× is a correlation, not a causal claim: large customers buy AI and buy more of everything. The two-year payback figure is the vendor's own model assumption about its own hardware, not an audited metric. And the widely cited figure "over 90% of the Fortune 100 use Gemini Enterprise" does not appear in this curated Google blog post — but it's verbatim, from Kurian's own remarks, in the official investor-relations transcript of the same conference. The aggregators cited it correctly; they were just quoting the full conference record, not the blog.

My take: The most interesting figure isn't the revenue, it's the 1.8×. It's the business case for why Google trains 1,000 outside engineers for free. Every FDE sitting with a customer isn't a cost center, it's a sales channel with a company badge — the first to see which process needs a product next. That's exactly why this model won't disappear once the models get better.

The Agent That Runs for Three Weeks [6]

The seven agents with first names — Casey, Paige, Carter, Hunter, Marshall, Piper, Fin — already appeared here on September 14. What got lost back then is the part that's actually technically new: on September 11, 2026, Salesforce simultaneously announced "a new long-horizon runtime for Agentforce, enabling agents to pursue goals across days and weeks instead of completing only a task or interaction." The sales agent Hunter is running on it in pilot; it's set to go generally available in November '26.

Long-Horizon Runtime: Until now, an AI agent worked like a phone call — question in, answer out, done, memory gone. A long-horizon runtime is more like an intern with a work order: it has a goal ("qualify these 200 contacts"), keeps working on it for weeks, remembers what it's already tried, and checks back in on its own.

My take: This shifts the risk class, not just the feature list. A chatbot that gets something wrong costs one conversation. An agent that pursues the wrong goal for three weeks costs three weeks — plus everything it sent out in the company's name in the meantime. Starting in November, the control question is no longer "was the answer right," it's "who would have caught it by day nine." Nobody ships an answer to that yet. That, incidentally, is a business opportunity — see below.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Embedded Engineer, Sized for SMEs

ELI5 & value: Half the industry admitted this month that purchased AI only pays off if someone sits inside the customer's process for weeks and builds it in there. Accenture bills that with up to 1,000 heads. A 200-person business gets none of those heads — and buys licenses that sit on the shelf instead. twiceD sells the same shape at one-person scale: six to eight weeks inside a single real workflow, one agent in one real process, documented handoff. The value isn't "AI," it's a measured before-and-after number — in the range Google reports for YouTube: 37% shorter handle time in one load case.[3]
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick a process with a measurable baseline number — handle time, cycle time, error rate — and measure that number for two weeks before building anything. (2) Build on what the customer already licenses (Microsoft 365 Copilot, Gemini Enterprise, Agentforce) — no proprietary model, no proprietary platform. (3) One agent, one workflow, human confirms before every write step. (4) Handoff as the product: a runbook plus the same measurement setup, so the customer can repeat the number without twiceD. Deliberately out of scope: platform migration, a second process, hosting.
Market & gap: The field is crowded and growing fast: Accenture is staffing up to 1,000 forward-deployed engineers with Google Cloud, built on roughly 50,000 Google Cloud-skilled professionals;[3] Microsoft has had a comparable practice since March 2026, Amazon has its own unit, OpenAI runs it as "The Deployment Co.," Anthropic also places engineers in customer environments.[4] In Germany, Capgemini, adesso, and msg play the same role a size smaller. What ties them all together: their model only pays off at a project size where a bench of 1,000 people amortizes. So the gap isn't technical, it's a price bracket — the €40–80k project with one process and the owner in the room is served by none of them. twiceD's leverage: German contract language, GDPR and on-prem instincts, and the willingness to commit to exactly one process instead of selling a platform.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High — this is billable time with close to zero maintenance load, because the handoff is the product. Realistically 20–30 hrs/week during an engagement, essentially zero after. Scale via one contractor per parallel engagement, not a department. Honest downside: the model doesn't compound — no product, no recurring revenue, revenue equals hours. The only part that's reusable across customers is the measurement setup from step 1; that's worth deliberately maintaining as its own asset. newsroom.accenture.com · techcrunch.com

2 · Operational Guardrails for Long-Running Agents

ELI5 & value: Starting in November, Salesforce is selling agents that pursue a goal over weeks.[6] Inside the customer organization today, nobody owns the question: what is this thing doing on day nine, and what has it cost by then? What's needed is a control surface for the business side — not for developers: a cost cap, a kill switch, a plain-language decision log, an escalation rule. The value is avoided risk: an agent doing the wrong thing in the company's name for three weeks is a different damage class than a wrong chat reply. (Distinction from the September 19 idea: that one was vetting before the purchase — this one is operating afterward.)
Prototype (V0): (1) Take a long-running agent the customer already operates. (2) Feed its traces into Langfuse — MIT-licensed, free to self-host, Docker/Kubernetes/Terraform. Tracing does not get built in-house. (3) On top of that, a single page for the business side: goal, progress, cost so far, the last five decisions in plain sentences, a stop button. (4) Two hard rules: a budget cap and an escalation trigger on defined events. Deliberately out of scope: a proprietary model, proprietary tracing, multi-tenant SaaS.
Market & gap: Agent observability exists and is good — Langfuse (Berlin, MIT license, free self-hosting), Arize Phoenix, Datadog LLM Observability. Langfuse describes itself as "Trace, evaluate, and improve AI agents with one open platform" and explicitly as "made for developers." That's exactly where the gap is: the SME that bought an agent doesn't have a developer to read a trace. What's needed is translating developer telemetry into a business control surface, plus the audit trail a compliance officer can present. Honest data flag: I didn't find a solid market size for this segment today — but the ownership question did resolve: Langfuse was acquired by ClickHouse in January 2026 (primary-sourced, ClickHouse's own blog) as part of its $400M Series D; MIT license and free self-hosting remain, per ClickHouse. That changes nothing for the idea — the tool stays open and self-hostable. There's still no sizing figure for the observability market here.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium — building it is small, because the heavy lifting sits with Langfuse; operating it isn't. Whoever provides a control surface for a customer's running agent is effectively on call. Only viable in one of two shapes: "we build, you operate" with a fixed support window, or exactly one retainer customer at a time. My estimate for the operating case: 10–15 hrs/week per active customer — that's an experience-based estimate, not a source. Sovereignty angle as a selling point: Langfuse runs fully on-prem, traces never have to leave the building. langfuse.com · salesforce.com
