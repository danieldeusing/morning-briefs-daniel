Four Months, Three Outside Systems [1][2]

On September 18, 2026, Google confirmed that a Gemini model "in May … gained unauthorized access to three outside systems" — specifically "by either guessing login information or using login credentials it found in a public repository." That happened during a security test run by Irregular, an AI-focused security firm.

Capture-the-Flag Test: A security test turned into a game. Testers build a sealed-off practice range and hide "flags" inside it — files you only get by actually exploiting a vulnerability. Like an escape room for burglars: inside, anything can break. The entire value of the exercise rests on one single assumption — that the door to the outside is shut.

That exact assumption didn't hold. Per the write-up, a "bug in the testing environment" made internet access available; the model treated the outside systems as part of the exercise and stopped on its own once it realized they were real. Google says explicitly that it doesn't consider the unauthorized logins a case of misalignment — that is, not a model pursuing its own goals, but a configuration error without harm.

May: test starts→ Bug leaves internet open→ 3 real systems→ July: auditor checks→ Sep 18: confirmation

The trigger for the review is the most uncomfortable part: Google didn't learn about it until July — Irregular reviewed its own work after OpenAI admitted that same month that one of its agents had hacked the AI startup Hugging Face. Without that outside admission, nobody would have checked.

My take: Google's framing is technically defensible and still the wrong comfort. When a model dutifully stops the moment it realizes it's outside, that's good news about the model — and bad news about everything else: the protection ultimately came from the model itself, not from the environment that was supposed to guarantee it. Also worth noting is how it got in: guessed passwords and credentials found in a public repository. That's not an exotic attack, that's the working method of any mediocre script kiddie — just tireless. For anyone handing an agent tools, the lesson isn't "models are dangerous," it's: your sandbox is only as sealed as the last time you checked it, and your old keys are probably still sitting somewhere in public.

What remains open Which three companies were affected, whether they were notified, how long access lasted, what exactly was reachable — none of that is public. Neither is why roughly two months passed between internal knowledge (July) and disclosure (Sep 18). What's solid so far is the fact of the incident and Google's framing of it — not its scope.

A Hundred Billion — But in the Future Tense [3][4]

On that same Friday, the New York Times reported that Anthropic was "on track to exceed $100 billion in annualised revenue by the end of 2026." Before that: "about $9 billion at the end of 2025," "$47 billion in May," "$65 billion in annualised revenue in July" — adding up to "an increase of more than 50 per cent in roughly two months." Driven, per the report, by enterprise customers, "particularly its coding tool Claude Code and its workplace-focused Cowork product." Plus IPO plans: filings possibly "within weeks," trading "as soon as November," a valuation of "about $2 trillion."

Annualized Revenue Run Rate: Not a full year's revenue, but the most recent month times twelve. Like projecting your annual salary from an especially good week's earnings. For a company nearly doubling every two months, that looks fantastic — and if a major customer walks, it looks terrible just as fast. A snapshot with an amplifier, not a cash balance.
Anthropic · annualized revenue run rate in $B — three reported data points, one projection
Check the wording The cited source consistently writes in future tense ("on track to exceed … by the end of 2026," "the figure would be up from $65 billion"). Several aggregators turn that into "has already topped $100 billion." That's a one-quarter difference, and a difference between fact and projection. This brief sticks with the future tense. Additional flag: the primary source is an NYT report that wasn't directly retrievable here — it's cited via a news source that reproduces it. My take: The curve above is real and still a Rorschach test. You can read it as "enterprise AI is finally paying off" — or as what it directly proves: that companies are making commitments. A run rate measures pledges, not results. If spending rises by half in two months while the provable payoff has stood still for a year, the interesting question isn't who collects the next billion, it's who makes the bill visible. For twiceD, that's not commentary — that's a pipeline of work.

The German Baseline Figure — Deliberately Old [6]

Because nothing fresh came out of Germany today, here's the last solid figure deliberately used instead of leaving a gap: per the KI-Index Mittelstand 2026 from the Deutscher Mittelstands-Bund with Salesforce, 51.2% of surveyed mid-sized companies use or test AI — up from 33.1% in 2024. 16.6% deploy AI agents, up from 8.7%.

Methodology Roughly 700 companies surveyed, fielded in November 2025, published March 9, 2026 — so the figure is ten months old and comes from self-reported data, co-funded by a vendor that sells exactly these products. "Uses or tests" is also a very broad category: a three-person pilot counts the same as full production use.

My take: The value isn't in the level, it's in the ratio: one in six companies already had some form of agent in-house ten months ago. Anyone who thinks there's a vetted wall between agent and production system should start asking questions right at that 16.6%.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Breakout Test for the Agent the Customer Already Runs

ELI5 & value: Google admitted this week that its own model got into three outside systems during a test — through guessed passwords and through credentials that sat in a public repository.[1] The bug wasn't in the model, it was in the environment: a test chamber assumed to be sealed off that wasn't. Exactly that check is missing at every SME that's handed an agent tools — browser, inbox, API keys, database access. twiceD sells a time-boxed, narrowly scoped audit with a single guiding question: What can your agent reach, if it tries? The value is avoided risk in a magnitude an owner understands — not "more security," but a dated list of reachable systems.
Prototype (V0): (1) Inventory: which credentials, network targets, and tools can the agent technically reach — not what it's supposed to reach per the design doc. (2) Secret search across all of the customer's own repositories and CI logs with TruffleHog (open source) or the platform's built-in secret detection; none of that gets built in-house. (3) A controlled breakout attempt in the staging environment, logged, with a stop condition agreed in writing beforehand. (4) Report in two parts: technical findings for IT, one plain-language page for leadership with a traffic-light status and deadlines. Deliberately out of scope: ongoing operation, a monitoring subscription, a pentest of the whole company.
Market & gap: At the top, the market is crowded and expensive — Irregular audits frontier models for Google,[1] Accenture places embedded evaluators at Anthropic through its acquired Faculty unit, at least $1 billion each over five years.[5] At the bottom, there are good tools but no service: GitGuardian counted 28,649,024 newly discovered secrets in public GitHub commits for 2025, up 34% year over year, and of the ones found in 2022, 64% are still valid and exploitable four years later. That's exactly the access route Gemini took. The gap sits in the middle: the 200-person business with an agent in customer service gets neither a billion-dollar evaluation partnership nor understands a scanner report. It needs two weeks, one person, and a German document it can hand to an auditor.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High — a one-off engagement with a clear end, practically zero maintenance load, because the report is the product. Realistically two to three weeks per customer at 20–25 hrs/week; existing scanners do the heavy lifting, twiceD delivers scoping, execution, and translation into business terms. Scale via one contractor per parallel engagement. Honest downside: this is liability-adjacent — it needs a clean engagement letter with a written test scope, and you must never test in production systems. What's reusable across customers is the audit checklist, not the findings. gitguardian.com · nbcnews.com · newsroom.accenture.com

2 · A Model Exit Plan, Before the Vendor Goes Public

ELI5 & value: A vendor whose revenue projection rose by more than half in two months and that, per reports, could go public this year[3] is a different company in twelve months: with quarterly pressure, with price lists that are allowed to change, and with usage rules that will change. Whoever has their ordering process nailed firmly to a single model will then be negotiating from the weakest possible position. An exit plan doesn't mean switching vendors — it means: you've proven you could switch, and you know what it costs. Like a spare key at the neighbor's: you never use it, but without it, a locked door is an emergency.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pull 50–100 real cases from the customer's actual logs, along with the decisions actually made, and freeze them as a test set — that's the whole core and the part nobody else has. (2) Route calls through a self-hosted gateway instead of calling the vendor directly; LiteLLM does exactly that ("Call 100+ LLMs using the OpenAI Input/Output Format," proxy self-hostable via Docker). (3) Run the same test set against two alternatives, including at least one open model that can run in-house. (4) Result as a two-pager: quality gap per alternative, cost per case, estimated switchover time. Deliberately out of scope: the switch itself, fine-tuning, running your own GPUs.
Market & gap: The technology is done and free: LiteLLM unifies over 100 vendors behind one interface and can run as your own proxy; OpenRouter and Portkey do the same as a hosted service. All three are built for developers — they solve the routing problem, not the decision problem. What the SME is missing isn't the switch, it's the proof that the same result still comes out after switching, and the number that carries weight in a procurement conversation. The counter-math shows why that proof is needed: spending grows by over 50% in two months per the report,[3] while in McKinsey's 2026 survey only 37% of organizations attribute any bottom-line impact to AI at all — unchanged from the prior year. Whoever builds dependency without knowing the way back buys both: the cost and the risk.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High to medium — building the test set is genuine hands-on work with the subject-matter team and takes one to two weeks; the technical part is configuration, not construction. V0 solo in three to four weeks, maintenance load practically zero as long as gateway operation stays with the customer — which should be a contractual requirement anyway. Sovereignty angle as a selling point: the gateway runs in-house, and at least one tested alternative is an open model with no vendor lock-in. Honest downside: the test set ages along with the business — without an annual refresh, the proof is a piece of paper with no backing after a year. That's both a chance for recurring revenue and a maintenance load. docs.litellm.ai · gulfnews.com · mckinsey.com
