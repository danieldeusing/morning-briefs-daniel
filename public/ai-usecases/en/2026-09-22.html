The Majority Is In — Yet the Old Number Keeps Getting Quoted [1][2]

Bitkom announced on September 14, 2026: "For the first time, the majority of companies — 57 percent — use AI." Alongside it, a look back: "A year ago it was just 36 percent, two years ago only 20 percent." As far as solid German figures go, this is the threshold: for a company here, using AI is now the default, not the exception.

Share of companies in Germany using AI — the three waves Bitkom itself places side by side (in %)
Representative self-reporting: Nobody actually checked inside 603 companies whether AI is running there. They called and asked, and someone answered. Like a survey on exercise habits: the result reliably measures what people say about themselves — and only roughly what they do. "Representative" here just means the sample mirrors the real economy by size and industry, not that the answers were verified.
Methodology 603 companies with 20+ employees, surveyed by phone, fieldwork weeks 28 through 33 of 2026, conducted by Bitkom Research on behalf of the digital trade association Bitkom. Two caveats belong here: Bitkom is a trade association whose members sell exactly these products — adoption numbers aren't a neutral outcome for it. And "uses AI" carries no defined threshold: three people with a chat subscription count the same as a fully integrated production system. Companies under 20 employees don't appear at all — the larger share of German businesses.

The error currently making the rounds. A different figure, 41%, keeps circulating in parallel, with the addendum "48 percent are planning or discussing it." That figure isn't invented — it's from a Bitkom release dated March 11, 2026, from a different survey wave: 604 companies, fieldwork weeks 2–6/2026. Anyone who turns that into a jump from 41 to 57 in half a year is adding two different measurements together. The two waves even name different prior-year baselines for the same metric: the March release states "A year ago, only 17 percent had AI in use," while the September release says 36 percent for "a year ago." Both cannot be measuring the same thing.

March wave: 41%≠ Sept wave: 57%→ The gap isn't growth

My take: The clean reading is the simplest one — use one series and write its date next to it. The September series reads 20 → 36 → 57 and is internally consistent; anything else needs justifying first. In practice: every proposal and grant application citing a German adoption figure needs the survey date in the same line. More important, though, is what the majority threshold does to the sales conversation. "Should we even bother?" is settled. The interesting prospect from now on isn't the one without AI — it's the one who's had something running for two years and can't say what it delivered. Good footing for consulting work, bad footing for anyone still selling introductory workshops.

What the 57 Percent Actually Do [1]

The breakdown is less flashy than the headline, and useful for exactly that reason. AI works in German companies "most often … in customer contact, such as handling inquiries (72 percent), as well as in marketing and communication (54 percent)." Those are the two areas where a text model contributes immediately without hooking into any specialized system — a request comes in, a draft goes out.

The more interesting movement sits further down: internal knowledge management at 22 percent, up from 11 percent the year before. Doubled, and still only one company in five. That's exactly where the line runs between "we use AI" and "we built something": a subscription is enough for customer inquiries; for your own knowledge, somebody has to touch your own data.

Expectation, not evidence On employment, the same release states: 45% expect a slight and 12% a marked decline in headcount due to AI over the next two years. Those are respondents' expectations about the future — the weakest category of survey data, not counted jobs. The contrast within the sample is telling: among companies already using AI, only 9% expect a marked decline; among non-users, more do. Whoever knows it expects less upheaval than whoever fears it.

My take: The gap between 72 and 22 percent is the real story in this survey. Three in four use AI where they don't have to integrate anything; barely one in five where it's impossible without integration. The second figure is growing faster, but from very low — and it describes the work a mid-sized company has no department for. Anyone selling services should work in that gap, not in the headline above it.

Sound Is Getting Cheap [3][4]

Alibaba's Qwen team released Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash on September 18, 2026: a model that takes text, image, audio, and video directly, with a "Context Window: 1M tokens (991K max input, 131K max output)." Across 29 evaluations, the average score improves by more than 25 percent over its predecessor, per the vendor. But the number that matters in practice is a different one: per the published metrics, the cost of ingesting an hour of audio drops by more than 98 percent, and by more than 93 percent for mixed audio-video material.

Natively multimodal + context window: "Natively multimodal" means the model hears the audio track itself, instead of having a second program transcribe it to text first. Like the difference between a conversation and a transcript of one — tone, pauses, and talking over each other don't survive the detour. The "context window" is how much it can hold in view at once: a million tokens is roughly a few thousand pages, or many hours of recording, all at once instead of piece by piece.
Two caveats First: all improvement and cost figures are vendor claims about its own product, measured against its own predecessor model. No independent verification exists. Second, and more decisive for German customers: no open weights were announced — the model exists only as a hosted interface. For recordings of customers and employees, that's not a footnote, it's the first question. The model and benchmark side of this story belongs in the ai-dev brief; only the use-case impact counts here.

My take: With model announcements, answer quality is almost always the headline and the unit price is almost always the real story. A 98-percent price drop doesn't improve anything already running today — it moves the line past which something becomes worth opening up at all. Audio has long been the textbook case: every company sits on hotline recordings, meeting recordings, voice memos from field technicians, and nobody has ever analyzed them because the minute of audio cost money. That math is tipping right now — and what comes of it is no longer a model problem, it's the usual unglamorous work: a well-defined dataset, an agreed evaluation grid, a clean privacy path. That's exactly why it appears below as a project idea, not a product tip.

Open Weights With a Date: October 15 [5][6][7]

The Chinese lab StepFun introduced Step 5 Preview: per matching trade reports, "600 billion total parameters, 27 billion active parameters per token, a 1 million-token context window and vision input," aimed at agentic software development, financial analysis, and professional knowledge work. It's reachable via API now; the weights are set to be released on October 15, 2026.

Open weights: The "weights" are the numerical values that make up a fully trained model — its entire capability as a file. Open means: you download that file and run it on your own machines instead of sending every request to the vendor. Like the difference between a book you bought and one you're only allowed to read in someone else's rooms. For data that can't leave the building, that's the only architecture that's even an option.

For everything else, caution applies. The circulating operating figures — roughly $1 per million input tokens, $2.70 per million output tokens, and roughly 100 tokens per second of output — sit in a single trade report and haven't been cross-checked. Even the announcement's own publication date isn't cleanly dated in the available reports; the week is solid, the day is not.

Thinly sourced Price and throughput come from one source [7]. Without a second independent figure, they're good for order of magnitude, not for a calculation. Anyone planning around them should check the vendor's own price list on the day they actually use it.

My take: For this brief, only one line about Step 5 Preview truly matters, and it's a date. An announcement of open weights isn't an option, it's a date on which an option gets created — and it regularly falls through, or arrives with a license that restricts commercial use. Until October 15, Step 5 belongs on the watchlist, not in an architecture. What's usable from this today is negotiating leverage: anyone talking to a vendor about a multi-year contract negotiates better knowing that a model in this weight class might be ready for self-hosting three weeks later. The model and benchmark comparison itself belongs in the ai-dev brief.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Audio Mining: The Backlog That Never Paid Off

ELI5 & value: Almost every mid-sized company has recordings nobody ever listens to again: service-hotline call recordings, recorded meetings, voice memos technicians leave from their car, sign-off protocols dictated on the spot. Nobody has analyzed any of it, because a minute of audio cost money and the useful part was buried inside. That math is tipping right now: per vendor metrics, the cost of ingesting an hour of audio drops by more than 98 percent.[4] twiceD sells no product on the back of this, but a time-boxed project with a single question: What's actually in the archive you already have? Concrete value: which twenty complaints keep recurring, which product eats 40 percent of hotline minutes, which promise sales routinely makes that the contract doesn't cover. The first saves calls; the last avoids disputes.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick one well-defined dataset — twelve months of a single hotline queue, not "all recordings." (2) Don't build transcription in-house: either the hosted Omni model, or — if the recordings can't leave the building — a self-hosted speech-recognition setup. (3) The real core: agree an evaluation grid with the subject-matter team — fixed fields (product, error class, promise made, escalation level) instead of free-text summaries. Fields can be counted; summaries can't. (4) Output as a table into the existing ticketing or reporting system, plus 10 hand-checked samples against the original recording as a stated error rate. (5) The privacy and works-council path gets settled before step 1, not after. Deliberately out of scope: real time, voice bots, speech output, ongoing operation.
Market & gap: The top end is bought up and expensive: NICE closed its acquisition of the Düsseldorf vendor Cognigy on September 8, 2025, for roughly $955 million — the largest acquisition in the company's history; Cognigy serves over 1,000 brands, including Allianz, Bosch, DHL, Lufthansa, and Mercedes-Benz. The tooling layer below that is commodity: Deepgram, AssemblyAI, and Speechmatics sell speech recognition as an interface, with Speechmatics offering containerized, on-device, and air-gapped installs while AssemblyAI routes data through US infrastructure — for German customers, a difference that decides the choice on its own. Market-size figures here are notably soft: three research houses put 2026 speech-analytics at roughly $4.77 billion (at 26.1% growth), $5.70 billion (13.15%), and $4.2 billion (15.7%). When three estimates diverge by a third, and by a factor of two on growth, the number is decoration — the $955 million from an actual transaction is the more solid signal. The gap sits in between: one side sells the minute, the other a platform for the enterprise contact center. Nobody sells the 200-person company the answer to twelve months of backlog. And the German bottleneck isn't recognition quality — it's the path through privacy law and the works council for recordings of employees and customers. That's implementation work, not a model problem.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium to high. A project with an end: the product is a grid, a table, and a stated error rate — not an ongoing service. Realistically four to six weeks solo at 20–25 hrs/week, and the larger share of that sits with the subject-matter team and the data-protection officer, not in code. Maintenance load near zero as long as processing runs at the customer's site rather than being self-hosted — which is exactly how it should read in the contract too. The sovereignty variant (in-house speech recognition, an open model) is the strongest selling point and also the line item where the hours go: offer it separately, never bake it into the base price. Honest downside: without a works-council agreement on recordings, the project can hang for months on something twiceD can't speed up. That question belongs in the first conversation. cognigy.com · eu-startups.com · calcalistech.com (largest acquisition in company history) · deepgram.com · thebusinessresearchcompany.com · fortunebusinessinsights.com · technode.com

2 · Troubleshooting Assistant for Machinery Service: One Photo, the Whole Manual

ELI5 & value: A machine is down at a customer site. The technician calls the manufacturer's service hotline, and the person on the other end needs to know: which of forty machine types, which build year, which error code — the answer sits in a 600-page manual, a wiring diagram, and three old service reports. A model with a million-token context can hold an entire machine family's complete documentation in view at once, and as of last week can take the technician's phone photo or ten-second clip as input instead of just their description.[3] The value comes down to a single metric: avoided second dispatches. Every saved return trip is a technician's day plus travel cost — a service manager can do that math themselves in the first meeting.
Prototype (V0): (1) One machine family, not the whole catalog. (2) Load the documentation plus two years of service reports for that family — with a million-token context, you can skip building a document-search layer for the first version; that's exactly the V0 time savings. (3) Input: photo or short video, error code, one sentence. Output: three hypotheses ranked by likelihood, each with the exact document passage it's drawn from — no citation, no output. (4) Test against 30 closed historical cases with a known root cause, and report the hit rate at rank 1 and within the top 3. (5) Hand it to two hotline staff as a second opinion, never as the answer. Deliberately out of scope: spare-parts ordering, dispatch scheduling, AR overlays, customer access.
Market & gap: This space is occupied, but cut differently. PTC sells ServiceMax AI, a generative assistant that draws on equipment history stored in the ServiceMax platform. Augmentir is an AI-native platform for frontline manufacturing and service workers. Siemens runs Senseye, acquired in 2022, for predictive maintenance. Aquant plugs natively into Oracle Field Service, SAP FSM, Salesforce Field Service, ServiceMax, and ServiceNow, and touts up to 26% service-cost savings in its own 2026 benchmark report — a vendor figure, to be read accordingly. The field-service-software market is estimated for 2026 at $6.26 billion or $6.7 billion depending on the source; here too, the spread devalues the number. What matters is the shared assumption underneath: every one of these products assumes the equipment history already sits in a platform. The 150-person machine builder has it in PDFs, on a network drive, and in two people's heads — and doesn't buy a field-service suite just to get an assistant. That's exactly where the gap opens: where the documentation itself is the data, a million-token context makes the project possible without a data project up front.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium. V0 solo in four to six weeks; the hard work isn't the build, it's a clean document set and 30 reliably matched historical cases — the customer has to supply those, and rarely manages it on the first try. Maintenance load is real but bounded: documentation changes per machine generation, so a refresh per product line, not per week — that's a maintenance contract, not an ongoing burden. V0 runs on a hosted interface; the sovereignty variant with open weights in-house is what makes the sale possible to companies that won't hand engineering documents to a US or a Chinese cloud — and StepFun's announced October 15 release is exactly one more candidate for that shelf.[6] Honest downside: this concerns repair guidance for safety-relevant equipment. It can only be shipped as a sourced second opinion, responsibility stays with the technician, and that has to be written into the contract — otherwise, don't take the job. ptc.com · augmentir.ai · siemens.com · aquant.ai · mordorintelligence.com · marktechpost.com
