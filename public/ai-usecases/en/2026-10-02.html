Dots and Space: Agents That Never Clock Out [1][2]

At DevDay on 2026-09-29 in San Francisco, OpenAI showed off two consumer-facing products alongside its new models. InfoQ sums them up this way: "DevDay also introduced Dots, persistent agents that can work on ongoing tasks" and "ChatGPT Space, a shared workspace where teams and agents can work with common context." So a Dot isn't a chat waiting for the next question. It gets an ongoing task and keeps working on it between conversations.

Further details come from search summaries of OpenAI's announcement, because the page itself wasn't reachable. According to these reports, each Dot gets its own machine with a browser in the cloud. Inside a Space, a Dot can join a page or reply to a comment. Access is said to be rolling out gradually, first to Pro and Business Premium accounts in eligible markets. Whether the EU is included couldn't be confirmed. The claim that Dots run on "GPT-6 Astra" shows up in only a single search summary and isn't treated as confirmed here.

Persistent agent: A regular chatbot is a calculator. It computes when you type and stops when you stop. A persistent agent is more like an intern with their own desk. On Monday they get a task, say "watch the prices of our three competitors," work on it independently through the week, and check in when something changes. The "own machine with a browser" is that desk: the agent can open pages, click around, and save files without your laptop even being on.
Sourcing What's confirmed by two independent reports is that both products exist and what their underlying idea is. The details on access and rollout are summaries of the OpenAI page without a verbatim retrieval. The model claim is only thinly sourced. The model and pricing side of DevDay is covered in the ai-dev brief.

My take: For a company, the interesting question about a Dot isn't how good it is. What matters more is who's supervising it while it runs. With a chat, there's always a human in front of it; with an agent that has its own browser, there isn't. That changes three things nobody in the Mittelstand has regulated yet. First: what credentials does the agent work with? Second: what's it allowed to send without asking first? Third: who reads the log? And there's one detail that's easy to miss. The moment a Dot answers a customer inquiry, an AI is talking to a human. In the EU, that's covered by the Article 50 labeling requirement in force since August (more on that on the right). Anyone who wants to roll out Dots needs a page of rules first, not a subscription.

88 Percent Use It, 37 Percent See Anything From It [3]

McKinsey's annual "The State of AI" survey, per a report from 2026-08-26, arrives at this finding: "88% of organizations now regularly use AI in at least one business function." But "only 37% of respondents say they can attribute any EBIT impact to their AI use." Only about 6 percent count as "High Performers" — firms that credit AI with a clear, company-wide contribution to profit. The same report also notes: 80 percent of respondents say AI has improved their personal productivity.

88% use AI→ 37% attribute any EBIT effect→ ~6% with a clear profit contribution
EBIT effect: EBIT is the profit from the actual business, before interest and taxes. "Attributing an EBIT effect" means pointing at a line in the income statement and saying: these costs went down because of AI, or this revenue went up because of it. That's something entirely different from "I'm faster with my email." Whether that saved hour shows up as money anywhere isn't something the second kind of claim measures at all.
Methodology & Sourcing Backed only by a secondary source. The McKinsey page itself timed out on retrieval. All figures come from the summary at enterprisedna.co. According to that summary, 1,719 professionals and executives worldwide were surveyed. Fieldwork dates and country count only circulate in search summaries and are therefore left out here. On top of that come the usual caveats: it's self-reported by managers, "uses AI in at least one function" has no minimum threshold, and the study comes from a consultancy that sells exactly this kind of implementation. Before anyone puts this number in a proposal, it belongs checked against the original.

My take: What's interesting is the gap between 80 and 37. Almost everyone feels more productive; a good third find that reflected in the result. The simplest explanation is also the most uncomfortable one: saved minutes, as long as nobody redesigns the process, scatter across the workday instead of landing on the balance sheet. For a Mittelstand firm with chat subscriptions, that means: the next sensible step isn't a better model. It should be picking one process and measuring it before and after. What that looks like is shown in the example next to this one.

Walmart: What a Provable AI Number Looks Like [4][5]

This isn't news from this week, and that's worth knowing. The CIO report is dated 2025-09-19, the Pactum figures from 2023. Even so, it's the best-documented large-scale deployment with concrete before-and-after numbers. I didn't find a comparably clean German or Brazilian example this week, with a named company and a measured result.

Walmart bundles its AI into four "super agents": the Associate Agent for employees (HR questions, for example), the Developer Agent for its own software development, plus Sparky and Marty. Per CIO: "More than 900,000 Walmart employees now use these tools to perform over 3 million queries daily." That query count, though, is exactly the kind of metric McKinsey's 88 percent would have. Things get interesting in the processes where something was actually measured:

  • Shift scheduling: store managers now need 30 minutes instead of 90.
  • Maintenance: costs cut by 19%, through predictive analytics.
  • Refrigeration: 30% fewer emergency alerts at cooling systems, using digital twins.
  • Distribution centers: capacity "nearly doubled" through AI and automation.
Walmart: store-manager shift-scheduling time, before and after (minutes, company figures per CIO, 09/2025)

The negotiation bot. Then there's the oldest and best-documented piece. Using software from Pactum, Walmart negotiates discounts and payment terms with suppliers no buyer would ever sit down with. Per PYMNTS (April 2023), the bot can "negotiate with 2,000 suppliers at once," it "closes deals with 68% of the suppliers with which it's used" and brings "an average savings of 3%." Nearly three out of four suppliers would rather negotiate with it than with a human. For background, Harvard Business Review names the size of the problem: Walmart can't negotiate individually with "all of its 100,000-plus suppliers."

Tail spend: That's the long tail of small suppliers where a human-led negotiation never pays off. So their contracts just keep rolling. Like the cell-phone plan you never cancel, because the call to the hotline takes longer than it saves. A bot makes that call a thousand times at once.
Context Everything here is a company claim, relayed by trade media, not independently verified. Pactum's 3% savings figure applies to the negotiations that closed, not to Walmart's overall procurement. And the data is 12 to 41 months old.

My take: Walmart's numbers aren't interesting for their size, but for their shape. Each one hangs on one process with one unit: minutes per shift schedule, cost per maintenance job, close rate per negotiation. That's exactly what the 63 percent without a measurable result are missing. What they're missing isn't better AI — it's the number from before. Anyone who doesn't measure how long shift scheduling takes before rolling something out can't attribute anything to it afterward.

AI Act: What Applies, What's Delayed [8][9][10]

Several compliance pages currently contradict each other, and the reason is simple: some are outdated. The current status is now law. The "Digital Omnibus" package, as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, was "published in the Official Journal, and entered into force on 27 July 2026." Before that, Parliament had approved it on 06/16, the Council on 06/29. What this means for companies:

  • Already applies: transparency obligations under Art. 50, since 2026-08-02. Anyone communicating with people via AI — through a chatbot, for instance — has to make that recognizable.
  • 2026-12-02: the grace period for labeling AI-generated content (Art. 50(2)) ends, for systems that were on the market before 08/02. The same day, the ban extends to "nudifier" applications.
  • 2027-12-02: obligations for stand-alone high-risk systems under Annex III (e.g., hiring, creditworthiness). Originally, 2026-08-02 applied.
  • 2028-08-02: AI as part of regulated products or safety components (Annex I).
Annex III vs. Annex I: Annex III lists AI that's risky on its own. A program that pre-sorts job applications decides about people no matter where it runs. Annex I covers AI that's embedded in an already-regulated product, say a machine or a medical device. There, it's tied to the product's own approval process, which has its own long timelines. That's why it gets another eight months on top.
Source conflict resolved The timeline at dataguard.com doesn't mention the Annex III delay at all. Latham & Watkins described it in May as an agreement that "still requires formal adoption." That condition has since been met. Anyone working from a guide that still lists 2026-08-02 for high-risk systems is working from the outdated version.

My take: For high-risk systems, the delay is a genuine breather. Still, anyone who repeats it as "the AI Act doesn't kick in until 2027" is making a mistake. The obligation that hits nearly every Mittelstand firm isn't delayed. It's been in force since August: the chatbot on the website has to say it's one. With Dots answering customer inquiries, that obligation doesn't shrink — it widens.

BaFin Takes Over AI Oversight in the Financial Sector [11]

Germany's implementing act for the AI Regulation "wurde am 28. Juli 2026 im Bundesgesetzblatt veröffentlicht und gilt seit dem 29. Juli 2026." Part of it is the KI-MIG (AI Market Supervision and Innovation Promotion Act). It governs "wann Bafin oder Bundesnetzagentur (BNetzA) die KI-Marktüberwachung übernehmen." BaFin is responsible for AI systems "im direkten Zusammenhang mit einer regulierten Finanztätigkeit stehen" that are deployed by companies under its supervision. For everyone else, the Bundesnetzagentur is the central authority.

Market surveillance: That's the authority allowed to check whether a product follows the rules, and pull it from the market if it doesn't — like food inspection, but for AI. For a bank, that's new: its credit AI now gets checked by the same regulator that already checks its balance sheet.

My take: For banks and insurers, this is already two months old. For anyone supplying them software, it's the actual news. Anyone building an AI component for a financial institution is now delivering into a BaFin-supervised environment. Documentation there is part of the delivery from now on, not an extra.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals, with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Proof of AI Impact: One Number Per Process, Before the Next Agent

ELI5 & value: Many Mittelstand firms have been paying for AI subscriptions for a year and are rolling out agents right now. Ask leadership what it delivered, and all you get is feelings. Worldwide, per the McKinsey secondary report, only 37% of companies can attribute any effect to the bottom line.[3] twiceD sells a fixed-term project that delivers exactly one answer: for three selected processes, it measures what they cost before and what they cost after. The shape of the number is Walmart's shift scheduling, 90 down to 30 minutes.[4] The value for the client: they know which subscription to cancel, which process to expand, and what to tell the advisory board.
Prototype (V0): (1) Select three processes with leadership, not "AI" as a whole. Quoting, complaint handling, and shift scheduling work well. (2) Fix one unit per process — minutes per transaction, error rate, or turnaround time — and pull a baseline measurement over two to four weeks from ticketing, ERP, or calendar data. (3) Where there's no baseline yet, say because an agent is already running, measure a comparison group without AI. (4) A one-page result per process: unit, before, after, sample size, euros per month. (5) Then a recommendation: expand, leave as is, or shut down. Deliberately out of scope: dashboards running continuously, tool selection, and implementation. Those come as a follow-on engagement.
Market & gap: The vendor with the strongest measurement solution already exists, and that's exactly the problem. Microsoft's Analytics Hub provides 24 open-source reporting templates to measure "how your people actually adopt Microsoft Copilot & agents." So what gets measured is the use of the vendor's own product, by the vendor itself. The big consultancies, McKinsey among them, sell transformation programs at enterprise scale. The governance platforms (see idea 2) check risk, not impact. What's missing is a vendor-neutral look at one process in euros, for a company with 50 to 500 people that's running Copilot, ChatGPT, and soon Dots all at once. Demand is there: 16.6% of Mittelstand firms are already deploying agents, per the KI-Index.[6]
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. It's pure consulting with a clean end point. There's no maintenance load, because nothing gets operated. One pass through three processes realistically takes three to four weeks at 15 to 20 hours a week. Most of the time goes into conversations and pulling data, not code. The analysis (cleaning data, comparison calculations, the report) speeds up a lot with AI. Scaling means a template and more clients, not more headcount. The honest downside: the result might be that a tool delivers nothing. That has to be a possible outcome contractually, or the measurement is worthless. And without baseline data, there's no claim to make. Clients who've already rolled out AI everywhere are harder. microsoft.github.io · enterprisedna.co · cio.com · silicon.de

2 · Article 50 Check and AI Inventory for Companies Without a Governance Budget

ELI5 & value: Since August 2026, it has to be recognizable when a customer is talking to an AI. As of 2026-12-02, the grace period for labeling AI-generated content also ends.[8] Most Mittelstand firms don't even know everywhere AI is running inside their business: in the website chat, in the HR vendor's applicant-screening tool, in the marketing agency's product copy, soon in a Dot. twiceD delivers an inventory with a traffic-light rating. It shows which AI is in the house, which obligation kicks in when, and what needs to change by December. On top comes the list of which systems could fall under Annex III in 2027, applicant pre-screening, for example. The value: a fine gets avoided, and above all, it becomes possible to show customers, the bank, and the insurer that the company has a handle on this.
Prototype (V0): (1) A fixed questionnaire plus a review of the website, contracts, and the tool list. For shadow AI, add half a day of interviews with department heads. (2) The AI inventory as a simple table: system, vendor, purpose, affected people, risk class per initial assessment. (3) A deadline map with the real dates: Art. 50 since 08/2026, 2026-12-02, 2027-12-02, 2028-08-02. (4) Concrete changes for Art. 50, such as disclosure text in the chat and labeling generated content, partly implemented right away. Deliberately out of scope: a legal opinion on specific cases, the conformity assessment for high-risk systems, and a governance tool.
Market & gap: The platform market is crowded. An industry comparison (as of 09/2026) rates 22 vendors, including Credo AI, Holistic AI, OneTrust, IBM watsonx.governance, and Trustible. For a business with 150 people, three AI tools, and a website chat, that's five figures a year for a problem that a table and a few text changes solve. On top of that: many guides still show the old deadlines.[10] So the gap is the one-time, current overview at a fixed price, without a platform subscription.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High, with one limit. Two to three weeks per client at roughly 10 to 15 hours a week. The template gets better with every client. Maintenance means an annual refresh as a small follow-on engagement, especially ahead of 2027-12-02. The limit is legal, not technical: twiceD can't sell a binding legal opinion on a specific case. So the offer has to be framed as a technical inventory. Assessing edge cases needs a partner law firm.
