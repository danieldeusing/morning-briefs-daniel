AI vs. AI: The Appeal Wave in the Job Centers [1][2]

According to the monthly statistics of Germany's Federal Employment Agency (BA), an estimated 54,482 new appeals came in during July 2026. That's 9,670, or 21.6%, more than in July 2025. As of the reporting date, 148,404 appeals were still pending. BA chief Andrea Nahles told Deutschlandfunk the number had risen sharply, and named "the greater use of artificial intelligence" as the reason. It's "people's legitimate right" to appeal, she said, but reviewing the long submissions costs staff significantly more time.

Appeal: Anyone who gets an official decision and thinks it's wrong can file a written appeal within a deadline. The agency then has to review the case again before anyone goes to court. Think of it like a complaint a store has to take seriously. A chatbot can write a ten-page complaint in minutes, citing statutes and court rulings. Some of it holds up, some of it is made up. The caseworker has to sort out which is which.

The Rheingau-Taunus district in Hesse has set up its own project for exactly this. Its project list includes "AI appeals" (May to September 2026): a tool meant to support caseworkers with AI-generated appeals and be "tested as a prototype." The goal is "more efficient processing as the volume of automatically generated submissions grows."[2] Whether the prototype actually works isn't stated in the document. It's from April.

Source note The BA figures come from a 2026-08-26 report by buergergeld.org that cites the BA's monthly statistics. I did not pull the BA statistics myself. Nobody measures what share of appeals were actually written with AI. The link rests on the BA chief's assessment, not on a count.

My take: this is the most honest AI story of the week, because it shows where the costs move. For citizens, filing an appeal has become almost free. For the agency, it's gotten more expensive, because longer texts cost more review time. The obvious response is AI on the other side. Then, in the end, one machine is checking another machine's text, with a human carrying the legal responsibility in between. Anyone building a tool like this has to build it as a reading aid, not as a decision-maker. More on that in the project idea below.

One District Rolls Out AI Through a Single Office [2][3]

The document from the Rheingau-Taunus district is a rare example of a German district disclosing its AI projects along with a timeline. It's based on an AI strategy that the district council adopted on 2026-02-10. Six projects are underway or in preparation (as of 2026-04-17):

  • Organization-wide language model (February to October 2026): starting in October, "regulated AI access for all staff." This is also meant to reduce the use of "unsecured external services."
  • Internal AI server (through May 2026): a test environment for sensitive cases.
  • Two voice bots (starting June 2026): one for the information desk, one for the job center. A three-month pilot is followed by a comparison.
  • AI appeals (see left).
  • Minutes app for youth welfare and youth support services, final solution by the end of 2026.
  • Agentic AI: use cases starting September 2026, implementation starting 2027.
Shadow AI: Employees paste text into a private chatbot because the agency doesn't offer its own. That's like plugging a flea-market USB stick into a work computer: convenient, but nobody knows where the data goes. "Regulated access" is the official tool with rules, so nobody has to reach for a private one anymore.

According to the document, a single office — AI and innovation management — runs all six projects. The goal is to handle growing workloads "not through job cuts, but through relief." For comparison: Baden-Württemberg published its administrative assistant F13 as open source on openCode on 2025-07-23. The federal government, states, and municipalities may reuse it; Saarland is testing it.[3]

Open question The document doesn't say whether the language model actually launches in October, which vendor supplies it, or what it costs. I couldn't find a more recent announcement from the district.

My take: the sequence is smart. First regulated access against shadow AI, then an internal server for sensitive data, and only then agents. Many SMBs do it the other way around: they buy the agent first. For twiceD, the list also works as a catalog. Each of the six projects is still pending in many other districts, and every district has to procure or reuse it on its own.

Accenture: $74 Billion in Revenue, No AI Number of Its Own [4][5]

Accenture reported its fourth quarter and full fiscal year 2026 results on 2026-10-01. Quarterly revenue rose to $18.7 billion, up 6% in dollars and 7% in local currency. New bookings came in at $22.2 billion (+4%). For the full year, the company reports $74.2 billion in revenue (+6%) and $84.5 billion in new bookings. It employs around 814,000 people. For fiscal year 2027, Accenture expects growth of 3 to 6% in local currency.

New bookings: That's the value of contracts a service provider newly signed during the quarter. Revenue is what it actually billed. So bookings are like a contractor's incoming orders, and revenue is the invoices that got paid. When bookings grow more slowly than revenue, things tend to quiet down in the following quarters.
Source note The figures come from the earnings exhibit to the 8-K filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) — the primary source. I didn't find a separate AI revenue or bookings figure in this document. AI booking numbers for Accenture circulate online. I couldn't match any of them to this quarter, so they're not included here.

In July, Microsoft set up a subsidiary for exactly this, Frontier Co.: $2.5 billion and 6,000 professionals who are meant to build AI systems "directly with customers" and work toward measurable business outcomes. Partners for the global rollout are Accenture, Capgemini, EY, KPMG, and PwC.[5]

My take: the biggest seller of AI implementation is growing in the single digits and expects even less next year. That doesn't fit the picture of an AI gold rush in consulting. But it does fit well with McKinsey (right): companies are paying for AI, yet only a minority see a result, and that's holding back the next budget. The fact that Microsoft itself is sending 6,000 people to customers shows where the bottleneck is. It's not the model — it's embedding AI into real workflows. That's good news for small implementers. The big players compete there for large corporations, not for a district with 6 projects.

7-Day Context: The Fall's Two Surveys [6][7]

Both studies have appeared in earlier issues. They remain the frame for everything above, so here's a brief status update. According to the Bitkom survey "KI in der Wirtschaft 2026" (published 2026-09-14), more than half of German companies with 20 or more employees now use AI productively for the first time: 57%, up from 36% (2025) and 20% (2024). Another 38% are planning or discussing adoption. Only 4% still consider AI irrelevant.

Bitkom: Share of companies (20+ employees) using AI productively, in % (survey, weeks 28–33/2026)

AI runs most often in customer contact (72%) and marketing (54%). In internal knowledge management, the share has doubled to 22% (2025: 11%).

Methodology Bitkom: 603 companies, surveyed by phone, representative according to the report. The figures come from a report by ms-aktuell.de, not directly from bitkom.org. An earlier Bitkom wave from April 2026 reported 41% with a different question. Please don't mix the two numbers. McKinsey: self-reported by managers; the report doesn't state the sample size. The study comes from a consultancy that sells exactly this kind of implementation.

McKinsey's "The State of AI in 2026," according to FM Magazine (2026-09-01), paints this picture: 80% of respondents say AI has improved their own productivity. Only 37% attribute any effect on operating results to AI. The share of "high performers" stands at around 6%, same as in 2025.

My take: the Bitkom number says AI has arrived. The McKinsey number says it mostly hasn't proven anything yet. That customer contact leads the pack is no coincidence: that's where response times are easiest to measure.

Regulation: Where the Deadlines Stand [8][9]

Nothing new since yesterday, but important for public-sector projects like the ones above. The "Digital Omnibus" amendment to the AI Act has been in force since 2026-07-27. It pushes the obligations for high-risk AI back to December 2027. The ban on non-consensual sexual AI content already takes effect in December 2026, and obligations for AI embedded in products start in August 2028. Since 2026-08-02, the European Commission may inspect providers of large general-purpose models and impose fines of up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide revenue.

High-risk AI: The AI Act sorts AI by potential harm. A spell-checker is harmless. A system that helps decide whether someone gets a social benefit can harm a person. The second group faces strict obligations like documentation, human oversight, and risk assessment, similar to a TÜV safety inspection for elevators.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals, with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Appeal Reading Aid for Job Centers and District Administrations

ELI5 & value: Citizens are having chatbots write their appeals. The texts are getting longer and contain real arguments alongside invented court rulings. A reading aid breaks an appeal like this down for the caseworker: Which points actually concern this decision? Which cited statutes and rulings actually exist? What's missing? The human still makes the decision. The value is review time per case. At the BA, around 148,000 appeals were pending in July, and the inflow grew 21.6% year over year.[1]
Prototype (V0): (1) Collect 50 real, redacted appeals as a test set with a district job center. (2) A pipeline builds three parts from them: the arguments as a list, the matching spot in the decision for each argument, and a check of every citation against freely available statute texts. (3) A traffic-light flag per citation shows: found, not found, not checkable. (4) A draft case note that the caseworker edits. MVP deliberately narrow: one area of law (SGB II, Germany's unemployment-benefit code), no decision recommendation, no automatic reply to citizens. Building blocks: F13 as an open-source base for government AI[3], an open model on an internal server, like the one the Rheingau-Taunus district already runs.[2]
Market & gap: BA chief Nahles and the Rheingau-Taunus district, which has been prototyping such a tool since May, confirm the need.[1][2] For the base, there's F13: a general-purpose assistant for summarization and research, free to reuse.[3] I didn't find a specialized provider for checking AI-generated appeals. That could be a gap, or a limit of this short bit of research — please verify before pitching this. The gap for twiceD: F13 is a toolbox, not a ready-made workflow for one area of law. A small provider can fit exactly this one workflow onto F13 or an internal server, without data ever leaving the building.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium. The V0 is doable solo in 4 to 6 weeks, since the model and the base are already in place. Two other things are hard. First, public-sector procurement takes a long time, so start with a pilot inside a district, not with a tender. Second, a tool like this sits close to a decision about social benefits. That could fall under the AI Act's high-risk rules, which apply from December 2027.[8] That needs legal review up front. As a pure reading aid with no recommendation, maintenance stays manageable, around 4 to 6 h/week per client, scaling via one contractor per new district. buergergeld.org · rheingau-taunus.de · baden-wuerttemberg.de
