The White House AI pact: four layers of oversight, zero penalties Policy

On Sep 29, 2026, President Trump signed a document titled "White House Accord on Super Intelligence: Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities" at a lunch in the White House. Signing alongside him were the heads of six companies: OpenAI, Google, Meta, Anthropic, Nvidia, and xAI.[3] At the table, among others, sat Jeff Bezos, Mark Zuckerberg, Satya Nadella, Sundar Pichai, Jensen Huang, Elon Musk, Dario Amodei, and Alex Karp.[1][2]

The companies pledge four layers of oversight for their most powerful models:[3]

Internal controls→Internal safety review team→Independent external auditors→Audit committee on the board
Self-regulation, "morally binding": The companies write their own rules and promise to follow them. No agency checks, no court punishes. It's like a class that writes its own code of conduct and grades itself. Trump calls the document "morally binding"; Speaker Mike Johnson calls it "voluntary."

Trump's position in one sentence, to Semafor: "there should be tremendous self-regulation."[1] TNW sums it up dryly: the meeting "produced no new rules". Reviews of the strongest models stay voluntary, based on an order from June.[2]

EU contrast The EU AI Act is law, with deadlines and fines. The next stage takes effect on Dec 2, 2026: AI that generates child-abuse images or non-consensual intimate images becomes banned. By Aug 2, 2027, every EU state must have at least one national AI sandbox. Obligations for high-risk systems follow starting Dec 2, 2027, and Aug 2, 2028.[4]

My take: the structure itself isn't dumb. Four layers with external auditors are exactly what the EU requires by law. The difference is who gets to say "no" when it matters. Here, in the end, that's still the company itself. For German companies, the pact changes nothing legally. But it points a direction: once even the US talks about "external auditors," AI review becomes a normal part of corporate controls, the way annual financial audits are today.

AI czar: Clayton proposed, not appointed Policy [5][6]

At the same event, Trump told Axios about intelligence coordinator Jay Clayton: "A good man. He's right here. That's a good idea." He said he'd name someone for the role within "three or four days." On CNBC, Clayton dodged twice when asked whether he'd accept. He only said: "Super intelligence is a national security issue." What authority the czar would have remains open. Trump also talks of a new agency called "AI Force."[6]

Watch the wording A report on Oct 4 described Clayton as already "appointed." The primary reports from Axios, TNW, and IBTimes don't say that. IBTimes writes on Sep 30 that the administration still hadn't explained "what authority the new AI czar would have".[5] Trump's own deadline ended around Oct 2–3. I haven't found an official appointment as of today.

My take: an intelligence chief as AI czar says a lot. In Washington, AI is being treated as a security issue, not a consumer or economic one. For Europe, that means export rules and data centers will likely get stricter, while product rules likely get looser.

Everyone uses AI, few profit from it Study

McKinsey has published "The State of AI in 2026: On the Road to ROI." The key number: 37% of organizations see any EBIT effect from AI at all. That's the same as last year. McKinsey itself writes that the share "has stalled at 37%". Only about 6% still qualify as "high performers," getting at least 5% of EBIT from AI. At the same time, 80% of respondents say AI makes them personally more productive.[7]

EBIT: Earnings before interest and taxes — what the actual business earns. A baker might feel faster with a new machine. But if there's no more money left at month's end, EBIT won't show it.
80% feel more productive→44% scale AI across the company*→37% see an EBIT effect→~6% get ≥5% EBIT from AI

The German figure comes from Bitkom (Sep 14, 2026): 57% of companies with 20+ employees use AI. A year ago it was 36%, two years ago 20%. But only 3% feel they're tapping the potential "fairly strongly." 59% tap it "not at all." 45% report a measurable contribution to business success. The biggest costs aren't licenses (21%) but infrastructure and cloud (51%), data preparation (50%), and integration (41%).[8]

Methodology McKinsey: 1,719 respondents across 97 countries, online survey from May 4 to Jun 8, 2026, weighted by countries' economic weight. This is self-reported, not balance-sheet analysis. McKinsey itself sells AI consulting. *We could only verify the 44% figure (up from 38% last year) on scaling and the 56% on "at least three functions" via three secondary sources; the PDF wouldn't load. Bitkom: 603 companies surveyed by phone, weeks 28–33 of 2026, representative of companies with 20+ employees. "Measurable contribution" is also self-reported. The two studies ask different things and aren't directly comparable.

A word on Gartner: what's confirmed is the forecast that by the end of 2026, 40% of enterprise apps will contain specialized AI agents.[9] A widely shared "80%" figure couldn't be traced to any Gartner source. So I'm not citing it.

My take: this is the most important number of the week, and it's boring. Usage is growing fast, and the profit effect is standing still. That fits the Bitkom cost breakdown: the money flows into data and integration, not into the model. Anyone who rolls out AI without measuring beforehand can't show afterward that it paid off. That's exactly where today's project idea comes from.

Google pays publishers for AI answers Consumer [10]

According to a report from Sep 30, 2026, Google is running an invitation-only pilot program that pays roughly 100 publishers for how heavily their content feeds AI answers in AI Overviews, AI Mode, and Gemini. The amounts vary widely: one early participant got more than $1 million a year. Smaller sites got about $1,000 over several months. For some, that's less than 0.1% of their ad revenue.

AI Overviews: The AI summary Google shows above regular search results. It answers the question directly, drawing on text from news sites. It's as if the librarian told you the content of the book. Handy for you, but then nobody checks the book out anymore.

My take: this is a price signal, not a business model. If small sites get less than a tenth of a percent of their ad revenue, that doesn't replace a click. What's interesting is the principle: Google apparently measures how much a given text contributes to an AI answer. For companies with their own specialized content (manuals, guides), "showing up in AI answers" will soon matter as much as search-engine optimization does today.

Brazil: a sandbox report and a bank that transfers money by voice Finance Public

ANPD sandbox. Brazil's data-protection authority, ANPD, is running its first AI sandbox with three companies from medical diagnostics, transport, and financial security. It centers on Art. 20 of the LGPD: the right to get an automated decision explained. Full results are due "até o final de outubro". A second round on protecting children and teens ("ECA Digital") is planned for late 2026, with implementation in 2027.[11]

Regulatory sandbox: A fenced-off playground where companies may test a new product under the regulator's supervision. Both sides learn which rule makes sense before it applies to everyone. The EU requires such sandboxes in every member state by August 2027.[4]

Itaú "ia.i". The assistant in the Itaú app speaks natural language and carries out transactions, including Pix transfers by voice command, with explicit confirmation from the customer. The rollout started with 300,000 users. The target was 1 million by September and 100% of customers by the end of 2026.[12] I haven't found whether the million mark was hit in September.

Pix: Brazil's instant-transfer system run by the central bank. Money reaches the recipient in seconds, around the clock. For almost all Brazilians, Pix is everyday life now, the way cash used to be.

For context: in a ranking of AI maturity among Latin American banks, Nubank, Itaú, Bradesco, and Banco do Brasil take the top four spots. At Nubank, more than 5% of the workforce works in AI roles, roughly three times the regional average.[13]

My take: a voice assistant that moves money is the stress test for AI agents. Here, a mistake costs real money. Itaú solves it with human confirmation, and that's exactly the right rule. The timing is interesting: ANPD is publishing its lessons on Art. 20 just as the country's largest private bank hands decisions to AI. I expect the duty to explain will be the topic of the next twelve months.

OpenAI fires three safety staff, two days after the pact Governance [14]

Per the WSJ, cited by SiliconANGLE on Oct 1, 2026, OpenAI fired three people from its safety team (the source doesn't break down their exact roles). The accusation: they allegedly passed confidential information to an outside organization that tests AI safety. No outlet names them. OpenAI says the three "mishandled sensitive information outside established company procedures".

My take: this is a case study for the pact. It promises "independent external auditors." Here, the internal safety team allegedly passed information outward, and the company treated it as a rule violation. Who decides what an external auditor gets to see? As long as the company alone decides that, the review isn't independent. I'll leave the model and developer-tooling angle to the ai-dev brief.

Project ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals, with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · AI impact check: measuring whether AI pays off

ELI5 & value: Many mid-size companies have rolled out Copilot, ChatGPT, or a chatbot. If the managing director asks after a year "What did it get us?", all anyone has are feelings. Bitkom: 57% use AI, but 59% tap its potential "not at all."[8] McKinsey: 80% feel more productive, only 37% see an effect on profit.[7] The impact check is like a scale before and after a diet. For two or three concrete workflows, it measures time, errors, and cost before and after AI. Value: the company learns which licenses to cut and where to invest more. And it gets a solid number for the bank, the advisory board, or a grant application.
Prototype (V0): (1) An inventory of all AI tools in use, with licenses, costs, and user counts from the Microsoft 365 admin center and invoices. (2) Pick two workflows with the client, for example writing quotes and answering customer emails. (3) A two-week baseline measurement: turnaround time, rework, and cost per case, from ticket or ERP timestamps, or by sampling otherwise. (4) The same measurement again after four to six weeks. (5) A ten-page report with one recommendation per tool: keep, expand, or cancel. The MVP stays deliberately narrow: two workflows, no custom software, no permanent dashboard. Building blocks: Microsoft Graph Reports for usage data, a spreadsheet or a simple notebook for analysis, an open-weights language model to classify samples.
Market & gap: platforms for measuring AI usage already exist. Larridin claims to measure usage, cost, competency, and impact across the whole company. Worklytics mainly shows who uses AI and how often, benchmarked against other companies. GetDX and Jellyfish measure AI inside development teams.[15] These are US platforms built for CIOs at large companies that need to connect many data sources. In Germany, the Mittelstand-Digital centers, funded by the federal economics ministry, offer free services. The Zentrum Handel's "AI readiness check" tests five areas with three questions each.[16] But it helps before rollout, not with measuring afterward. The gap: an impact measurement for companies with 20 to 250 employees that already use AI. Hands-on, fixed-price, in German, with data kept in-house. Exactly the group that, per Bitkom, is growing fast and, by its own account, getting little out of it.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. This is consulting with a method, not a platform. There's no operations, no GPU, and no maintenance after the project ends. Per client, I'd budget roughly 5 to 8 consulting days, spread over about eight weeks, half of it waiting between the two measurements. One person can build the template (questionnaire, measurement tables, report skeleton) in two to three weeks. Growth comes via a contractor who runs the measurements using the template. Risk: clients often want the improvement work afterward too. That's good, because it becomes the next, bigger project. Honest limit: where there are no timestamps, the measurement stays a sample.
