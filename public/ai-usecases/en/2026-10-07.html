Uber and Starbucks: when the AI bill arrives before the payoff ROI [1]

A report dated 2026-07-07 (updated 2026-08-31) rounds up two cases originally reported by Quartz and Restaurant Dive.[1]

  • Uber burned through its entire 2026 AI budget in 4 months. Verbatim: "Every dollar went to Anthropic's Claude Code, and the spend was not the result of misuse or runaway experimentation." Per the report, COO Andrew Macdonald couldn't yet tie the higher spend to better business results.
  • Starbucks shut down a camera-based AI inventory system less than 9 months after launch. Employees called it unreliable, with miscounted and misidentified products. The system was seen as a key part of CEO Brian Niccol's plan to keep products in stock.
Token budget: AI models bill by "tokens," small chunks of text. Every question and every answer costs tokens. A coding assistant that reads and writes code all day burns through a huge number of them. It's like a taxi meter: it keeps running as long as the work goes on, whether or not the result is any good.
Methodology Both cases are secondhand media reports. The report doesn't give a budget figure for Uber or an error rate for Starbucks. Uber's tool is a coding assistant. The tool itself is a topic for the ai-dev brief — here it's only about the budget question.

My take: the two cases show two different mistakes. At Uber, the AI works, but nobody had set in advance which number was supposed to improve. At Starbucks, the AI wasn't good enough for the task, and that only showed up in operation. Both are testable before rollout: set a metric and check it against real data. What's interesting is the contrast with the Bitkom study on the right: German companies barely see token spend as a cost problem. My guess is that's because most of them are still using AI at a small scale.

Shadow AI: only 30% of companies can see it Study [2]

The "2026 Enterprise AI Trends Study" by Smarsh, conducted by FTI Consulting and published 2026-07-10, measures the gap between adoption and control:[2]

  • 55% of companies actively use AI, 62% are investing in AI and machine learning.
  • Only 26% say their AI policies keep pace with adoption.
  • Only 30% can detect "shadow AI."
Shadow AI: Employees use AI tools the company doesn't know about or hasn't approved. For example, someone pastes a customer contract into a free chatbot to summarize it. The data then leaves the company without anyone noticing. It's like a personal USB stick at the office, only much easier to use.
Methodology Smarsh sells software for archiving and monitoring communications, so the study backs its own business. The report gives no sample size and no country breakdown. Reading: the direction is plausible, but don't apply these percentages to Germany.

My take: this number fits the Uber case. If you can't see your own AI use, you can't measure its cost or its benefit either. For a mid-sized company, the first step isn't a governance platform — it's a simple list: which AI tools are we using, with which data? This idea already ran here as a twiceD proposal on Oct 2, so I'm not repeating it today.

Germany: the costs sit elsewhere Study [3]

Per Bitkom, dated 2026-09-14, 57% of German companies with 20 or more employees use AI. A year ago it was 36%, two years ago 20%. Companies name infrastructure (51%), data preparation (50%), and integration with existing systems (41%) as the big costs. Only 8% cite token spend.[3]

Methodology Phone survey, 603 companies, weeks 28–33 of 2026, self-reported. A blog post from May cites a "Bitkom study" with 41% adoption and 17% the year before. Those numbers don't match the primary source, so I'm not using them.

My take: data preparation and integration together are the real work. That's good news for small service providers: this work is a craft, not model research.

The counterexample: vendor onboarding in hours, not days Logistics [5]

A report dated 2026-07-10 (updated 2026-09-01) describes a logistics company in Southeast Asia. It cut new-vendor onboarding from 5 days to under 4 hours. Data entry is 99.8% accurate. The Vietnamese provider SotaTek built it with a chain of four agents.[5]

Extraction: read documents→Validation: check data→Compliance: check rules→Execution: create master record
Vendor onboarding: Before a company can order from a new vendor, it has to set the vendor up in its system. That means collecting company data, tax ID, bank details, certificates, and proof documents. Someone in purchasing or accounting checks all of it and types it in. This often takes days because documents are missing or wrong.
Methodology A case from the provider itself; the customer isn't named. "5 days" isn't defined more precisely: it could be calendar time or working time. The 99.8% figure applies only to data entry.

My take: this is the opposite of Uber. A narrow task, a clear start and end, a number you can measure before and after. The figure isn't independently verified. But the workflow exists at every mid-sized company, and every buyer knows the wait.

EU AI Act: eight weeks left until Dec 2 Reg [7]

The Art. 50(2) obligation — AI-generated images, audio, and text need a machine-readable watermark — already applies to newly-placed systems since 2026-08-02; under the Digital Omnibus compromise, 2026-12-02 is only the deadline by which systems already running longer must retrofit it. The same day, the ban starts on AI systems that generate child sexual abuse images or non-consensual intimate images of real people. The high-risk obligations come later: 2027-12-02 for standalone systems like candidate screening or credit checks, 2028-08-02 for AI embedded in products like medical devices or machinery.[7]

My take: anyone publishing AI-generated images or text externally should check now with their tool vendor whether the watermark gets added automatically. Everyone else has until 2027.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals, with a market gap and an honest solo-feasibility read

1 · Vendor onboarding with bank verification for mid-sized companies

ELI5 & Value: A new vendor sends a form, a commercial-register extract, a tax ID, and its bank details, often as a PDF by email. Someone in purchasing types it into the ERP system, chases down missing documents, and signs off. An assistant reads the documents, fills in the master record, checks the VAT ID and matches the name against the IBAN, and hands the person a finished draft for approval. Value: vendors can be ordered from in hours instead of days, fewer typos in the master record, and protection against forged bank details. The logistics case above shows the direction: 5 days down to under 4 hours.[5] Since 2025-10-09, banks in the euro area check on every transfer whether the name and IBAN match.[13] So a wrong name in the master record will surface at the latest during the payment run. It's better to catch it already at onboarding.
Prototype (V0): (1) One customer, one vendor type, a fixed field schema for the master record, agreed with purchasing and accounting. (2) A language model reads PDFs and emails and fills in the fields as JSON. For sensitive data, an open model hosted on the customer's own premises. (3) Validation rules: VAT ID via the EU's VIES service, a check against the EU sanctions list, required documents complete, company name matching the account holder exactly. (4) A simple web page where a human reviews the draft, approves it, and requests missing documents via a pre-written email. (5) Export as an import file into the ERP system. Deliberately out of scope: a vendor portal, risk scoring, credit checks, a direct ERP write interface.
Market & Gap: The market for vendor-management software sits at roughly $14.7 billion for 2026 per a market report, growing 10.3% a year.[10] The big names are SAP Ariba, Coupa, HighRadius, HICX, and Zycus. Almost all of them list "Contact for pricing" instead of actual prices.[11] In Germany, tacto positions itself as "the AI for industrial procurement," focused on sourcing and terms, not vendor onboarding.[12] tacto itself cites 4 to 12 weeks as the typical duration for vendor onboarding.[12] My own read, not measured: the big suites assume the customer adopts their entire procurement platform. A mid-sized company with 50 new vendors a year isn't buying an Ariba project for that. It just wants the master record to be correct. The gap for twiceD: a narrow component sitting in front of the existing ERP, with German validation rules, and no cloud if the customer wants it that way. The new bank-verification rule provides a concrete trigger: company names in the master record now have to match the account holder.[13]
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. All the hard parts already exist: document-reading models, VIES, the sanctions list. twiceD builds the field schema, the validation rules, and the approval page. Solo, the V0 needs about three to four weeks. Most of the time goes into aligning the schema with purchasing and accounting. Honest note on upkeep: the validation rules change rarely, but vendors' document formats change often. I'd budget one to two hours a week per customer. That's manageable for one person with five to eight customers, after that a contractor. Honest limit: this doesn't pay off for 10 new vendors a year. The target group is companies with many new vendors or frequent changes to bank details. A bank change via email is a known path for invoice fraud, and the pilot has to catch that first. thebusinessresearchcompany.com · highradius.com · tacto.ai · agicap.com

It's Wednesday, October 7th, and it's another quiet day. Our research turned up no new, sourced AI deployment story with a real impact number, so today's episode looks at three cases from this past summer that line up well together.

First up is Uber. According to a July report, the ride-hailing giant burned through its entire 2026 AI budget in just four months — all of it on the coding assistant Claude Code. The report says that wasn't misuse or runaway spending. The catch: the company still can't tie that higher spending to better business results.

Then there's Starbucks. The coffee chain pulled the plug on a camera-based AI inventory system less than nine months after rolling it out. Employees said it miscounted and misidentified products.

Those stories line up with a new study from the vendor Smarsh. Only twenty-six percent of companies say their AI policies keep pace with how employees are actually using the technology. And only thirty percent can even detect so-called shadow AI — tools employees pick up on their own without permission. Smarsh sells software for exactly this problem, so take those numbers with a grain of salt.

In Germany, meanwhile, Bitkom says fifty-seven percent of companies now use AI. Their biggest costs are infrastructure, data prep, and integration — only eight percent point to the models' own token costs. And Gartner projects worldwide AI spending will hit roughly two-point-six trillion dollars this year.

The counterexample comes from logistics. A Southeast Asian company now onboards new vendors in under four hours instead of five days, using a chain of four AI agents. It's a vendor-reported case, but the workflow is narrow and measurable — exactly the shape of the AI projects that actually deliver.

On the regulatory front, the EU's next AI Act deadline lands in eight weeks, on December 2nd. That's when watermarking becomes mandatory for AI-generated content, and apps that create non-consensual AI nude images of real people become illegal.

Today's twiceD project idea takes that logistics case and brings it to German small and midsize businesses: an AI assistant that reads new vendors' paperwork, fills in the master record, checks tax IDs and bank details, and hands a human a ready-to-approve draft. The big procurement suites, like SAP Ariba or Coupa, are overkill for a step this small, and the feasibility for a small team is high.
