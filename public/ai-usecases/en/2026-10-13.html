SAP ECC: regular maintenance ends

SAP: AI for Everyone, but Not for ECC Without a Cloud Contract B2B SaaS [1][2]

At its in-house event SAP Connect in Las Vegas (Oct 5–7), SAP doubled down. Joule Studio, Joule Work and new assistants and agents are due to be generally available in October, as SAP-operated products via the customer portal "SAP for Me".[1] According to SAP, Joule Work already runs for 110,000 of its own employees. SAP cites 20% more productivity in finance, HR and procurement.[1] Analysts were cool. Thomas Randall (Info-Tech) told CIO: "SAP Connect is not giving us any breakthrough moments."[1]

AI agent in an ERP: An ERP is a company's central accounting and inventory software. An agent is an AI program that does not just answer inside it but carries out steps itself, for example matching a payment to an open invoice. Like an intern who does not just answer questions but also files the receipts.

The real story is at heise from Oct 12, 2026. Many German companies still run the old SAP ECC. Its regular maintenance ends at the end of 2027.[2] There is an AI connector for ECC, so far in an early-adopter program for finance and procurement. But access goes only to those who shift "at least 50 percent of their previous annual on-premises maintenance fees" into cloud ERP subscriptions.[2] The outgoing DSAG chair Jens Hungershausen calls the condition too narrow, especially for the Mittelstand.[2]

ECC vs. S/4HANA: ECC is the old SAP generation, S/4HANA the new one. The switch is a major project lasting months or years. Like moving into a new house: SAP is now essentially saying, "the new kitchen is only yours if you already pay half the rent in the new house".

What companies actually do is shown by the DSAG Investment Report 2026: 56% use S/4HANA on-prem, that is, in their own data center, and 42% plan medium or high investments there this year.[2] According to a DSAG survey from January, 43% have implemented AI use cases, many of them as tests or pilots and often with third-party products rather than SAP's own.[2] Concrete SAP examples per heise: TÜV Rheinland matches payments to open items, Carl Stahl compares tender texts against material data.[2]

Method The 20% is a vendor claim about its own people, with no method and no definition of "productivity". For the report and survey, DSAG gives no sample size at heise. heise also writes that SAP wants to have more than 400 agents by year-end. I could not confirm that and therefore leave it out of the assessment.

My view: This is the clearest case this year of AI serving as a lever for a contract migration. Technically, nothing stands in the way of attaching a language model to ECC data. SAP ties it to cloud revenue. For the Mittelstand that means: anyone who wants to move to S/4HANA Cloud by 2027 anyway loses little. Anyone who wants to stay on-prem, and per DSAG that is many, will look for AI from third parties. That is exactly where there is room for small service providers (see the project idea below).

Only 4.5% of Americans Pay for AI Consumer [3][4]

The investors at a16z have published the 7th edition of their "Top 100 Consumer AI Apps". New this time is an analysis of US credit card spending. Result: only 4.5% of US consumers pay for any AI subscription.[3] According to heise, that referred to a paid subscription to ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude in August. The share has doubled within a year.[4] The median paying user spends $25 a month, the top percent around $903.[3] Per heise, ChatGPT is clearly ahead, with Claude on par with Gemini among paying US customers.[4]

Median: The value in the middle when you line up all paying users by size. Half pay less than $25, half pay more. Unlike the average, the few heavy spenders at $903 do not skew the median.
Method Credit card data from a US panel. The article I read does not name the data provider. Subscriptions via corporate cards, app stores or family accounts may be missing. I have not read the a16z report itself; the numbers come from two secondary sources (Crypto Briefing, heise) that agree. a16z holds stakes in AI companies.

My view: Per heise, nearly every second American uses AI, but only one in 22 pays. That is the number to put next to every "AI adoption" headline. For B2B vendors it is a warning: usage does not mean willingness to pay. A business case should show what someone spends money on, not just that they click.

Bots vs. Scammers: Apate Wastes Scammers' Time Finance [5]

The Sydney company Apate (CEO: Dali Kaafar) puts AI bots on fraud calls and messages. The bots play unsuspecting victims, stall the scammers and collect data along the way. According to heise from Oct 12, 2026 (citing Wired), around 350,000 bots are running, some conversations last over 2 hours, and they have collected more than 250,000 pieces of information, including the scammers' bank accounts.[5] For scale: Truecaller estimates around 68 billion scam calls a year worldwide.[5] heise writes candidly: "Apate's bots can waste the scammers' time, but they cannot stop them entirely."[5]

Honeypot: A trap that looks real so attackers waste their time on it. Like a decoy wallet with a tracker: the thief thinks it is loot, and the police learn where he lives.
Method All figures are company claims. The bot count is contradictory: heise/Wired cite about 350,000, other reports (Cointelegraph) around 200,000. Banks and telcos such as Commonwealth Bank and TPG are said to be customers. I have that only from search results, not from primary sources.

My view: A rare case where AI voices are on the good side. The benefit is hard to measure, though: hours won from scammers are not damage prevented. More interesting for banks is the by-product, the perpetrators' account data. That is a tangible dataset for fraud defense.

AI Act: December 2, 2026 Is Back, With a Caveat Reg [6][7]

Yesterday I suspended the countdown to the labeling deadline because commentaries contradicted each other. Today a second commentary names the same date as the law firm Cuatrecasas. A Cloud Security Alliance research note from Sep 21, 2026 states: "The window closes December 2, 2026."[6] The rule behind it: generative AI systems that were already on the market before Aug 2, 2026 have until then to implement machine-readable labeling of their outputs under Art. 50(2). New systems must label immediately.[6][7] A commentary with a later date could not be found again today. The Art. 50(1) duty that a chatbot identifies itself as AI has applied since Aug 2, 2026, according to the commentaries.

Machine-readable labeling: An invisible label in the image, text or audio that software can read, such as a digital watermark or metadata. Like the security thread in a banknote: you hardly see it with the naked eye, but the checking device spots it instantly.
Caveat Both sources are commentaries, not statutory text. By its own account, the CSA note was produced with AI assistance. I still have not checked the text in the Official Journal (EUR-Lex). That is why the date appears in the timeline with a note and as a working assumption, not as a confirmed deadline.

My view: With two matching sources, December 2, 2026 is the sensible planning assumption, a good 50 days from today. Anyone who publishes AI images or texts externally should check now whether their tool sets metadata or watermarks. That costs little, whatever the Official Journal text ends up saying.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals, with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · ECC AI Bridge: an AI workflow for SAP ECC customers without a cloud contract

ELI5 & value: A mid-sized company running SAP ECC wants one clear AI use case, for example: automatically match incoming payments to open invoices. That is exactly what TÜV Rheinland does with SAP AI.[2] But SAP offers the ECC AI connection only in exchange for shifting at least 50% of maintenance fees into cloud subscriptions.[2] The ECC bridge delivers this one workflow without a contract change: a model running on the customer's premises reads the ECC data, proposes the match, and a human confirms. Analogy: instead of buying a new car just because you want a navigation system, you plug a good one into the old car. Value: clerk hours in the clearing backlog, without a million-euro migration.
Prototype (V0): (1) With one pilot customer, export one month of bank statements and open items as a test set. (2) Pull the data read-only through the existing SAP interfaces (RFC/BAPI or OData), write nothing. (3) Rule-based matching for the simple cases, a local open-weight model only for the hard ones (unclear payment reference, partial payment, collective payment). (4) A plain web interface with proposal, rationale and "Confirm". Posting is still done by the human in SAP. MVP deliberately narrow: one customer, one company code, read-only access, no agent that posts by itself. Building blocks: SAP standard interfaces, an open-weight model on a local inference server, an existing web template.
Market & gap: There are three routes. First, SAP itself: the ECC AI connector, but only against cloud shift and so far as an early-adopter program.[2] Second, third-party maintenance providers such as Rimini Street: it advertises "Rimini Agentic UX" and ready-made AI workflows for finance, compliance and maintenance. It promises ECC operation "well beyond 2040" and "up to 90% TCO savings" (vendor claim).[12] But that usually requires switching the entire SAP maintenance, a big decision. Third, third-party products: per DSAG, many of the 43% with AI use cases use such products.[2] Which ones exactly and what they cost, I did not check today. That is the first step before an offer. Gap for twiceD: a single, well-defined workflow for companies that want neither an SAP cloud contract nor third-party maintenance. On-prem, German-speaking, hands-on, at a fixed price.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium. V0 solo in 4 to 6 weeks, if the customer provides the SAP access. Maintenance stays limited as long as access is read-only. Estimated 2 to 4 hours per week per customer for operations and model updates. Scale via a contractor with SAP experience per new customer, not a department. Three honest catches: First, it needs SAP expertise in FI/CO that Daniel does not have, so an SAP partner from the start. Second, the licensing question: if a third-party system later posts into SAP itself, that can be chargeable under the SAP contract. Clarify this against the customer's contract before the offer, which is why V0 only reads. Third, the market shrinks with the end of ECC maintenance at the end of 2027. The window is real, but time-limited.
