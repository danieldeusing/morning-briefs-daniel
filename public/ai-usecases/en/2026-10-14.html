Character.ai Is Insolvent: What Chatbot Liability Costs Consumer [1][2]

According to heise on October 14, 2026, Character Technologies, the company behind Character.ai, has filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. Per the court filings, its debts are in the range of $50–100 million, spread across 100 to 199 creditors. Its assets fall in the same range.[1] Among the largest unsecured creditors, heise lists Google at about $4.2 million, outstanding legal fees at about $2.6 million, and DigitalOcean at just under $2 million.[1] heise writes: "Now they are looking for someone to buy the company and keep it running, otherwise Character.ai has to shut down."[1]

Chapter 11: A US bankruptcy procedure in which the company keeps operating while a court oversees debts and a sale. Like a restaurant that keeps cooking under supervision while a broker looks for a new owner. If no one turns up, it closes.

The first interested party is known. Disney is offering $20 million for selected claims the company holds against third parties, plus a non-exclusive license to certain IP rights. Higher bids are possible in the proceedings.[2] The background is what makes this explosive: Character.ai offers companion chatbots, AI characters for conversation and role-play. Five lawsuits from families in Colorado, Florida, New York, and Texas were settled earlier this year without admission of guilt, and the amounts are confidential.[1] Still open, per heise, are a lawsuit by the state of Kentucky, a proceeding by Pennsylvania's medical board (a bot allegedly posed as a licensed doctor), and a consolidated coordination in California. The US trade regulator FTC is investigating.[1]

Companion chatbot: A chatbot that does not complete tasks but simulates a relationship: friend, partner, favorite character. The business lives on users becoming emotionally attached. That is exactly what makes it risky for children and people in crisis.

One detail from the backstory: in 2024, according to heise, Google paid about $2.7 billion for a license to Character.ai's technology. Founders Noam Shazeer and Daniel de Freitas went back to Google DeepMind with about 30 experts.[1]

My take: This is the first major case in which liability for AI conversations pushes an AI company to the brink of closure. The sequence is instructive: the talent and the technology license went to Google in 2024, while the lawsuits stayed in the remaining shell. For any company that lets a chatbot talk to customers, the lesson is concrete. A bot that poses as a doctor or drifts into dangerous territory with minors is no longer a PR problem but a line item on the balance sheet. Anyone deploying one needs tests, logs, and clear limits before the first lawyer's letter arrives (see the project idea below).

Epic: "AI First" for the Disney Shooter, and the Launch Slips Consumer [3]

According to heise on October 13, 2026, citing Bloomberg, Epic Games is switching production of its Disney shooter, featuring Marvel and Star Wars characters, to AI. The directive: "No more hand-written code," all graphics through AI workflows, tools like Claude included.[3] The launch was planned for March 2027. It will not hold, and there is no new date. According to Bloomberg, work is progressing more slowly than expected, with staff and time underestimated.[3] Epic's communications chief: "We are betting on AI-accelerated development."[3]

For context: in March 2026 Epic cut more than 1,000 jobs, which CEO Tim Sweeney said was unrelated to AI. Disney invested $1.5 billion in Epic in 2024.[3] In a 2026 GDC survey with more than 2,300 participants, over a third use generative AI at work, and more than half consider it harmful to the industry.[3]

Method I read the Bloomberg report only via heise, not in the original. The GDC survey is an open industry poll, not a representative sample.

My take: This is a rare real-world data point, and it does not fit the glossy narrative. A studio with plenty of money and its own engine switches entirely to AI, and the project gets slower, not faster. That does not mean AI production fails. It means the changeover itself costs months. Anyone selling clients "AI first" should price in this learning curve. The ai-dev brief covers the tooling side (Claude Code, Cursor).

Wildberger Wants to Tax AI Revenue Policy [4]

Digital Minister Karsten Wildberger (CDU) floated a tax on AI revenue in Germany in an interview with Tagesspiegel, dpa and heise report on October 13, 2026. Quote: "We have to tax the extremely high revenue from this technology." And: "When growth and value creation happen here, taxes must also be paid here."[4] The money is meant to fund retraining and the restructuring of the labor market.[4]

What's missing No tax rate, no estimate of revenue, no timetable, no definition of "AI revenue." I have not read the original Tagesspiegel interview, and the heise text is partly behind a paywall. I have found no reactions from industry groups or the coalition yet.

My take: So far this is a signal, not a law. The hard question is the boundary: is a Mittelstand company's AI subscription "AI revenue"? Or an ERP with a built-in assistant? Without a clear definition, such a tax is more likely to hit German vendors booking revenue here than large platforms. For twiceD: watch, do not plan. It only gets concrete with a draft.

Microsoft Decision-1: A Model That Only Decides B2B SaaS [5]

According to heise (report of October 13, 2026), Microsoft introduced Decision-1. According to heise it is based on "Qwen3.5-9B" and returns no text, but one of three formats: yes/no, a choice, or a score.[5] Price: $0.042 per 1 million input tokens, output free, via Microsoft Foundry and OpenRouter. Microsoft names no customers.[5]

Decision model: Instead of an essay, you get back only a checkmark or a grade. Like an examiner who stamps applications only "approved" or "rejected." That is cheaper and easier to check; typical cases are ticket sorting or pre-screening of applications.

My take: All numbers are vendor claims, and even heise calls the comparison with GPT-6 Sol not very meaningful. The pattern is what is interesting: small, cheap specialist models for one task instead of one big all-rounder. The model details belong in the ai-dev brief.

AI Act: Labeling Deadline Unclear Again Reg [6]

Yesterday I named 2026-12-02 as a working assumption for the end of the grace period under Art. 50(2), meaning machine-readable labeling of AI outputs for systems already on the market. Today another differing commentary has turned up: OneTrust writes that the deadline for existing systems has been pushed to February 2027.[6] The post may be older than the final text. Less disputed, per the commentaries, are the major postponements through the Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744): high-risk obligations under Annex III from 2027-12-02, under Annex I from 2028-08-02.[6]

Caveat All commentary, no legal text. I could not reach EUR-Lex. The regulation number comes from the same family of sources.

My take: With two possible dates, plan around the earlier one. Anyone publishing AI images or text externally should check by early December whether their tool sets watermarks or metadata. If it ends up being February, you have gained two months of buffer, not lost them.

Studies: Lots of Pilots, Little Production Study [7][8]

Two new numbers from search fit the picture of recent days. PYMNTS Intelligence studied 437 department-level deployments at 60 large companies in September. Only 20% reached the "firmly embedded" stage. Those who deploy more deeply are more likely to see a financial return.[7] An HBR report sponsored by AWS puts 9% of large companies at fully rolled-out agentic AI. Gartner expects that more than 40% of agent projects could be canceled by 2027.[8]

Method Both sources come only from search results and were not loaded. PYMNTS gives no public methodology. The HBR report is sponsored by AWS, an AI infrastructure vendor. The Gartner figure is a forecast, not a measurement.

My take: The individual percentages are hardly comparable. The direction is the same in every study, though: rolling out is easy, anchoring it in operations is hard. The bottleneck is the last mile, not the model.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals, with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Chatbot Liability Check: Red-Teaming Customer Bots for the Mittelstand

ELI5 & Value: Many companies put a chatbot on their website: utilities, insurance brokers, pharmacies, online shops. Nobody systematically checks what the bot says when a customer pushes it. Does it give medical or legal advice? Does it pass itself off as a human or a professional? Does it talk to a child about dangerous things? Exactly such an allegation, a bot as a supposed doctor, is in one of the open proceedings against Character.ai.[1] The liability check attacks the bot with hundreds of German-language test questions, documents the failures, and delivers a report with measures. Analogy: a roadworthiness inspection for the chatbot before it is allowed on the road. Value: avoided liability and reputation risk, and proof for insurers, regulators, and management.
Prototype (V0): (1) Build a catalog of about 200 German-language attack questions in categories: fake professional, medical and legal advice, minors, self-harm, data leakage, "Are you a human?". (2) Run the catalog automatically against the client's bot with an open-source testing tool. (3) Review failures by hand and rate them by severity. (4) Deliver a report with examples and concrete fixes (system prompt, filters, handoff to a human), then a retest after the fixes. MVP deliberately narrow: one bot, one catalog, one report, no continuous monitoring, no legal advice. Building blocks: promptfoo, "a CLI and library for evaluating and red-teaming LLM-based apps," MIT license[11], or Giskard open source.[12]
Market & Gap: The tools exist. promptfoo is open source with about 26,000 GitHub stars and, per its own site, "now part of OpenAI."[11] Giskard from France offers an open-source version and a "Giskard Hub" for companies with "Continuous Red Teaming." On its homepage Giskard does not mention the AI Act, and the site names no prices.[12] Both are tools for developer teams: the customer must know what to test and interpret the results alone. I found no reliable market size for chatbot audits today, so I give none. The gap for twiceD: the Mittelstand company with a bought-in chatbot and no AI team. It needs not a tool but an appointment, a German-language test catalog for its industry, and an understandable report, at a fixed price.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. This is consulting work with almost zero operating load after delivery. The test catalog is one-time work and gets reused per industry. V0 with catalog in 2 to 3 weeks solo, then an estimated 3 to 5 person-days per audit. Scaling via a contractor who runs the tests while Daniel reviews the reports. Optionally a monthly retest later as a small subscription. Two honest catches: first, this is not legal advice, and liability questions need a partner law firm. Second, the customer must provide test access to the bot, and some chatbot platform providers allow automated testing only with consent. That belongs in the contract.
