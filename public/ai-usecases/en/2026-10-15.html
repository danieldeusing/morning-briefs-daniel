AI isn't eating jobs, but it is quietly screening out applications

A quiet Thursday. Nearly all the fresh reports date from Wednesday, and two of them from Germany belong together. The IAB finds no AI-driven job losses so far: in occupations highly exposed to AI, employment has actually grown faster since 2022. At the same time, an Indeed survey shows that AI often quietly co-decides in German HR departments, with free tools and without a human looking. Add an AI tool in US air traffic control that is now to be audited, and employees at Riachuelo building their own AI tools without programming skills. My take: today the risk lies less with the job than with the process nobody documents.

IAB: So far, AI is not a job killer in Germany Study [1]

The Institute for Employment Research (IAB) examined how employment has developed in occupations highly exposed to AI. heise reported on it on 2026-10-14. The result contradicts the fear narrative: among 30- to 39-year-olds, employment growth since 2022 in highly exposed occupations was "almost 6 percentage points higher" than in less exposed ones. Among 40- to 64-year-olds it was "around 5 percentage points more".[1]

Only the youngest show a slight decline. Among 20- to 29-year-olds, full-time employment in exposed occupations "fell by 1.2 percentage points more". According to the IAB, the figure is not statistically significant. The institute names the weak economy, not AI, as the main reason for the weak performance.[1] IAB researcher Melanie Arntz puts it this way: "Higher AI exposure does not necessarily mean weaker employment development."[1]

AI exposure: A measure of how many of an occupation's tasks an AI could theoretically take over or support. High exposure does not mean "will be replaced". A tax advisor is highly exposed because AI touches many of their tasks. But it can also let them serve more clients.

20–29 years (full-time)−1.2 percentage points (not significant)
30–39 yearsalmost +6 percentage points
40–64 yearsaround +5 percentage points
Method Read only via heise. The research did not find the original IAB report. Sample, data basis and the exact definition of "highly exposed" are therefore unknown. All values are thus "according to heise".

My view: This fits what we have seen in recent days: AI changes tasks before it cuts positions. The slight decline among the youngest is still the signal I would watch. Entry-level tasks are exactly the ones AI takes over first. If the figure becomes significant in one to two years, that is a real problem for training skilled workers. For Minister Wildberger's tax debate (yesterday's brief), this means: an acute job loss that would have to be paid for with an AI tax cannot yet be seen in this data.

Shadow AI in the HR department Deploy [2]

The day's second German report shows where AI already decides. The job platform Indeed surveyed 300 HR managers in Germany; heise and t3n reported on 2026-10-14.[2] The starting point: "43 percent said they receive more than 50 applications for every position." 56% receive more applications than twelve months ago.[2]

HR's answer is AI, but not always the official kind. 42% use approved software or AI for pre-screening. 28% have no company tool and, according to heise, fall back on free tools like ChatGPT; the survey gives no separate figure for free-tool use. 11% admit a human never sees applications rejected by AI. Only 39% say they can comply with GDPR and the AI Act in daily work.[2]

Shadow AI: AI tools that employees use on their own initiative, without approval from IT or data protection. Like a private USB stick with customer data: handy, but nobody knows where the data ends up. With job applications, that means résumés, i.e. personal data, ending up with a US service.
More applications→ no official tool→ free chatbot pre-sorts→ rejection without a human→ GDPR violation, later an AI Act problem
Method A vendor survey: Indeed itself sells recruiting tools. 300 respondents, selection procedure not stated. The research did not find the original, read via heise.

Legally this is no gray area. GDPR Art. 22 prohibits purely automated individual decisions with significant effects, and a rejection is such a decision.[11] The AI Act classifies AI for screening and filtering applications as high-risk. Obligations such as human oversight and documentation apply from 2027-12-02 under the Digital Omnibus.[2][7]

My view: This is the most important number of the day for twiceD. More than one in four HR staff have no company tool and fall back on free tools. The problem is not missing AI but missing permitted AI. Simply telling HR "ChatGPT is banned" pushes the behavior further into the shadows. The company needs an approved tool with a human in the loop and a log. More on that in the project idea below.

US air traffic control: AI tool SMART to be reviewed Public [3]

The US aviation authority FAA is deploying an AI tool called SMART (Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories). According to Nextgov on 2026-10-14, it is meant to merge "200 different data streams" into one system to give a better overview of air traffic. The contract went to Air Space Intelligence in June: $875 million over 12 years for FMDS and SMART combined.[3] According to Nextgov, SMART has been deployed in the Washington, D.C. region since 2026-09-21; other reports describe validation this fall.[3]

Now almost two dozen Democrats on the House transportation committee demand a review by the Government Accountability Office (GAO). Their letter dates from a Tuesday, so presumably 2026-10-13. They write that the use of AI has "rightfully raised serious questions about its reliability". They also worry that the tool interferes with the decisions of experienced air traffic controllers.[3] Transportation Secretary Duffy pushes back: SMART is a tool for managing traffic better. The controllers are "the ones who will always manage airspace".[3]

Traffic flow management: This is not the controller telling a plane "climb to 10,000 feet". It is planning one level above: how many aircraft may enter which airspace and when, so that nothing backs up? Like a traffic computer planning the traffic lights of an entire city, not the police officer at the intersection.
Source Only one source (Nextgov). The article gives no measured results, such as fewer delays since launch. Exactly such metrics are what the GAO request is meant to obtain.

My view: This is a textbook case for any AI deployment in critical infrastructure. The tool is "only" decision support, the human decides. But what counts is the question: how do we measure that it helps, and who notices if it harms? If an agency has no public metrics after three weeks of operation, outside review follows. Mid-sized companies bringing AI into control rooms or production planning should set the yardstick before launch, not after.

Riachuelo: Employees build their own AI tools Retail [4]

The Brazilian fashion retailer Riachuelo (around 33,000 employees) has staff without programming skills build AI tools, at first on their own initiative, before the structured Builders programme. Exame reports on 2026-10-14: two HR employees built a platform for performance reviews in 12 days.[4] According to tech chief Ney Santos, the company now has "mais de 1.500 agentes de inteligência artificial".[4]

The big numbers are projections, not results. The platform is meant to free up "3.600 horas de trabalho". For ten AI initiatives in 2026, the company cites a "retorno potencial de R$ 16 para cada R$ 1 investido".[4]

Method All company statements, not independently verified. "3,600 hours" and "16 to 1" are expected, not measured values. The text does not say how many of the 1,500 agents run in production.

My view: Riachuelo is the positive mirror image of the Indeed survey. There, employees quietly build their own solutions. Here, it also began on their own initiative, and now the Builders programme gives it a platform. That is the better way. But 1,500 agents are also 1,500 things someone has to maintain, review and eventually shut down. An HR performance review is also exactly the area the AI Act classifies as high-risk if a similar tool runs in the EU. In a year, I will not care about the number of agents but about how many of them are still running.

Berlin: Sovereign AI and health data Public [5][6]

SAP and Mistral: Politico had reported that the federal government was withdrawing from a project for sovereign AI in public administration with SAP and Mistral. The Digital Ministry (BMDS) disputes this, according to heise on 2026-10-14: that is "apparently a misunderstanding". The letter of intent was not legally binding, except for the duty to negotiate in earnest. There were never any procurement or funding commitments.[5] The government wants to align future projects with the European EDIC approach.[5]

Letter of intent (LoI): A "we'd like to do this together" on paper. No contract, no money. Like a handshake before buying a house: friendly, but not a notary appointment.

Health Research Data Center (FDZ Gesundheit): After one year, heise takes stock. Data from the electronic patient record of "75 million people with statutory insurance" is to flow into the research data center. According to heise, the process starts at the end of October, followed by a six-week objection period. The phased transfer begins in mid-December, and applications for data are possible from 2027.[6] As an example of AI on such data, heise cites Delphi-2M from DKFZ and EMBL, which is meant to predict more than 1,000 diseases. A lawsuit by the Society for Civil Rights (GFF) is pending.[6]

My view: With the SAP/Mistral project, the noise is bigger than the news. A letter of intent without money is not a withdrawal either when it expires. With the FDZ it is the other way around: a big, quiet step. From 2027, Germany gets a trove of data for health AI. Anyone building tools for it must take pseudonymization and secure processing environments seriously. The lawsuit shows this is not yet settled.

Caveat The source is a law firm, not EUR-Lex itself. The regulation number 2026/1744 comes only from a search result. The OneTrust post with "February 2027" that I cited yesterday apparently described the proposal, not the final text.

My view: Good that this is now clear. But careful: advisory sites are not up to date either. The Compound Law page on AI in recruiting still gives 2026-08-02 as the deadline, even though it was last changed on 2026-10-13.[11] If even the advisors disagree, how is HR supposed to know?

Studies: big numbers, old numbers Study [8][9]

On 2026-10-13, Exame cites the Stanford AI Index 2026 (published in April): generative AI reached 53% of the world's population in three years, and the benefit to US consumers is put at $172 billion a year.[8] The same article contains a Wharton survey of more than 800 executives: weekly use rose from 37% to 72%, but from 2023 to 2024.[8]

A Publicis Sapient survey (survey Apr./May 2026 (2026-04-29 to 2026-05-14), published June, 1,550 AI decision-makers in six markets) shows the gap: 73% use AI regularly, only 10% say AI is core to their operations.[9]

Method The Wharton values are two to three years old and not October news. The 53% and $172 billion were not checked against the Stanford source, and the article gives no method for the 53%. Publicis Sapient is a consultancy that sells AI projects. Source from a search result only.

My view: "Everyone uses it, hardly anyone builds operations on it" is the same message as yesterday. The Indeed survey shows what this gap looks like in everyday work: usage yes, but private, unregulated, without a log.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals, with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Permitted AI pre-screening: recruiting assistant with a human in the loop

ELI5 & value: In many mid-sized companies, HR staff quietly pre-sort applications with ChatGPT. According to the Indeed survey, 28% have no company tool and fall back on free tools, and 11% admit a human never sees rejected applicants.[2] That is already a GDPR risk today (Art. 22) and, from 2027-12-02, a high-risk case under the AI Act.[7][11] twiceD delivers the permitted alternative in two steps. First, a short check: where does HR use AI today, and which of it is a problem? Second, a small, approved assistant that summarizes applications and pre-sorts them by fixed criteria. Only a human signs off on rejections, and every step is logged. Analogy: instead of every employee driving their private car, the company provides a company car with a logbook. Value: less legal risk, less typing work, and proof for data protection and the works council.
Prototype (V0): (1) Half a day of stocktaking with HR: which tools, which data, who decides. The result is a one-page risk list. (2) An assistant that summarizes résumé and cover letter against the job's criteria, using an open model on its own server or an EU-hosted model. (3) An approval screen: the assistant suggests, the human clicks "invite" or "reject". (4) A log per decision, exportable for data protection and the works council. The MVP is deliberately narrow: one job type, one customer, no applicant database of its own and no integration with all applicant systems. Building blocks: an existing applicant system via export or e-mail inbox, an open model via a standard runtime, a simple web form.
Market & gap: Specialized auditors exist, but they target other customers. Holistic AI did a bias audit for the job platform Hired "to demonstrate compliance ahead of NYC Local Law 144", i.e. for a US law and for a software vendor.[12] Law firms like Compound Law explain the legal situation but deliver no tool, and their page still shows the old deadline.[11] Large applicant platforms build in AI pre-screening, but the mid-sized company is left alone with the proof. I found no reliable market size for "AI Act recruiting for mid-sized companies" today, so I give none. The demand side is well documented, though: only 39% of respondents can comply with GDPR and the AI Act in daily work.[2] Gap for twiceD: the company with 50 to 500 employees, no AI team, with a works council. It needs no audit portal, but a small, approved tool in German, set up in days.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium to high. The check is consulting with no operational load, estimated at 2 to 3 person-days per customer. The assistant is a hosted service and has real maintenance: model updates, logs, data protection requests. Estimated 2 to 4 hours per week per customer, as long as it stays with one job type. V0 in 3 to 4 weeks solo. Scaling via a contractor per new customer, with a standard template instead of one-off builds. Three honest catches: the works council has co-determination rights under § 87 BetrVG, which costs time before launch.[11] twiceD is not a law firm, so the legal classification needs a partner lawyer. And as soon as the assistant ranks on its own, twiceD may become the provider of a high-risk system, with its own obligations. That is why V0 deliberately stays with "summarize and suggest", and the human makes the decision.
