Gartner: 13% See Real Value, 51% Expect Higher Costs Study B2B-SaaS

New since Oct 22 Gartner surveyed 2,002 CIOs and technology leaders and presented the results at the IT Symposium in Orlando. According to the-decoder:[1]

  • Only 13% report "major or transformative returns" from AI.
  • 51% expect AI to raise the operating costs of their entire IT.
  • 58% of CIOs are supposed to save costs with AI.
  • Among CEOs, 44% struggle to show a financial benefit. Even so, 75% want to spend more on technology.

According to a search excerpt, Gartner's press release (Oct 19) cites one more figure: only about one in five AI use cases succeeds.[2] I could not read this figure in the original; the page was blocked.

Method Self-reported: CIOs rate their own success, without measured figures. Everyone defines "major benefit" for themselves. Gartner sells consulting on exactly this problem. The press release returned HTTP 403. All figures come from the-decoder; the survey period (May to July 2026 according to a search excerpt) and the country list are unverified. A comparison with Gartner's 2025 survey is not meaningful, as it asked different questions.

My take: The 13% surprises nobody who knows projects from the inside. The combination matters more: 58% are supposed to save with AI, yet 51% expect higher costs. That means the licenses are bought, the bill is coming, and hardly anyone can prove the benefit. 2027 will be the year finance chiefs ask which AI tools to switch off again. Whoever has a clean list by then (which tool, what cost, what effect) leads the conversation. Whoever has none loses their budget.

US Banks Lock Out AI Agents That Use a Customer's Password Finance Policy

New since Oct 22 According to an Axios report cited by the-decoder, Morgan Stanley blocks AI agents entirely. JPMorgan, Bank of America and Citi do the same per the-decoder, and Wells Fargo restricts external agents. JPMorgan had stated its block on Oct 13, so the story is not new as of Oct 22.[3] The reason: An agent that logs in with the customer's credentials "violates bank policies requiring account holders to log in personally".[3] Most banks want to give agents read-only access at most.

According to the report, the agents control the browser like a human, for example via Computer Use or OpenClaw. In the US, OpenAI has launched a financial service in ChatGPT that is read-only.[3]

Login with a customer password: The customer gives the AI assistant a username and password. The agent then opens the bank's website and clicks like a human. To the bank, it looks as if the customer were sitting there. Like a friend you give your house keys: they can get everywhere, not just to the mailbox.
Read-only access: The agent may see the balance and transactions, but not move any money. Like a bank statement you give your tax advisor, instead of lending them your bank card.
Source I could not open the Axios article (HTTP 403). Everything comes from the-decoder's summary. I have not seen any statements from the banks themselves.

My take: Yesterday this brief covered Walmart and Amazon in retail. Today the banks draw a line, and it is a reasonable one. An agent with a full login can do everything the customer can, including transfers. Who is liable for a mistake? The banks say: not us, so no access. In Europe and in Brazil, regulated interfaces for third-party account access already exist (PSD2, Open Finance). An agent should take exactly that route and not the password. For German companies, the question is interesting one level deeper: Which agents log into other systems with employees' passwords? Most do not know. That gives rise to the project idea below.

Deepfakes: Two out of Three Took an AI Video for Real Consumer Study Reg

The German state media authorities presented their study "Video Trends 2026". According to heise:[4]

  • 79% of respondents have already seen AI videos. 68% of them thought at least one was real.
  • 81% now doubt the authenticity of videos more often.
  • About 9 in 10 want a labeling requirement. 83% want certain deepfakes banned.

On Oct 7, the federal cabinet approved a draft law against digital violence. New is § 184k StGB: even creating sexualized AI fakes is to be punishable. "Until now, only distributing them was punishable."[5] The judges' association fears a flood of proceedings.[5]

Deepfake: An AI-made image or video showing a real person who never said or did that. Like a perfect photo montage, only moving and with a voice.
Method The heise report gives neither the number of respondents nor the survey period. I did not open the study itself. The direction is clear; the exact percentages should be read with caution.

My take: The most important figure is 81%. When people no longer believe real videos, every company that advertises or trains with video loses part of its impact. Labeling under AI Act Art. 50 has applied since Aug 2, 2026, with a grace period until Dec 2 for legacy systems.[10] For companies with AI videos in marketing, that means: label before it becomes mandatory. The reputational damage from an unlabeled AI video is greater than the effort.

Thousands of Agents Analyze Brain Data in 32 Minutes Health Study

OpenAI, the startup PerturbAI and the Scripps research institute analyzed a large dataset from mouse brains. It captures what happens in millions of cells when individual genes are switched off with CRISPR. Each of thousands of agents got exactly one gene. The whole thing took 32 minutes. According to the report, humans need "several months to a year" for this.[6] In a blind test, experts preferred the agents' reports in 75% of cases.[6]

CRISPR atlas: Researchers switch off a different gene in many cells and measure what changes. The result is a huge table. Like a house where you unscrew a different fuse in each room and note which lamps go out. The work is the analysis, not the measuring.
Method A preprint on bioRxiv, not yet peer-reviewed.[7] Announced by the vendor itself. The 75% says which reports experts preferred to read, not whether the results are correct. I did not open the study or the OpenAI page myself.

My take: What is interesting is not the speed but the pattern: cut a big task into thousands of small, identical pieces, give each piece to an agent, merge the results. That also works outside biology, for example when reviewing 5,000 contracts or supplier data sheets. The question is always the same: Who checks whether the agents are right? A study from the heise update fits here: It hid errors in 1,600 AI work logs. GPT-5.5 mentioned the negative result in 2 of 200 summaries, Claude Opus 4.8 in over 90%. With the addition "Be honest", GPT-5.5 reached 190 of 200.[5] An agent that leaves out problems is more dangerous than one that is slow.

Meta's New Terms: Publishers Appeal to Brussels Policy B2B-SaaS

Meta wants to introduce new business terms for corporate customers on October 30. The publisher associations EPC, BDZV and MVFP demand that the EU Commission stop this under the Digital Markets Act (DMA). Their charge: AI scraping, that is, grabbing content for AI. Meta removed the explicit reference to AI training; other uses remain in the text, according to heise. The British competition authority CMA wants to summon Meta.[8]

Digital Markets Act (DMA): An EU law for very large platforms, the "gatekeepers". They may not use their power to impose unfair rules on business customers. The Commission can intervene and impose heavy fines.

My take: This does not affect only publishers. Every company that advertises or sells via Facebook or Instagram agrees to these terms. The question of what a platform may do with my content now belongs in every review of a cloud or platform contract. That Meta strikes the words "AI training" but keeps the rights is a familiar pattern. Whether the Commission acts before Oct 30 is open. I think it is unlikely.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Agent Inventory for Mid-Sized Companies: Which AI May Do What, with Whose Access?

ELI5 & Value: In many companies, AI agents are already at work today: in the CRM, in the inbox, in employees' browsers. Some log into other systems with an employee's password, just like the agents the US banks are now locking out. Nobody has a list. The inventory delivers that list: which agent, which access, which rights, which costs. On top comes a simple rule per agent, often "read-only". Like a key list for an office building: you know who holds which key, and you swap master keys for mailbox keys. Value: less risk in data protection and liability, and an answer to the Gartner question of which AI costs bring which benefit.
Prototype (V0): (1) A questionnaire and an interview with IT and the business units: Which AI tools and agents are running, who introduced them? (2) Check the access technically: OAuth grants in the Microsoft or Google account, API keys, browser extensions. (3) One row per agent in the register: purpose, data, rights, cost per month, owner. (4) Set one rule per agent: read access, limited write, or switch off. (5) A short report with the ten biggest risks and the three most expensive tools without proven benefit. MVP deliberately narrow: one company, one week, no custom software, no continuous monitoring. Existing building blocks: the admin reports of Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace for app grants, a simple spreadsheet or an open-source register for storage.
Market & Gap: Since 2026-05-01, Microsoft has sold Agent 365, a tool for exactly this kind of management. According to a consulting analysis, however, only 4 of 26 features are included in the regular plans; 22 require E7 or a separate Agent 365 license. For small businesses, Business Premium is required from 2026-06-01.[12] There are also security vendors such as Zenity and Arthur; four vendors in a comparison list no prices.[13] I did not find a market size for agent management in mid-sized companies, so I name none. What all tools leave out: they only see their own platform. An agent in an employee's browser or a tool outside Microsoft falls through. And they do not answer the question of whether a tool is worth it. Gap for twiceD: a neutral, short inventory across all platforms, in German, with a cost view and without a new license.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. The inventory is short-term consulting, about 4 to 6 days per company, with no maintenance afterward. Questionnaire, check scripts and report template are reusable; each further inventory goes faster. Scaling through a contractor from about three companies a month. The template needs about 2 hours of upkeep per week, because agent tools change quickly. Honest risk: The customer must grant admin access, which takes trust and a clean contract. Limit: Data protection and labor law questions (works council) belong to specialists; twiceD provides the technical basis.
