Microsoft DxO: diagnostic AI beats doctors, but only on old cases Health Study

According to Gizmodo (Oct 22), Microsoft's "Diagnostic Orchestrator" (DxO) has now been published in the peer-reviewed journal Science. I found no Science or Microsoft page on it. The study first appeared in 2025 as a non-peer-reviewed preprint on arXiv. According to Gizmodo:[1]

  • On hard cases from the New England Journal of Medicine (NEJM), DxO made the correct diagnosis 83.2% of the time. General practitioners with reference works reached 36.1%, and without any aids about 22%.
  • On emergency-room cases the score was 80.3% to 46.8%.
  • It ran best with GPT-5, and got better still with GPT-5.5. Microsoft plans to release the SDBench test set openly.
Diagnostic orchestra: Instead of a single AI model, several AI roles work together. One forms hypotheses, one picks tests, one checks the cost, one objects. Like a team of doctors on rounds, except every role is software.
old NEJM case→ AI requests findings step by step→ diagnosis compared with known solution→ no real patient yet
Method Retrospective only: the AI solved past, already-solved cases, with no living patients. Gizmodo does not say how many doctors were compared. The cost savings are only simulated. Microsoft itself says the system needs a check in real operations before use. Against specialists, Microsoft expects the gap to shrink. NEJM cases are also deliberately rare puzzle cases, not everyday practice. An earlier 2025 preprint cited about 85% and a different test design. I do not mix those numbers. I know the date of the clinic test (Beth Israel Deaconess, "in the coming months") only from a summary of the Verge article, which I could not open.[2]

My take: This is serious research, not just press copy: peer-reviewed and published in Science, according to Gizmodo, with an open test set. Still, "more than twice as good as doctors" is the wrong headline. The comparison ran against general practitioners, not specialists, and the cases are exceptions. The interesting number only comes with the clinic test. In the EU, such a system falls under Annex I of the AI Act as a medical product, so duties apply only from August 2028. Before that it needs normal approval under the MDR. For Europe this means: it will be years before something like this runs in a German clinic.

MDR: The EU regulation for medical products. Software that suggests a diagnosis needs a review by a notified body, like an X-ray machine, before it may enter the clinic.

Apollo: "zero payoff" outside the tech sector Work Study

Torsten Slok, chief economist at the investment firm Apollo, wrote on Oct 24 that the technology has "delivered zero financial payoff to corporate America outside of tech".[3] He compares the seven big tech companies ("Mag 7") with the other 493 firms in the S&P 500 stock index. His data sources are Bloomberg, Macrobond, the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco productivity series and the Ramp AI Index. His conclusion: "the AI story will be decided in the S&P 493, not the Mag 7".[3]

S&P 493: The S&P 500 is an index of the 500 largest US firms. Subtract the seven tech giants (Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia and others) and 493 "normal" firms remain: banks, retailers, industry. If AI really pays off, it should show up in their profits.
Method A short opinion piece with charts. The text gives no concrete values, so I cite none. What "payoff" measures exactly (profit margin, productivity, revenue) is not clear from the text. Apollo is an investor with its own interests.

My take: "Zero" is sharpened for effect, but the direction fits the Gartner survey from Oct 22 (13% see great benefit, details in the Oct 23 brief). I read it this way: the costs of AI are already on the balance sheet, the payoff is not yet. That is normal for a new technology. Electricity took decades in factories until firms had rebuilt their halls. For consultants this means: clients' question shifts from "What can AI do?" to "What does AI bring in exactly our process, in euros?" Whoever answers that with a before-and-after number sells more easily.

New York: the list of more than 38 school programs losing their AI Public Policy

New York City schools must switch off the AI features in many previously allowed learning programs. Days before school started, in early September, Mayor Mamdani announced that the city "will fully discontinue or disable the AI components of more than 38 previously allowed programs". He named no products. On Oct 23, Chalkbeat published the list.[4] Affected programs include:

  • Amira, an AI reading tutor in more than 200 schools: banned.
  • A chatbot as a teaching assistant, built by the school authority with Microsoft and running in 6 schools: discontinued.
  • Gemini: banned. It is not on the list because, officials say, it was already being deactivated for students.
  • All In Learning: AI grading is switched off.
  • Magma Math: AI switched off on September 4. On October 5, the Education Department approved it to keep operating in city schools.
Status According to Chalkbeat, the city does not explain what criteria it used. Some vendors say they use no generative AI at all. So the list is more a sign of uncertainty than a vetted assessment.
AI component: Many learning programs are ordinary software with an AI feature on top, for example automatic grading or a chat window. The city does not ban the whole program, but requires that this one feature be off. Like a car where you disconnect the autopilot but may still drive yourself.

My take: The most important thing here is not the ban but the question behind it: the city apparently did not know exactly which of its purchased programs contain AI. Otherwise vendors would not object. German school authorities, municipalities and Mittelstand firms have exactly this problem too. AI no longer arrives as a product of its own, but as an update in software you have used for years. In the EU, AI in education counts as high-risk under Annex III, with duties from 2027-12-02. Whoever has no list by then has to switch off, if in doubt, like New York. This becomes the project idea below.

Related, but only weakly sourced: the New York Times reportedly said on Oct 23 that at the AI private school Alpha School about half of the students missed their math target. I know this only from a headline summary, I could not open the article. Alpha School disputes earlier criticism, its own data is not public.[5]

US banks and agents: one week later Finance Policy

Update since Oct 23 PYMNTS collected the statements of bank chiefs from the quarterly results (Oct 19, citing Bloomberg and FT):[6]

  • Schwab: "We're not going to allow them to move money". Each week the bank blocks unauthorized agents "in the tens and 20s". For research and reading, Schwab offers its own MCP servers.
  • JPMorgan: external agents see account balances through permitted interfaces such as Plaid, but may not send money. Jamie Dimon warns of "rogue agents" and "the liability transfer".
  • Bank of America: limits access to customer accounts until liability rules are in place. A human is to decide when money flows out.
  • Citi: external agents may not log in or execute anything. Open-banking providers get read access only.
MCP server: An official interface through which an AI agent may query data, instead of logging in like a human. Like a counter with an ID check instead of a borrowed house key. Technical details are in the ai-dev brief.
Source I know the BofA and Citi statements only as a summary, not as a verbatim quote. Whether Morgan Stanley blocks agents "entirely", as the-decoder writes, is not confirmed by other sources. That Oct 23 claim is therefore uncertain.

My take: The pattern is becoming clear: read yes, move money no, and through an official door instead of the password. The open question is liability. Dimon's phrase "liability transfer" hits it: if an agent transfers wrongly, who pays? Until that is settled, agent banking stays a read tool.

AI Act: what applies now and what comes only in 2027 Reg EU

Many firms are unsure about the deadlines because vendor blogs still cite old dates. The status after the "Digital Omnibus" (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since 2026-07-27):[7]

  • Applies since 2026-08-02: transparency. A chatbot must say it is an AI. Deepfakes must be labeled. "From 2 August 2026, organisations must continue to comply."[7]
  • 2026-12-02: grace period for machine-readable labeling ends for systems already on the market. New bans on AI-generated intimate images and abuse depictions start.
  • 2027-12-02: high-risk duties under Annex III, for example for AI in recruiting, credit checks and education.
  • 2028-08-02: high-risk duties under Annex I, for AI in products such as medical devices, machinery and toys.
Annex III and Annex I: Annex III lists AI that decides about people on its own, for example about a job application. Annex I covers AI built into a product that already needs a review today, for example a medical device. Products get more time because the review procedures already exist there.

My take: For the Mittelstand the postponement is no reason to wait. The transparency duty applies now, and many firms have a chatbot on their website that does not say it is an AI. That is cheap to fix and embarrassing if noticed. One example of outdated claims: a vendor blog from 2026-08-26 still names 2026-08-02 as the big deadline for "EU AI Act compliance".[8]

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · AI Feature Check for purchased software: school authorities, municipalities, Mittelstand

ELI5 & value: AI often no longer arrives as a new product but as an update in software you already use: the learning program, the CRM, the applicant software. That is why New York had to switch off AI in more than 38 programs, without clear criteria, and vendors objected.[4] The check produces a list: which purchased software has which AI feature, is it on, what data flows where, and does it fall under the AI Act? Like an allergen chart in a canteen: you need to know what is in each dish before someone asks. Value: the transparency duty has applied since 2026-08-02, education and recruiting become high-risk from 2027-12-02.[7] Whoever has the list can switch off or keep selectively, instead of banning across the board. Unlike the agent inventory from Oct 23, this is not about your own agents but about AI in other people's purchased software.
Prototype (V0): (1) Get the software list from purchasing and IT, pick the 20 to 40 most important programs. (2) For each program, search the docs, release notes and data processing agreement for AI features, using a language model as a reading aid. (3) Send a short questionnaire to each vendor: which AI, which model, where does the data run, can it be switched off yes or no. (4) Roughly classify each feature: transparency duty, possible high-risk case under Annex III, uncritical. (5) Deliver the result as a table plus two pages of recommendations: what to switch off, what to improve in the contract. MVP deliberately narrow: one customer, no own software, no automatic network scan, no legal advice (a partner law firm for that). Existing building blocks: a spreadsheet template, a language model for reading vendor texts, the deadlines from the Omnibus regulation.
Market & gap: Software for AI registers and compliance exists. CloudNuro advertises "Automated 360-degree discovery across 400+ integrations, including SaaS and cloud-native AI features".[8] A vendor comparison by Legiscope cites prices of "EUR 5,000-30,000/year (focused tools) to EUR 100,000+/year (enterprise GRC)".[13] Both are vendor claims, not independently verified. I found no market size, so I give none. What these tools leave out: they find what is technically connected, but they do not read contracts and do not ask vendors. They cost more than a school authority or a firm with 80 people wants to pay. And some blogs still cite outdated deadlines.[8] Gap for twiceD: a fixed flat fee, in German, with the current Omnibus deadlines, that school authorities and municipalities can also afford.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. This is short-term consulting: about 4 to 6 days per customer, depending on the number of programs. Afterwards no maintenance, only an annual re-check as a follow-up order. About 2 hours per week to maintain a vendor database that grows with every customer and makes the next check faster. Scaling through a contractor from about three customers a month. Honest risks: vendors answer slowly or not at all, and the line to legal advice must be clear in the contract. Public customers buy through procurement procedures, which takes time. A Mittelstand firm is the faster first customer. chalkbeat.org · mondaq.com · cloudnuro.ai · legiscope.com
