Bank of America: the password doesn't belong to the agent Finance US

Bank of America sent customers an email with the subject "Protecting your finances in the AI World". A spokesperson confirmed to TechCrunch that the email is genuine.[1] The key sentence: "If you do share your credentials, keep in mind that you're responsible for activity resulting from that access."[1] So anyone who gives an AI assistant their login so it can pay bills or check accounts bears the risk themselves.

According to TechCrunch, this is not an isolated case. Banks increasingly deal with AI agents that access customer accounts without the bank's permission. TechCrunch cites an Axios report from Oct. 22 here, which I could not read.[1]

AI agent with credentials: A program that logs in with your name and password and then clicks around by itself, like a human. To the bank, it looks as if you were sitting at the computer yourself. Like handing your front-door key to a stranger: the lock can't tell who is opening it.
Customer gives the agent the login→ Agent acts as the "customer"→ Bank can't tell human and agent apart→ Damage stays with the customer

My take: The bank is legally right, but that doesn't solve the problem. People want agents that pay for them. Today that only works through the shared login, and that makes every audit trail useless: the log always says "customer". The clean solution would be a separate, limited access for the agent, with its own name and its own limit. Banks don't offer that yet. Until then, the email is mostly liability protection. The same applies to companies: anyone who gives an agent the company account or the supplier login has the same problem, just with bigger amounts.

Veeam: the reverse gear for AI agents in Microsoft 365 Deploy B2B SaaS

Veeam is best known for backups. On Oct. 15, according to Veeam's press release, the company made "Intelligent ResOps" generally available (heise reported it on Oct. 27), as an add-on to Veeam Data Cloud for Microsoft 365.[2] The product does two things:

  • It detects AI agents, including Microsoft's Copilot, working in SharePoint, OneDrive, Teams and Exchange.
  • It rolls back their changes selectively, file by file. So you don't have to reset the whole mailbox or the whole storage to yesterday.
  • Prices according to heise: $2.49 (Foundation) and $4.99 (Advanced) per user per month, billed annually.[2]

The trigger sounds dramatic. In the preview phase, Veeam says it found 25 agents in a single Microsoft 365 account at one company, and 2 of them were actively changing data.[2] According to heise, Spain's data protection authority AEPD published the first report of a data breach involving a third-party AI agent on Sept. 14, 2026. The details come from the affected organization and are still being verified. I did not read the AEPD page itself.[2]

Per-file rollback (granular restore): A normal backup is like a photo of the whole desk. If an agent wrecks three documents, the photo resets everything, including today's good changes. A per-file rollback restores only the three wrecked pages.
Methodology The numbers 25 and 2 come from Veeam itself, from a single customer account. That shows this happens, not how often. The same goes for Veeam's own survey, according to which 88% of companies use or are testing AI agents, but only 7% count as "AI-ready".[2] Anyone selling a product against a risk likes to measure that risk as large.

My take: Despite the vendor numbers, this is the more important story of the day for mid-sized companies. Many firms switch on Copilot and first agents in Microsoft 365, but hardly any has a list of which agents exist and what they may do. That leads to the project idea below.

Apollo: "Zero payoff" outside the tech sector, and the money keeps flowing Study US

Torsten Slok, chief economist of the financial group Apollo, writes in a note titled "No signs of AI payoff outside the tech sector" that the technology has brought "zero financial payoff to corporate America outside of tech".[3] He means the "S&P 493", that is, the S&P 500 stock index without Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Tesla. According to TechCrunch, profit margins and revenue per employee show no movement there. His image: "AI may be a great calculator, but in business, intelligence is mostly judgment."[3]

Methodology The TechCrunch report gives no percentage figures, no time period and no data source. I did not read the original note. It is the assessment of a single economist, not a study. There is also a known measurement problem: new technology often shows up in company numbers only years later.

On the other side stands Anthropic's IPO filing (S-1) of Oct. 26. According to TechCrunch: "More than 11,900 customers spend at least $100,000 annually." Over 1,900 customers pay at least $1 million a year, and about 250 at least $10 million, each as of Sept. 30, 2026.[4] Part of the spending is driven by the coding tool Claude Code; more on that in the ai-dev brief.

Source I know the customer figures only from TechCrunch, not from the filing with the US securities regulator. Other figures from the summary, such as customer retention and first-half revenue, I am leaving out until they are checked against the original. These are company claims ahead of an IPO.

My take: Both can be true at once. Companies pay a lot for AI, but nothing shows up in their books yet. That is not a contradiction but a time lag, or a sign of poor implementation. For consultants, this means "AI saves costs" no longer convinces a skeptical CFO. You need a before-and-after measurement on one concrete process.

Uber: ChatGPT for all 30,000, measuring comes later Deploy Logistics

According to TechCrunch, Uber is giving "its entire global workforce — some 30,000 people" access to ChatGPT Work.[5] Before that, a pilot ran with 1,000 people. About 80% of early users were not developers. For the rollout, 30 AI engineers each worked with individual teams for two weeks. What this costs Uber, the company doesn't say. It plans to track usage.[5]

My take: The interesting number is the 80%. Most users sit in support, sales and administration, not in engineering. The "one AI engineer in the team for two weeks" model can be copied on a small scale. But "tracking usage" is not a success measurement. That is exactly the point Slok (left) criticizes: how many people open a tool says nothing about the gain.

AI Act: 35 days to go, and the Federal Network Agency is in charge Reg EU DE

The amendment to the AI Act (the "Omnibus") has been in the EU Official Journal since July 24, 2026.[6] The next deadlines according to the privacy organization FPF:

  • Wed Dec 2, 2026: labeling obligation for AI content under Art. 50(2). Covered here yesterday: new bans also start on the same day.
  • Thu Dec 2, 2027: obligations for high-risk AI under Annex III, for example in recruiting, credit and education.
  • Wed Aug 2, 2028: high-risk AI in products under Annex I.[6]

In Germany, the AI Market Surveillance Act (KI-MIG) regulates oversight. It appears in the Federal Law Gazette 2026 I No. 223 of July 28, 2026. The central authority is the Federal Network Agency (Bundesnetzagentur).[7]

Source The sources disagree on what exactly applies under Art. 50 on Dec 2. I therefore state only what FPF writes. I did not check the number and entry-into-force date of the Omnibus regulation against the EU Official Journal today. I know the KI-MIG from search excerpts, not from the Law Gazette itself.
Annex III (high-risk): A list of fields of use in which an AI decides about people, such as who gets a job or a loan. Strict rules apply there: documentation, human oversight, checks on the data. Like a mandatory vehicle inspection, but only for certain vehicles.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Agent inventory for Microsoft 365 in mid-sized companies: who is acting there, and are they allowed to?

ELI5 & value: In many companies, AI agents already work in email, folders and Teams, switched on by individual departments, with no central list. The idea is a fixed-price check: find all agents, record for each who is responsible, what it accesses and whether it may change data. Add clear rules and a tested way back when an agent breaks something. Like a stocktake in the warehouse, but for digital helpers with keys. Value: the company can meet an audit, the data protection officer or an incident like the AEPD report with a list instead of a shrug.[2]
Prototype (V0): (1) Start with one customer with 50 to 300 users, with read rights for the administrator. (2) Read out Microsoft's existing agent overview and the Entra and Purview logs rather than rebuilding them. (3) Build a table from that: agent, owner, access, write rights, last activity. A language model helps summarize the logs. (4) A rollback drill on a test folder, using the backup the customer already has. (5) A report with three to five rules, for example "no agent with write rights without a named owner". MVP deliberately narrow: Microsoft 365 only, no custom software, no continuous monitoring.
Market & gap: The tools already exist. Microsoft sells Agent 365 for $15 per user per month, billed annually, with the promise: "Get a complete view of all agents in your organization".[12] Veeam has offered finding and rolling back agent changes since Oct. 15, from $2.49 per user per month.[2] But both sell licenses, not decisions. Which agent may stay, who is liable and which rule applies, the customer has to work out alone. For a company of 150 people with no security department of its own, that is the real hurdle. I found no reliable market size for such audits, so I don't state one. Gap for twiceD: a short check in German, with an eye on GDPR and works council, that fits the tools the customer already pays for or will soon buy.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. The heavy technology comes from Microsoft and the backup vendor; twiceD delivers reading out, assessing and rules. About three to five days of work per customer, with no continuous operation and therefore almost no maintenance. An annual repeat check is a natural follow-up engagement. Scaling via a contractor with Microsoft 365 admin experience. Honest risk: the Microsoft interfaces change fast, so the checklist needs regular upkeep. And anyone without Microsoft 365 agents doesn't need the check. microsoft.com · heise.de
