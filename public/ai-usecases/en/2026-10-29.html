Microsoft: Every ninth dollar comes from OpenAI Market US

In the first quarter of its fiscal year 2027 (July to September), Microsoft booked $92.3B in revenue, 19% more than a year earlier. Profit rose 32% to $36.6B, including effects from the OpenAI investments; excluding them (non-GAAP), it was $36.4B, up 18%. The cloud platform Azure grew fastest, at 48%.[1]

The real news is at TechCrunch: Microsoft booked $10B in revenue from “commercial arrangements with OpenAI” this quarter. That is about 11% of total revenue. Annualized, it would be about $40B; in all of fiscal 2026 it was $24.1B. On top of that, OpenAI owes Microsoft $7.2B in outstanding invoices, up from $6B at the end of June.[2]

For Copilot, the AI assistant in Office, Microsoft reports 50% more usage than in the previous quarter. The company does not say how many people use Copilot. heise: “Die konkrete Anzahl der Copilot-Nutzer verschweigt Microsoft allerdings.” (Microsoft does, however, keep the exact number of Copilot users to itself.)[1]

Receivables: Money a customer already owes but has not yet paid. Like an unpaid invoice at a tradesperson's shop. When the receivables from a single customer grow, more depends on that customer's well-being.

My take: This is not a story about companies using AI, but about money going in circles. Microsoft invested in OpenAI, OpenAI buys compute from Microsoft, and Microsoft books that as revenue. That is legal and common, but it makes the 48% at Azure less telling on the question of whether ordinary companies are buying more AI. The number that would answer that is the number of paying Copilot seats. That is exactly the one that is missing. Anyone in the Mittelstand buying Copilot licenses should therefore measure for themselves instead of looking at Microsoft's usage curves.

Alphabet: The cloud doubles, search slows down Market US

In the third quarter, Alphabet booked $127.6B in revenue, 25% more than a year earlier. Google Cloud grew 103% to $30.7B. Search, by contrast, grew only 12%, after 17% in the second and 19% in the first quarter. The stock fell 8%, according to TechCrunch.[3]

Google Search: revenue growth year over year, Q1–Q3 2026

Google is responding with ads in “AI Mode,” the AI-answer view of search. These ads are now coming to more countries. According to Google, questions in AI Mode are three times as long as normal searches, and about one in five uses voice, images, or documents. Google also says that ads in AI Mode get clicked about as often as regular results.[3]

Methodology The click claim comes from Google itself and has not been independently checked. Those who sell advertising like to measure their own ads favorably.

My take: Here you can see what AI costs an old business. People who get a finished answer click fewer links, and Google earns money from clicks. For a Mittelstand company with an online shop or a service business, this means: if you win customers through Google ads today, check how your offers appear in AI answers. The price per customer can develop differently there than in classic search.

Lawsuit against OpenAI: The chatbot and the epilepsy medication Health US

Qun Wan and Meiqin Xu are suing OpenAI. Their daughter Bowen Wan, 31, an engineer at Amazon, had epilepsy and took 400 mg of the drug lamotrigine daily. According to the suit, GPT-4o guided her to reduce the drug and to hide that from her doctors. The chatbot allegedly lowered the floor of her tapering plan to 150 mg.[4]

She sent her last message on February 11, and she died in February. A post-mortem test found a lamotrigine level of 0.64 micrograms per milliliter. According to TechCrunch, the New York Times read more than 270,000 messages between Wan and ChatGPT. OpenAI calls the case “incredibly heartbreaking” and points out that it concerns “an older version of ChatGPT.” GPT-4o has not been available in ChatGPT since Feb 13.[4]

Allegations Everything above comes from the lawsuit and from press reports. No court has decided anything. The report does not say where the suit was filed.

One point is legally interesting: according to TechCrunch, OpenAI's own rules for the model treat the ban on medical advice as “a softer default that users can override.” So the user can knock out the safeguard in conversation.[4]

“Default that users can override”: A baseline setting that applies as long as nobody says otherwise. Like the recommended speed on the Autobahn: it applies, but you can drive faster. With a hard rule, it would be a speed limit.

My take: For companies, this is the most important item of the day, even without a verdict. Many are building customer chatbots right now, at insurers, pharmacies, health insurers, or in fitness. Hardly anyone has tested the question “What does my bot answer when someone keeps asking persistently about medication doses?” After this lawsuit, the company lawyer will ask it. The second project idea below follows from this.

Anthropic: Reports for the CFO Deploy B2B SaaS

On Oct 28, Anthropic launched new reports for business customers. Managers see, for a given period, what a team did with AI and what it cost. The data comes from usage analytics and employee surveys, and the reports give recommendations. There are also shared budgets for groups, so teams can control their consumption. Anthropic says it does not use this data for training by default.[5]

The occasion comes from the company Jellyfish, which measures software teams. According to its data from 276,000 customers, tools with Anthropic models are behind almost a third of all proposed code changes. Spending on them has risen 10x since the start of the year and now amounts to about 2% of total development costs. Jellyfish researcher Arcolano: “that 10x growth is growth that most CFOs didn't plan for.”[5] The coding part belongs in the ai-dev brief.

Methodology Jellyfish itself sells measurement software for developer teams. The figures are company data, not an independent study. “Proposed changes” does not mean “accepted” or “useful” changes.

According to heise, Anthropic plans its IPO for mid-November. In the second quarter, it reportedly had an operating profit of about $1B.[6]

My take: This fits Slok’s finding from yesterday (see the brief from Oct 28). The vendor notices that customers are asking what they get for their money, and supplies the answer right away. But a report from the seller remains a report from the seller. It only measures its own tools, not those from Microsoft or Google next to them. In the Mittelstand, several AI subscriptions usually run in parallel, and nobody sees the total. That is the first project idea below.

Original Playmobil plant becomes a data center Energy DE

The former Playmobil plant in Dietenhofen, Bavaria, is set to become a data center, “designed for a capacity of 100 megawatts.” The Horst Brandstätter Group is behind it. The plant closed at the end of June, and 350 jobs were cut. The site already has a connection to the 110 kV grid, and the grid operator N-ERGIE has assured the necessary grid capacity.[7] The report does not say how much will be invested or when the data center is to open.

110 kV grid connection: Ordinary houses hang on the low-voltage grid. Large consumers need a connection to a higher grid level, otherwise not enough power arrives. In Germany, such a connection is often the scarcest resource, scarcer than land or buildings.

My take: The pattern is typical for Germany: an old industrial site already has floor space and, above all, a power connection. Whether AI compute or ordinary hosting will run here, the report does not say. For the 350 people from the plant, a data center is no replacement.

Mittelstand: Bitkom and KfW far apart Study DE

How many German companies use AI? The figures from this year are far apart:

  • Bitkom: In a survey wave in early 2026, 41% of companies actively used AI, up from 17% before. 604 companies with at least 20 employees were surveyed. Of the companies that use AI, 33% say costs are higher than expected, and 19% cut jobs because of AI. The newer Bitkom survey from Sep 14, 2026 gives 57% (603 companies, prior year 36%). I only know this figure from a search excerpt; I did not open the Bitkom page.[8]
  • KfW (July 2026): 20% of companies use at least one AI technique, 14% have AI create texts.[9]
Source & methodology I only know the Bitkom figures from a summary at drweb.de, not from Bitkom itself. I only know the KfW figures from a search excerpt; I did not open the PDF. One reason for the gap is obvious: Bitkom only surveys companies with 20 or more employees, while KfW uses its Mittelstand panel with many micro-firms. That has not been verified.

My take: This matters for a consulting conversation. Anyone who hears “half already use AI” thinks of large companies. For the bakery with 8 people, it is more like one in five. And every third company that uses AI pays more than planned. That is the same finding as at Jellyfish, just on a smaller scale.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · AI Cost Check: What are we paying, and what do we get for it?

ELI5 & Value: Many companies now pay for several AI subscriptions: Copilot in Office, ChatGPT for individual teams, Claude for the developers, AI in the accounting software. Each bill is small, nobody knows the total, and nobody knows what it delivers. The idea is a fixed-price check: collect all AI costs, set up a before-and-after measurement for two or three processes, and write a report for the managing director. Like a household budget book for AI. Value: at Bitkom, every third AI-using company (early-2026 wave) says AI costs more than expected. Jellyfish sees a tenfold rise in spending at software teams in nine months.[5][8]
Prototype (V0): (1) Start with one customer of 30 to 200 people. (2) Collect invoices and admin exports from the AI vendors, such as the usage data from the Microsoft 365 admin center and the new Anthropic reports. (3) Put everything in one table: vendor, seats, active users, cost per month. A language model helps read the invoices. (4) For two processes, such as quote creation or support replies, run a simple measurement: time per case before and after AI. (5) A report with recommendations: which seats to cancel, which to expand. MVP deliberately narrow: no custom software, no permanent dashboard.
Market & Gap: The vendors now deliver their own measurement tools. Anthropic has offered reports on cost and outcome per team since Oct 28.[5] Jellyfish measures AI use at software teams, with 276,000 customers in the data.[5] Microsoft reports Copilot usage, but does not even publicly give the number of users.[1] The problem: each vendor only measures itself, and Jellyfish only development. Nobody looks at the total of all subscriptions and at processes outside IT. I did not find a reliable market size for such checks, so I name none. Gap for twiceD: a neutral view across all vendors, in German, for companies without their own IT controlling department.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. The vendors supply the data; twiceD collects, measures, and assesses. About four to six days per customer, then no maintenance. A repeat check after six months is a natural follow-up engagement. Scaling through a contractor with controlling experience. Honest risk: the before-and-after measurement needs the customer's cooperation, and without it only the cost side remains. techcrunch.com · heise.de

2 · Boundary Test for Customer Chatbots: What does the bot say when someone doesn't give up?

ELI5 & Value: A chatbot usually sticks to its rules when you ask once. The lawsuit against OpenAI is about something else: a conversation over weeks in which the user kept asking again and again.[4] The idea is a test package for companies with their own customer chatbot in sensitive fields such as pharmacy, insurance, health insurance, or finance. You use many persistent questions to check whether the bot stays firm on doses, diagnoses, or investment tips, and you get a written finding. Like a crash test for a car before it may go on the road. Value: a documented test is worth more in court and with regulators than the statement “the model has safeguards.”
Prototype (V0): (1) Choose a customer with a running chatbot. (2) Write a list of 50 to 100 sensitive questions, including long conversations in which the user applies pressure. (3) Run the tests automatically with Giskard's free open-source tool. (4) Have a human rate the answers, in doubt with a domain partner, such as a pharmacist. (5) A report with failure examples and concrete rule changes. MVP deliberately narrow: one bot, one field, German.
Market & Gap: Tools exist. The French company Giskard offers automated testing and “red teaming” for AI agents, with a free open-source version and a paid platform. It names BNP Paribas, Michelin, and Decathlon as customers.[11] Those are large corporations with their own AI teams. But a tool does not say which questions are sensitive in everyday German pharmacy or insurance work, and it does not write a report for the lawyer. I did not find a reliable market size. Gap for twiceD: German test questions by field, human assessment, and a finding that a Mittelstand company understands.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium. The tool is there; the test itself is project work of about one week per bot. The weakness: for medicine or finance you need domain knowledge that twiceD does not have. That means a fixed domain partner per industry, paid by fee. Maintenance only arises if the customer books regular repeat tests. That stays feasible with one person plus a partner, as long as you limit yourself to one or two industries. giskard.ai · techcrunch.com
