New York: The chatbot must have an exit Reg US

New York City's consumer agency (DCWP) published a draft of a new rule on Oct 20, 2026. It targets "doom loops," meaning customer service bots that send the customer in circles. Companies would have to disclose that an automated system is answering and offer "an easy offramp to speak to a human representative."[1]

The draft goes further: a company that gives customers only "unresponsive, irrelevant, or erroneous information" would count as deceptive. Violations would bring damages for customers and penalties "starting at $525." Citizens have 30 days to comment.[1] The agency names no concrete end date. A trade site arrives at about Nov 19, but I know that only from a search excerpt.[2]

Doom loop: You call the hotline, the bot does not understand you, sends you to the next menu, and in the end you are back at the start. You never reach a human. The rule would require an emergency exit, like the green door in a movie theater.

That companies are liable for their bots is not new. In 2024, Air Canada had to pay a customer CA$812.02 because its chatbot gave him wrong information. The airline had argued that the bot was responsible for itself. The tribunal called that "a remarkable submission."[3]

My take: A New York city rule does not apply in Germany. But it shows where things are heading: the customer service bot is no longer measured only by cost, but by whether the customer gets out. The EU AI Act already requires disclosure; it does not expressly require a way out to a human. Anyone selling a bot in Europe should build in both anyway. It costs little, and it takes the customer's lawyer's strongest argument away. The project idea below follows from this.

Amazon: Companies buy AI, and Amazon pays for the build-out Deploy US

The only fresh item of the day: on Oct 29, Amazon reported results for July to September. Revenue rose 12% to $201.9 billion. The cloud unit AWS grew 39% to $46.0 billion and earned $17.8 billion in operating income, up from $11.4 billion a year earlier.[4]

One sentence from the report matters for this brief: at Bedrock, the AI service of AWS, Fortune 500 companies spend "nearly 7x more year-over-year." According to Amazon, the volume of tokens processed grew by almost 17 times. For the agent service AgentCore, customers launched twice as many agents in the quarter as in the previous three quarters combined.[4]

Bedrock and tokens: Bedrock is a kiosk for AI models. A company rents models there from OpenAI, Anthropic and others, without its own servers. Billing is by tokens, which are word fragments. More tokens means: the AI reads and writes more text for the customer.

The flip side: Amazon expects about $220 billion in capital spending for 2026, mainly for data centers. As a result, free cash flow over the last twelve months has fallen to -$42.4 billion.[4]

Methodology "7x more spending" is Amazon's own figure with no base amount. On a small base, a factor of 7 sounds bigger than it is. Amazon does not say how many companies use Bedrock and for what.

My take: This is a real demand signal, stronger than any survey. Large companies pay money for AI capacity, and clearly more than a year ago. But Amazon spends more than comes in to serve that demand. The bet: customers stay once the data centers are built. The developer tools from the report (Strands, Kiro) are in the ai-dev brief, the market consequences in the economy brief.

Kyndryl: AI is in, the result is missing Study Global

The IT services firm Kyndryl surveyed 1,100 executives in eight countries. 57% say AI runs in their core processes, up from 35% in 2025. But only 11% have reached their two most important goals: "Only 11% have hit both." Only 25% fully trust AI when it works without human oversight.[5]

Methodology This is a vendor's survey, from a company that sells exactly the consulting the survey suggests as the solution. It does not say when the survey was run. I know the numbers only from a July 2026 summary at beri.net; I did not open Kyndryl's original report. "AI in core processes" is self-reported by respondents.

This fits an older prediction: Gartner expects that over 40% of AI agent projects will be canceled by the end of 2027.[6] Here too the base is thin: a poll of attendees of a Gartner webinar in January 2025, so a self-selected sample.

My take: Both numbers are weakly sourced, but they point in the same direction as the Amazon numbers above, just from the other side. Spending rises fast, results follow slowly. In between lies the business for small consultants: not introducing the AI, but making sure it delivers something measurable. That was yesterday's cost-check idea (see the Oct 29 brief).

Eight US states against OpenAI Consumer US

On Oct 14, eight states sued OpenAI: Arizona, Arkansas, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska and West Virginia. Each sues in its own court, over consumer protection and the protection of children. Florida had already filed its own suit on Jun 1. Iowa Attorney General Bird says OpenAI is "hurting kids and they are lying to Iowans." OpenAI points to "industry-leading age-specific protections."[7]

Allegations These are lawsuits, not a verdict. The Oct 14 date comes from search excerpts; the article itself is from Oct 15.

My take: Together with yesterday's epilepsy lawsuit, a pattern emerges: pressure on chatbots in the US is coming from all sides right now, from parents, states and cities. For a company with its own bot, that means: it is not enough that the model from the big vendor has "safety rules." The company that runs the bot is first in line for liability, as with Air Canada.

Microsoft MAI-DxO: Good grades, but no patient yet Health US

Microsoft showed new results for its diagnostic system MAI-DxO, on Oct 26 according to TechRepublic. It was tested on 304 published cases from the New England Journal of Medicine and on 325 emergency-room cases (MIMIC-IV-ED). Only whether the diagnosis was correct was measured, not whether patients did better. A hospital test with Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center is planned; whether it is running is unclear. Microsoft AI chief Suleyman: "It needs clinical validation."[8]

Retrospective vs. clinical: Retrospective means: you give the AI old cases whose solution you know, like a practice exam with the answer key. Clinical means: real patients, real consequences. Many systems shine in the practice exam and fail in everyday use.

Project ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Exit check for customer bots: Does the customer reach a human?

ELI5 & value: Many companies put bots on the hotline, in the web chat or in WhatsApp. Usually the only test is whether the bot answers questions. Hardly anyone checks whether a frustrated customer quickly reaches a human and whether the bot says it is a bot. The idea is a fixed-price check: a tester plays through 30 to 50 typical "I'm not getting anywhere" conversations, measures how many steps it takes to reach a human, and writes up a finding with concrete changes. Like a fire-safety inspection that checks that the emergency exits are clear. Value: fewer angry customers, fewer complaints, and a document in case a customer sues. Air Canada shows that the company is liable for its bot.[3]
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick a customer with a running bot, such as an energy utility, online retailer or insurance broker. (2) Write a list of conversations: cancellation, complaint, wrong invoice, customer types "human" three times. (3) Play through the text conversations semi-automatically, with a language model as the "difficult customer" and a free test tool such as Promptfoo or Giskard. (4) Measure per conversation: steps to a human, disclosure "I am a bot" yes or no, wrong answers. (5) Report with traffic-light rating and change proposals. MVP deliberately narrow: text chat only, no phone bots, one customer, German.
Market & gap: Test tools exist, but they target security, not customer experience. According to trade sites, Promptfoo received $18.4 million in funding from a16z in 2025, Lakera belongs to Check Point, and Giskard from Paris offers free testing and a paid platform.[13] I know these details only from comparison sites that are vendors themselves; who bought Promptfoo and when contradicts itself across sources. All three mainly look for attacks such as "prompt injection." Whether a customer gets out of the loop, they do not measure by default. I found no reliable market size, so I give none. The driver is regulation: New York wants to make the exit mandatory,[1] and the AI Act requires disclosure. Gap for twiceD: a small, German-language check with a clear result for companies without their own AI team, which often bought the bot ready-made from a vendor.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. The tools are free, a language model plays the difficult customer, and twiceD supplies the conversation list and the report. About three to five days per customer, no maintenance afterward. A repeat check after every major bot update is a natural follow-up job. Scaling through a contractor who plays through the conversations. Honest limit: this is not legal advice. For the question of which obligations apply in Germany exactly, the customer needs a lawyer, and the report should say so clearly. nyc.gov · letsdatascience.com · giskard.ai · appsecsanta.com
