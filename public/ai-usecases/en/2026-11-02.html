Correction: Bitkom 57%, and I was wrong yesterday Study DE

Correction to the brief of 2026-11-01 Yesterday's brief said the Bitkom prior-year value of 36% did not fit the 41% from March. That was wrong. They are two separate surveys at different times, and the values rise in the right order. The valid, current figure is 57%. The 41% is not a counter-value but an earlier reading.

According to the Bitkom release of 2026-09-14: "For the first time, at 57 percent, the majority of companies use AI." A year ago it was 36%, two years ago 20%. Another 38% plan or discuss its use. 603 companies with 20 or more employees were surveyed, in calendar weeks 28 to 33 of 2026.[1] The older Bitkom survey (release 2026-03-11, 604 companies, calendar weeks 2 to 6) came to 41%.[2] The 19% job cuts that also appeared yesterday belong to this March survey, not to the September survey.

2024: 20%→ 2025: 36%→ Jan./Feb. 2026: 41%→ Jul./Aug. 2026: 57%
Methodology Telephone surveys commissioned by Bitkom, an association of the digital industry. The 41% comes from a different survey with its own questions. The series above is therefore a guide, not a clean time series. "Uses AI" can also mean that an employee opens a chatbot now and then. The jump of 16 points in about six months is still large. Bitkom does not explain it.

New from the September release: Of the companies that use, plan or discuss AI, 11% already use AI agents, 29% plan to and 31% are discussing them.[1]

My take: The correction does not change the direction, it only makes it cleaner. More than half of companies with 20 or more people use AI, but only about one in nine of the interested companies has agents in use. Anyone building twiceD offers should plan with the 57, not the 41.

KPMG: Insurers feel ahead, but rebuild nothing Study Finance Global

KPMG surveyed executives at insurers with at least 500 employees in 20 countries, from May 20 to 29, 2026. According to Insurance Business (2026-09-29), 44% see themselves in the top quarter of the industry, and none sees itself clearly behind. 92% say AI helps with productivity and costs. But no insurer in the survey has fully rebuilt sales or underwriting around AI. For policy servicing or claims it was 3%.[3]

A KPMG column, published on 11/02 in BusinessMirror, gives further values: About half of the AI budget goes to the back office, only 5 to 10% to new products. 57% have built agents or decision aids for underwriting. But "just 29 percent say they are running front-to-back processes through AI agents or automation".[4]

Underwriting: Before an insurer accepts a contract, it checks how likely a claim is and sets the price. This is the heart of the business. Front-to-back: A process runs from first customer contact through to booking, without a person typing data from one system into the next.
Methodology KPMG does not state the number of respondents, at least not in the texts I read. I did not open the report itself (PDF). The headline figures come from Insurance Business, the budget and agent values from a column by KPMG itself. It is self-reporting by managers. KPMG sells consulting for exactly this rebuild. And one contradiction stands out: 57% have built agents for underwriting, but none has rebuilt underwriting. That probably means the agents help at the edges and the process stays old.

My take: This is the most honest number of the week. "We are ahead" and "we have changed nothing significant" fit together only if everyone does equally little. The money flows into the back office, so into saving costs, not new business. For twiceD this is not a bad sign. Back-office processes are small enough for a solo provider: a document intake, a review lane. The big rebuild is sold by the big consultancies.

Work: More companies expect fewer people Study DE Global

Two surveys show the same direction. According to Bitkom, 45% of German companies with 20 or more employees expect a slight decline in headcount because of AI over the next two years, 12% a significant one. 14% expect a slight increase.[1]

In McKinsey's "State of AI 2026", according to a search summary of the McKinsey page, 39% of respondents expect fewer employees because of AI next year, after 32% the year before. The same summary cites 1,719 respondents in 97 countries, surveyed from May 4 to June 8, 2026. According to it, about 37% see an impact on operating profit, as many as in 2025, and only about 6% count as "high performers".[5]

Methodology I did not open the McKinsey page itself today. All McKinsey values come from a search summary, so check them against the original before passing them on. The two surveys are not comparable: Bitkom asks companies in Germany with a two-year view, McKinsey asks managers worldwide with a one-year view. Both are expectations, not counted jobs.

My take: This is the core of the week. The profit effect stalls, but the expectation of less staff rises. That means: many companies plan the savings before they are measured. A mid-sized company that does not backfill jobs because "the AI does that" should measure one process first. Otherwise the work is missing, and the result does not come.

AI Act: 30 days left Reg EU

Nothing new, only the clock running. The amendment to the AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, appeared in the Official Journal on 2026-07-24 and has applied since 2026-07-27.[7] The deadlines:

  • Wed 2026-12-02: For systems that were already on the market before, the transition period ends for machine-readable labeling of AI content under Art. 50(2), "a four-month grace period (until 2 December 2026)".[8]
  • Thu 2027-12-02: High-risk AI under Annex III, such as hiring and credit decisions.[8]
  • Wed 2028-08-02: High-risk AI in products under Annex I.[8]

What the labeling can look like is described by the voluntary code of practice of the EU Commission, finally published on 2026-06-10. It has a part for providers (marking and detection, Art. 50(2)) and one for deployers (notices on deepfakes and certain texts, Art. 50(4)). It names metadata and watermarks and requires at least two layers "where necessary".[9]

Machine-readable labeling: An invisible stamp in the image, the sound or the file description that says: "An AI made this." A person does not see it, a checking program does. Like a watermark in a banknote that you only see under UV light.
Source Deadlines from law-firm texts (Gibson Dunn, National Law Review); I did not read the text in the Official Journal. The Gibson Dunn text dates from before formal adoption. Whether your own system counts as "already on the market before" is a legal question for the individual case.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Labeling check under Art. 50: to the stamp in 30 days

ELI5 & Value: Many mid-sized companies built their own AI features last year: a product-text generator in the shop, an image generator for catalogs, a chatbot on the website. Anyone who offers such a system themselves must label AI content in machine-readable form under Art. 50(2). For systems that were already on the market before, the transition period ends on 2026-12-02.[8] The idea: twiceD checks in a fixed package which systems are affected, and builds in the labeling. Value: a clear answer before the deadline and a built-in stamp instead of an open legal question.
Prototype (V0): (1) A checklist from Art. 50 and the EU code: Which systems generate text, image, sound or video, and who is the provider? (2) For an image system at the customer, build in metadata under the open standard C2PA. (3) As a second layer, a watermark, because the code requires two layers "where necessary".[9] (4) A short test log: what is marked, with what, how to test it. MVP deliberately narrow: one system, image or text, no legal advice. The classification as provider or deployer is confirmed by the customer's lawyer.
Market & Gap: The building blocks exist. C2PA is, by its own site, an "open technical standard", and its steering committee includes Adobe, Google, Microsoft, OpenAI and Truepic, among others.[10] With SynthID, Google offers a watermarking tool "designed specifically for AI-generated content", but it mainly marks Google's own content.[11] So the big model providers label what comes from their own interfaces. I found no reliable market size, so I name none. Gap for twiceD: The mid-sized company that built a model into its own shop via API does not get the stamp automatically. Law firms explain the law but build nothing in. In between, someone is missing who connects both, in German and with a fixed deadline.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High for the check, medium for the build-in. The checklist can be built solo in a week and is the same for every customer. The build-in per system takes a few days if the code is accessible. After the build-in, maintenance is low, about one to two hours per month and customer for tests after updates. Honest limits: The window until 12/02 is short, and after the deadline it becomes a normal maintenance offer. twiceD must not provide legal advice, so always work with the customer's lawyer. Scale via one contractor per customer. c2pa.org · deepmind.google · jonesday.com
