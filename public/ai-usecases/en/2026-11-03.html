Banks Find Outside Agents in Customer Accounts Deploy Finance US

On Oct 25, this brief covered the pattern at US banks: agents may read, but not move money. Now it turns out the agents are already inside. According to CBS (Oct 23), Axios reports that large US banks are detecting unauthorized activity by outside AI agents in customer accounts. Axios journalist Madison Mills calls it a problem for "dozens of financial institutions".[1] The path is simple: the customer gives the agent a username and password, and the agent logs in like a human.

Customer shares password→ Agent logs in as the customer→ Bank sees "outside" access→ Who is liable for a wrong transaction?

Reactions differ. Jamie Dimon said on Oct 13: "We're not going to allow just any outside agents to come in inside the company". Schwab blocks such agents. Morgan Stanley says it has seen no unauthorized agent activity. Chase says: "Customers should not give their username and passwords to anyone." David Sacks, the White House AI czar, pushes back on the alarm: it may violate bank terms, "but these are in no way cyberattacks". He proposes read-only access.[1]

The most interesting answer comes from Wells Fargo. According to SecureWorld (Oct 26), new online terms take effect on Nov 24. From then on, any transaction by the customer's authorized representatives, including their AI agents, counts as authorized by the customer, "including those you did not want or intend". Per Wells Fargo's own notice (wellsfargo.com/online-banking/updates, effective 24 Nov), requests made through AI tools may be treated as if they came from the customer; the quote may come from the existing authorized-representatives clause. The customer alone must supervise the agents.[2]

Source I know the Wells Fargo clause only from SecureWorld; I have not read the original agreement. One search summary attributed the same clause to JPMorgan. The article itself names Wells Fargo. Please check the bank's agreement before passing it on. I did not open the Axios article, only the CBS report about it.

Plaid shows the opposite direction. Since Sep 28, Decagon's service agents can "connect, verify, and act on" a bank account the customer has shared via Plaid. That also means completing a payment in conversation. The customer can cut the connection at any time.[3]

Delegate: Someone you let act for you, like a power of attorney for the neighbor who picks up a package. The Wells Fargo clause says: an AI agent is such a neighbor. If she picks up the wrong package, that is your problem, not the post office's.

My take: On Oct 25 the open question was liability. Wells Fargo answers it, in the bank's favor. That is convenient, but honestly also logical: whoever shares their password gives up control. The better solution remains the official door: a dedicated access with clear rights that you can block individually. Banks and Plaid are building this door now. For every other operator of a customer login, this is a preview. The agents will come to them too, and they should build the door first.

Hospital AI Writes More Expensive Bills, Insurers Say Study Health US

This story is five weeks old but has not appeared here yet. According to an analysis by the insurer association Blue Cross Blue Shield Association (BCBSA), hospitals' AI billing tools raised the costs of Blue Cross plans by an estimated $942 million against 2023 levels, across 2024 and 2025 combined (BCBSA counts only cases with no change in care; analysis dated 24 Sep). Around $653 million of that comes from secondary diagnoses that push a case into a more expensive group. Per case, hospitals received almost $12,000 more on average. The share of inpatient cases billed as "medically complex" rose from 37% in early 2023 to 40% in late 2025. More than 60% of hospital systems use such tools.[4]

Medical Coding: Every diagnosis and treatment gets a code. The codes determine how much the insurer pays. An extra secondary diagnosis, such as malnutrition, can lift a case into a more expensive price group. The AI reads doctors' notes and lab values and suggests codes that a human might have overlooked.
Methodology The figure comes from the insurers, meaning the side that pays. It has not been independently verified. I know it only from an AI Weekly summary (Sep 27) that cites the New York Times. Fierce Healthcare blocked retrieval, and I have not read the BCBSA release itself. The per-case amount varies between $11,000 and $12,000 depending on the outlet. The hospital association AHA said in August that more coding can also mean patients are getting older and sicker and are better documented.[4] Both can be true. How much of the $942 million is "real" illness and how much is upcoding cannot be separated from these numbers.

My take: This is the rare case where AI has a measurable effect, and it is exactly the one the customer wanted: more revenue for the hospital. One side's gain is the other side's bill. The insurers' answer will be AI on their side that checks every bill. In the end both sides arm up. That is an arms race, not progress. Germany also bills hospital cases through flat-rate case payments, that is, through codes. Anyone introducing coding AI here should first clarify how to justify the suggestions to the payers.

Ro Hands Medication Advice to "Robin" Deploy Health Consumer US

The online clinic Ro launched "Robin" on Thursday, Oct 29, an AI agent for its customers. According to Business Insider (quoted by Let's Data Science), Robin handles routine tasks: side effects, nutrition plans, medication changes. It uses doctor input, patient feedback, prescriptions and phone data, and suggests medications, dosages and habits. The report gives no figures on customers, price, model or accuracy, nor on how doctors review the suggestions. Ateev Mehrotra, a health researcher at Brown University: "I would love some evidence before millions of Americans start doing this".[5]

My take: Everything I want to see is missing here: a study, an error rate, a clear human who signs off. An agent that suggests dosages is no longer a chatbot but a medical product. In the EU it would very likely fall under the Medical Device Regulation. Until Ro shows data, this is a product launch, not proof.

AI Act: 29 Days to Go Reg EU DE

Nothing new, the clock is running. The amendment to the AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, appeared in the Official Journal on 2026-07-24 and has applied since 2026-07-27.[6] The deadlines:

  • Wed 2026-12-02: Ban on AI that generates non-consensual nude images or abuse depictions of children. "These prohibitions apply from 2 December 2026."[6]
  • Expected Wed 2026-12-02: For generative systems already on the market before 2026-08-02, a "limited four-month grace period" applies to machine-readable labeling under Art. 50.[6] A law firm text gives "until 2 December 2026" for this.[7]
  • Thu 2027-12-02: High-risk AI under Annex III, such as hiring and credit decisions.[6]
  • Wed 2028-08-02: High-risk AI in products under Annex I.[6]
Source The end of the labeling grace period is not certain. The Gibson Dunn text predates formal adoption. Other search hits give other dates because they cite different drafts. I could not retrieve the text in the Official Journal today. The remaining Art. 50 obligations have applied since August and were not postponed.[6]

In Germany, the implementing act (KI-MIG) has been in force since 2026-07-29. "The Bundesnetzagentur thereby becomes the market surveillance authority, contact point and complaints body." An AI regulatory sandbox for small and mid-sized companies has been announced.[8] I found no start date for it.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Agent Door: Official Read Access for Customer Portals

ELI5 & Value: Many mid-sized companies have a customer portal: dealers order there, check delivery status, download invoices. What the banks are experiencing now will arrive there too. Customers give their AI agent the portal password, and the agent clicks through. That is insecure, breaks with every change to the interface, and nobody knows who did what. The idea: twiceD builds an official door, a dedicated access for agents with fixed rights, read-only at first. Value: less password sharing, a clear log of who queried what, and customers whose agents work reliably. As with the bank: better a counter with ID check than a borrowed house key.
Prototype (V0): (1) Analyze the portal's access logs: how many logins look automated? That is also the sales proof. (2) Build an MCP server with three read functions: order status, open invoices, stock levels. (3) Sign-in via OAuth with separate rights per agent, as the MCP specification provides.[9] (4) A page in the portal where the customer sees their agents and blocks them individually, as with Plaid.[3] MVP deliberately narrow: read-only, one portal, no orders by agents. Existing building blocks: the official MCP SDK, an existing OAuth server (for example Keycloak), the data interface the portal already has.
Market & Gap: For banks, Plaid builds this: agents get access to a shared account through Plaid, and the customer can cut it.[3] But that applies only to bank data. Cloudflare offers rules that let operators allow or block AI crawlers and agents, "to define their block vs. allow expectations".[10] That is a bouncer, not a door: it blocks or lets through, but offers the agent no clean path to the data. The open standard for the door exists; MCP sign-in builds on OAuth 2.1.[9] I found no market size for this field, so I name none. Gap for twiceD: a mid-sized company with a 10-year-old dealer portal has neither Plaid nor its own platform team. It needs someone to build the door onto its existing system, with a log for data protection and in German.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium to high. The log analysis and the read server are doable solo in 3 to 5 weeks if the portal has a data interface. Without an interface it gets much more expensive, so check that in the first conversation. Maintenance: about 2 to 4 hours per month and customer for MCP specification updates and tests. Honest limits: demand is still a bet today. The banks show the trend, but whether dealer portals already see agent traffic is only proven by step (1). So start with the log analysis as a small, paid entry point. Write rights (orders) only later, with human approval, because that is where Wells Fargo's liability question returns. Scale through one contractor per customer; operation on the customer's server is possible. modelcontextprotocol.io · developers.cloudflare.com · plaid.com

First, banks in the U.S. According to an Axios report picked up by CBS, dozens of financial institutions are finding outside AI agents in customer accounts. Customers hand their password to the agent, and it logs in like a person. That's not an attack, says White House AI czar David Sacks. He suggests read-only access. JPMorgan's Jamie Dimon, by contrast, doesn't want outside agents in the house.

One answer to the liability question comes from Wells Fargo, according to SecureWorld. Starting November twenty-fourth, any transaction by a customer's agent is supposed to count as authorized by the customer, even one they didn't want. I haven't read the original agreement, so this is secondhand. My take: if you give away your password, you give away control. The better fix is an official door for agents.

The second story comes from hospitals. According to the insurer association Blue Cross Blue Shield, AI billing tools have raised insurers' costs by about nine hundred forty-two million dollars over two years. The software finds more secondary diagnoses, and those make a case more expensive. But a caution: the number comes from the insurers themselves, the side that pays. The hospital association says patients are also getting older and sicker.

And the online clinic Ro has launched an agent called Robin that suggests medications and dosages to customers. There are no figures on accuracy or on review by doctors. A Brown University researcher says he'd like to see evidence before millions of people use it. I agree.

On the AI Act, twenty-nine days remain until December second. That's when the ban on AI that generates non-consensual nude images takes effect. The grace period for labeling in older systems is expected to end the same day, though the exact date isn't fully certain.

That leads to the project idea for twiceD: an agent door for mid-sized companies' customer portals. Instead of dealers giving their password to an agent, the agent gets its own read-only access through the open MCP standard, with a log and the option to block it individually. The starting point is an analysis of how many logins already come from automated tools.

In the coming days I'll watch whether other banks follow Wells Fargo's lead, and whether a company from Germany or Brazil finally steps forward with a measured AI impact.
