CrowdStrike: an emergency stop for AI agents Deploy B2B-SaaS US

At its Fal.Con Europe conference in Barcelona on Nov. 3, CrowdStrike unveiled the "AI Kill Switch" as part of its Falcon Guardian product. "The AI Kill Switch is available today."[1] It works in three steps:

  • Stop: The Falcon sensor ends the running agent. According to the release, first at the gateway, on the endpoint only "soon".[1]
  • Block restart: A rule prevents the agent from starting again on the same machine or the same machine group.[1]
  • Cut the network: The agent loses access to the company network. An authorized admin triggers this, and every action is recorded in the audit log.[1]

The detection side followed on Nov. 4. "Agent Insider Threat Detection" is meant to learn the normal behavior of each agent and its user and "flags deviations even when no policy is violated". The release says "(Q4)", so the feature is not yet available.[2] Already on Nov. 3, CrowdStrike had announced plans to buy Fiddler (agent monitoring) and Dreadnode (automated AI security testing), plus an integration with Anthropic's Claude Cowork.[3]

Agent deviates→ Detection alerts (from Q4)→ Admin hits the stop→ Stop, restart block, network gone
AI agent: An AI program that does not just answer but acts on its own: sending emails, querying data, creating tickets. Like an intern with a key ring. It works fast, but you need to know which doors it can open.
Insider threat: A risk that comes from inside, from someone with real access. Classically that is an employee. Here it is an agent with real permissions that does things nobody wanted, without breaking a rule.
Source Everything here comes from CrowdStrike's own releases. The release on the kill switch gives no customer count and no measurements. Only one customer is quoted, Everpure's security chief. The only quantity given is generic: "Organizations are deploying thousands of AI agents."[2] There is no independent check of how well the kill switch works.

My take: The product release is marketing, but the signal behind it is not. Security firms only build kill switches when their customers ask for them. That means large companies run agents with real permissions, and security teams have lost track of them. For mid-sized companies the lesson is simpler and cheaper than a CrowdStrike license: anyone who deploys an agent must know beforehand how to shut it down. Concretely, that means which keys it revokes and who is allowed to do it. Today's project idea comes from that.

The control wave: Kong and others B2B-SaaS Global

CrowdStrike is not alone. According to the news aggregator aiagentstore.ai, Kong is extending its "Kong AI Management in Kong Konnect" (entry of Nov. 5). It is meant to govern which agents may call which tools.[4] On Oct. 30, Persona presented there a series of agents for identity checks, with decisions under human oversight. And on Oct. 31, Waters announced "AI Link", software that connects large language models to lab software so researchers can request lab tests in plain language.[4]

Source I know these three items only from a single aggregator that shows no source links. I did not open the companies' releases. All statements are vendor claims without numbers.

My take: The pattern is the same as with CrowdStrike: control comes after deployment. The Waters example is the most interesting one for everyday work, because it shows where agents are heading now: into specialist software with expensive equipment behind it. A wrong lab order costs real money. Without independent numbers, though, it remains a hint, not proof.

AI Act: 27 days to go Reg EU

Nothing new, the clock is running. The amendment to the AI Act (AI Omnibus) "was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July 2026".[5] The deadlines:

  • Wed, 12/02/2026: ban on AI that generates intimate images without consent or depictions of child abuse. "These prohibitions apply from 2 December 2026."[5]
  • Thu, 12/02/2027: high-risk AI under Annex III, such as hiring and credit decisions (previously 08/02/2026).[5]
  • Wed, 08/02/2028: high-risk AI in products under Annex I.[5]
Open For existing AI that generates images or text, there is a four-month transition period for labeling. The sources differ on exactly when it ends. So I give no date. I know the regulation's number only from a law-firm source, not from the Official Journal.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Agent registry with emergency stop: know what is running, and be able to shut it off

ELI5 & value: In mid-sized companies, AI agents rarely arrive through a big project. They arrive on the side: a chatbot in support, an agent in accounting, a subscription a department booked on its own. Each has keys, such as API access to the ERP or a mailbox. Nobody has the list. The agent registry is that list, plus a rehearsed emergency stop: for each agent it is set which keys it has, who may shut it down and how that works in five minutes. Value: a runaway agent becomes a small incident instead of a big one. Like a fire safety plan at a workplace: you rarely need it, but when you do, you need it immediately.
Prototype (V0): (1) A half-day workshop with IT and the departments. Result: a list of all agents and AI subscriptions. (2) For each entry, record the permissions: which accounts, which interfaces, which data. The access data comes from the existing identity system, such as Entra ID or Keycloak. (3) For each agent, a short shutdown recipe: revoke keys, deactivate account, cancel subscription, responsible person. (4) A dry run in which one agent is actually shut down, with timing. (5) A small script that checks the registry against the identity system and reports new, unrecorded AI access. MVP deliberately narrow: no own detection of behavioral deviations, no endpoint agent, no legal advice on the AI Act. Existing building blocks: the customer's identity system, a simple table or wiki as the registry, the AI vendors' admin interfaces.
Market & gap: At the very top of the market sits CrowdStrike. Its kill switch stops agents via the Falcon sensor, so only at companies that already have Falcon.[1] Behavioral detection only arrives in Q4.[2] For the AI Act side there is Aktlas, a platform founded in 2026 "for the Mittelstand". There, companies record their AI systems "in a central inventory", classify the risk and generate reports. There is a free tier for two systems, paid from €79 a month.[8] Aktlas targets legal documentation, not shutdown. Kong governs tool access at the gateway, per an aggregator, unconfirmed.[4] I found no market size, so I give none. Gap for twiceD: between CrowdStrike's enterprise security and Aktlas's compliance form, the practical part is missing. Someone has to look into the systems, find the keys and actually practice the shutdown once. A tool cannot do that alone; it takes hands-on work in German.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. This is mostly consulting with a small script, not a service that has to run around the clock. A registry for a customer with 5 to 15 agents is doable in 3 to 5 days of work, solo. Low maintenance: a quarterly appointment of 2 to 4 hours per customer to review the registry and do a new dry run. Honest limits: demand is weak today, because most mid-sized companies have not yet had an incident. The first incident in the industry will sell the product better than any slide. So it is best offered as an add-on package to an agent project, not as a standalone product. Scale via a contractor per customer. Fits the idea from Nov. 1 (agent reads but writes nothing): first keep the permissions small, then practice the emergency stop. crowdstrike.com · munich-startup.de · aiagentstore.ai
