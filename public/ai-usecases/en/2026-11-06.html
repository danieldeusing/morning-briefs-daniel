AI Nation 2030: Berlin sets target numbers Policy Public DE

The federal government's AI task force has presented its report "Deutschland als KI-Nation 2030" (Germany as an AI Nation 2030), ahead of the Digital Summit in Berlin. The Digital Ministry (BMDS) led the effort. The trade paper ZfK reported on it on Nov. 2, and heise analyzed it on Nov. 5.[1][2] The report has six fields and 18 key measures. The targets for 2030, according to heise:

  • Companies: "at least 80 percent of large companies and 55 percent of small and mid-size companies using AI" (German original: "mindestens 80 Prozent der Großunternehmen und 55 Prozent der kleineren und mittleren Unternehmen KI einsetzen").[1]
  • Administration: "90 percent of federal agencies are to use at least one AI application for core tasks by then." (German original: "90 Prozent der Bundesbehörden sollen bis dahin mindestens eine KI-Anwendung zur Bearbeitung von Kernaufgaben nutzen.")[1]
  • Economy: More than 10% of economic output is to come from AI. More AI startups are to be added.[1]
  • Data centers: 300% more AI data center capacity than in 2025, and at most 2.5 years from application to operation.[2]

The report justifies the pressure with money. In 2025, according to ZfK, around $286 billion of private AI capital flowed into the US, and $3.9 billion into Germany.[2] The "next year and a half" are to decide "whether Germany keeps up" (German original: "ob Deutschland den Anschluss hält").[1]

Source I did not open the original report (PDF at the BMDS) myself. All numbers come from two trade outlets. On the startup target, the sources contradict each other: one says three times, another four times as many. That is why I name no factor. According to ZfK, the report names neither the data centers' power demand nor the cost of the measures: "without a price tag" (German original: "ohne Preisschild").[2] After the intro, heise sits behind a paywall.
SME: Small and mid-size enterprises. In the EU that usually means: fewer than 250 employees. That is the Mittelstand, exactly twiceD's customers.

My take: The 55% target sounds ambitious, but maybe it is not. Bitkom reported as early as Sept. 14 that 57% of companies with 20 or more employees use AI (see Nov. 3). The two numbers do not measure the same thing: Bitkom counts from 20 employees up, while the task force counts all SMEs, including small businesses. If a chatbot subscription already counts as "AI use", the target is almost met and says little. So the important question is how the report measures "use". I could not check that. For twiceD the target is still useful: funding programs follow such numbers, and 90% of federal agencies with AI means many tenders.

E.on sends humanoids into the substation Deploy Energy DE

Neura Robotics from Swabia, E.on and the eastern German energy service provider EnviaM are testing humanoid robots for the power grid. heise reported this on Nov. 5, citing a dpa report.[3] The robots are to inspect substations and other critical facilities and do security rounds. Later they are also to take on heavy, monotonous or dangerous work. The model is called 4NE1, "the 1.8-meter-tall, roughly 64-kilogram model" (German original: "das 1,8 Meter große und rund 64 Kilogramm schwere Modell"). It is meant to move through facilities built for humans: stairs, doors, switch cabinets.[3]

First the robots train at the TUM RoboGym in Munich. There, skilled workers demonstrate the procedures. After that, the grid subsidiary Mitnetz Strom tests in the real grid. "First practical experience in grid operations is expected in the course of 2027." (German original: "Erste praktische Erfahrungen im Netzbetrieb werden im Laufe des Jahres 2027 erwartet.")[3] The AI model Neuron AI is to process images, sounds, touch and forces. E.on and EnviaM keep control over their data and the trained skills.[3]

Source One source (heise/dpa), no figures on the number of robots, locations or cost. This is a pilot, not operations.

My take: What is interesting is not the robot but the contract clause: the grid operators keep their data and the trained skills. That is sovereignty as a purchase condition, and in critical infrastructure at that. The "skilled labor shortage" argument is real. But it will be years before a humanoid walks through a substation on its own. For the next two years the simple variant pays off more: cameras and sensors that a human evaluates.

São Paulo: Meta may not simply take press content for AI Reg Consumer BR

The 44th Civil Court in São Paulo has issued a preliminary injunction against Meta. Three associations brought the suit: ANJ (newspapers), Abert (radio and TV) and Aner (magazines).[4][5] The dispute is about new terms of service from Meta that are due to apply from Nov. 20. Anyone who wants to stay on the platforms is to make their content available for free. The court forbids Meta to use "conteúdos jornalísticos sem autorização, inclusive para treinar sistemas de inteligência artificial" (journalistic content without authorization, including to train artificial intelligence systems).[5] That part of the terms is suspended. Meta may not penalize media outlets that refuse.[4]

"A Justiça fixou multa diária de R$ 1 milhão em caso de descumprimento, limitada a R$ 100 milhões." (The court set a daily fine of R$1 million for noncompliance, capped at R$100 million.)[5] heise converts this to about €162,000 per day.[4] The ANJ calls it "a first success". Neither source mentions a response from Meta.

Preliminary injunction (liminar): A quick, provisional court order. It applies until the court has decided at its leisure. Like a red light that can switch to green.
Open The date of the order is unclear: O Liberal reported on Nov. 3, heise on Nov. 5. Whether Meta will appeal is not in any source. O Liberal does not name the year of the "20 de novembro" deadline. From context it is 2026. The R$ 100 million cap comes from O Liberal alone (heise does not mention it). The daily R$ 1 million and the court are confirmed by two sources.

My take: Brazil is strict on AI training. The data protection authority ANPD already stopped Meta's training on user data in 2024. Now copyright comes on top, through an ordinary civil court. For companies this means: anyone who trains or feeds AI with third-party content in Brazil needs a clear license chain. "It's publicly on the web anyway" is not enough there. That also applies to small RAG projects that ingest press texts.

RAG: An AI system that looks things up in your own documents before answering, such as manuals or articles. Like an employee who quickly checks the binder before answering.

Apple turns off the AI tap Business B2B-SaaS US

"Apple is cutting the spending limits for its employees' AI use by a third." (German original: "Apple senkt die Ausgabenlimits für die KI-Nutzung seiner Mitarbeiter um ein Drittel.") That is how heise summarizes a Bloomberg report on Nov. 5.[6] The numbers:

  • Some engineers: daily limit in October from $300 to $200. For certain engineering teams, further cuts to $100 are apparently planned.[6]
  • Teams outside software development: $100 per day today. "For some teams the budget is even to drop to $25 per day." (German original: "Für manche Teams soll das Budget sogar auf 25 US-Dollar pro Tag sinken.")[6]
  • Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini are used. Some teams are said to be switching to cheaper or locally run models.[6]
Source Bloomberg cites people with knowledge of the matter, Apple says nothing. How much the cut saves is unclear: many did not use their old limit in full, according to the report. This is only via heise (citing Bloomberg); I did not find the Bloomberg original.

My take: This is the story behind the adoption rates. $300 per person per day over 220 working days is more than $60,000 a year, as much as a salary. Even Apple is now asking what that delivers. In the Mittelstand the problem is smaller, but it is coming: anyone who rolls out AI broadly (see the 55% target above) gets a new cost center with no control. The project idea below comes from that. How coding agents themselves drive budgets is covered in the ai-dev brief.

AI Act: 26 days to go Reg EU

Nothing new, the clock is running. The amendment to the AI Act (AI Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) "was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July 2026".[7] The deadlines:

  • Wed 12/02/2026: Ban on AI that generates intimate images without consent or depictions of child abuse. "These prohibitions apply from 2 December 2026."[7]
  • Wed 12/02/2026: End of the grace period for machine-readable labeling of generative AI that was already on the market before 08/02/2026. Legal500 cites "a limited four-month grace period", FPF gives December 2, 2026.[7][8]
  • Thu 12/02/2027: High-risk AI under Annex III, such as hiring and credit decisions. "from 2 December 2027 rather than 2 August 2026".[8]
  • Wed 08/02/2028: High-risk AI in products under Annex I.[8]
Update Yesterday I gave no date for the labeling, because the sources diverged. Today two sources agree: four months from 08/02/2026 gives 12/02/2026, and that is what FPF states. I still have the regulation number and the entry into force on 07/27 only from secondary sources (law firm, trade services), not from the Official Journal. Other law-firm sources confirm Regulation number 2026/1744 and the entry into force on 07/27/2026.
Machine-readable labeling: An invisible label in an AI image or AI text, such as a watermark or metadata. People do not see it, but programs can check it.

Context, not new: how deep AI really runs Study Global

These numbers are not today's news. But they help put the targets from Berlin in perspective.

  • Stanford AI Index 2026 (April): Agents fail "roughly one in three attempts on structured benchmarks".[9] According to the same article, 88% of companies use AI, and the number of documented AI incidents rose from 233 (2024) to 362 (2025).[9]
  • MIT FutureTech/CMU (July 2026): Only 11% of S&P 500 companies have embedded AI deeply. Only from a search excerpt.
  • Smarsh/FTI (July 2026): 55% use AI, but only 26% say their rules keep pace. Only from a search excerpt.
  • Ramp AI Index: Anthropic at 34.4% of business customers, OpenAI at 32.3%. Only via a newsletter, date unclear.
Methodology I did not open the Stanford report itself, only VentureBeat from Apr. 15. The 88% and the incident numbers appear there without methodology details. MIT/CMU, Smarsh/FTI and Ramp are one secondary source each, without sample size and without a verified link. Read them as a direction, not a measurement.

My take: The pattern is always the same: many "use" AI, few have it in the core process. Exactly this gap is hidden by a target like "55% of SMEs".

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · AI Cost Brake: budgets per team before the bill arrives

ELI5 & Value: In mid-size firms, people use AI today through many routes: a ChatGPT subscription here, an API key for a script there, an agent in accounting. Every route costs money per use, and nobody sees the total. The cost brake is a single gate that all of the company's AI calls pass through. There, each team has a budget, every request is counted, and at 80% there is a warning. Value: the company knows what AI costs and what for, before an agent burns through the monthly budget in one night. Like a power meter per department instead of one bill for the whole building. Apple is doing exactly this right now, only by hand and after the fact.[6]
Prototype (V0): (1) Inventory in half a day: which AI services, which keys, who pays. (2) A self-hosted LiteLLM proxy at the customer's site or in a German data center. All API keys move there. (3) Per team, a virtual key with a monthly budget and automatic reset. (4) A simple overview per team and model, plus a warning email at 80%. (5) A rule for which tasks go to a cheaper or local model. MVP deliberately narrow: API access only, no subscription licenses like ChatGPT Team, no billing to customers. Existing building blocks: LiteLLM (MIT license) with budgets per user, team and key, a Postgres database, the admin consoles of the model providers.
Market & Gap: The technology already exists. LiteLLM can "Apply a budget across all calls an internal user (key owner) can make on the proxy", with team budgets and reset after a period.[10] Gateway vendors like Kong are also building in AI controls, but I only know that from a news aggregator (see Nov. 5). I found no market size, so I name none. The gap is not the software but the rollout. A Mittelstand firm with 50 to 500 people has nobody to run a proxy, move all the keys and set budgets with the departments. Add sovereignty and data protection: the gate sees every prompt. Many companies want to run this with a German partner, not with a US SaaS. The task force's 55% target brings exactly these firms into the mainstream, and with scale come the costs.[1]
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium to high. Setup per customer is doable solo in 3 to 5 days, because LiteLLM supplies the heavy part. Operations are, honestly, not zero. The gate is a bottleneck: if it goes down, the whole company's AI stops. Plan for 2 to 4 hours per customer per month for updates, checks and new models, plus on-call for outages. One person can carry about 5 to 10 customers. Beyond that you need a second contractor as backup. The safe variant: the customer runs the proxy on their own server, twiceD sets it up and reviews it once a quarter. Then maintenance is almost only consulting. Fits with the agent registry from Nov. 5: the registry says which agents exist, the cost brake says what they cost. docs.litellm.ai · heise.de · heise.de
