US Midterms: The Data Center Is on the Ballot Policy Energy US

Euronews headlined on Nov 4: "Democrats win with a stance against AI data centers."[1] The figures from the article:

  • Voters: In the NBC exit poll, more than half named AI and data centers a "decisive or important factor" in their vote.[1]
  • Projects: In the second half of 2026, "at least 45 data center projects were stopped or disrupted by local opposition." According to Euronews, the figure comes from Data Center Watch and 10a Labs.[1]
  • Ohio: "Bans or other restrictions were passed in 15 municipalities."[1]
  • Texas: According to Euronews, Governor Abbott suspended permits for new data centers pending a review, after his challenger Hinojosa had promised a halt.[1] CIO.de wrote as early as Oct 26 that proceedings for "around 1,800 data centers" had been suspended.[2]

Sentiment has turned quickly. According to Pew (August, cited by CIO.de), half of US citizens believe data centers drive up electricity prices, up from 38% in January. A worse quality of life nearby was "feared most recently by 49 percent," versus 30% at the start of the year.[2]

Noise, power price, water on site→Municipalities stop projects→Election issue→Doubts about the AI build-out bill
Source I have not seen the NBC poll itself, only the account in Euronews. There is a counter-figure: in an NYT/Siena poll in Ohio, "only three percent" named AI and data centers as their most important issue.[2] So "important" does not mean "decisive for the vote." On the election result in Texas, the sources contradict each other. That is why I name none. The 1,800 proceedings are reported only by CIO.de, before the election.

According to heise, the stock market reacted with a "violent stock-market crash" in AI stocks.[3] The price figures and the context are in the Economy brief, not here.

My take: Until now, the question in the AI build-out was: are there enough chips and power? Now a third question is added: do the neighbors want it? This is the most important shift of the week, more important than any new model. Whoever uses AI services also pays for building the data centers in the end. If permits get slower, prices per request rise or fall more slowly. For mid-sized companies this means: efficiency becomes a selling point. Small and local models for routine work are not just sovereignty, but also a hedge against rising prices. On Nov 5, heise also reported that OpenAI promises "resident-friendly" rules for its data centers. I saw that only as a headline.

Germany: Data Centers Are Getting Thirstier Study Energy DE

The same debate is coming to Germany, with water instead of electricity prices. A short study by the Öko-Institut commissioned by Greenpeace estimates: data centers in Germany needed "around 13 billion liters of water for cooling" in 2025. By 2030 it could be "up to 27 billion liters per year." That is how heise reported on Nov 6, citing dpa.[4]

  • Planned are "almost 30 with 100 megawatts up to over 1 gigawatt of connected load," meaning very large facilities.[4]
  • A single 100 MW site needs "200 to 930 million liters" a year, according to the study, depending on cooling technology.[4]
  • The study assumes an average of 0.67 liters per kilowatt-hour of IT power. The industry association Bitkom says: almost two thirds of facilities use at most 0.1 liters per kilowatt-hour of connected load.[4]
Methodology The client is Greenpeace, so a party with a clear position. The data basis is thin: of around 1,200 operators required to report, only 139 usable reports came in.[4] The two figures use different bases (IT power versus connected load) and cannot be divided directly. Still, it is not a rounding dispute, but an open question about cooling technology. The 27 billion is an upper bound ("up to"), not a forecast.
Connected load (MW/GW): How much power a data center may draw at the same time. 1 gigawatt is roughly the output of a large power-plant unit. Almost all of the power turns into heat, and that has to go somewhere, often with water.

My take: The number itself is uncertain; the real issue is the measurement error. If only about one in nine operators reports usable data, nobody in this debate can calculate cleanly, neither Greenpeace nor Bitkom. The federal government's task force wants 300% more AI data-center capacity by 2030 (see 11/06). Then conflicts like this will reach German municipal councils too. The US is showing right now how quickly this becomes political.

Vale: AI Listens in on the Meeting and Writes the ERP Specification Deploy Mfg BR

This is the most concrete user story of the week. The mining group Vale is switching its ERP, a system with more than 30,000 users and over 350 connected systems. That is what Exame reported on Nov 5.[5] The problem with every ERP rollout: business departments explain how they work in many workshops. Then someone has to write requirements from that. At Vale, AI now does that first:

  • The workshops are recorded. AI turns the recordings into structured requirements, "mais de 270 requisitos" so far.[5]
  • As a result, the planning phase finishes up to three weeks earlier. The partner on the project is Accenture.[5]
  • Tech chief Theo Penedo makes it clear: "Não é adiantar o projeto como um todo, é adiantar a etapa de planejamento." In other words: the whole project does not get faster, only the planning.[5]
Source One source (Exame), company statements. Vale does not name costs or amounts saved. How many of the 270 requirements humans still had to rewrite is not in the article.
ERP: A company's central software for purchasing, warehousing, accounting and HR (SAP, for example). Switching ERPs is like heart surgery on a running operation. Most errors arise at the start, when requirements are written down wrong or not at all.

My take: I like this story precisely because it is modest. Vale does not promise "50% faster," but three weeks in one phase of a large ERP project. That is honest, and the lever is still big: a forgotten requirement later costs many times as much in an ERP project. AI does not replace a consultant here. It turns an hour of conversation into a first draft that a human reviews. This pattern fits almost any software project in mid-sized companies. The project idea below comes from it.

CoCounsel: An AI Agent for Law Firms in Brazil Deploy B2B SaaS BR

Thomson Reuters is bringing its law-firm agent CoCounsel Legal to Brazil, Exame reported on Nov 6.[6] First customers got access at the Fenalaw trade fair (Oct 27–29). General availability is due in November. The agent plans a research task, finds sources and drafts legal briefs. According to the vendor, it can analyze up to 10,000 documents with up to 100 questions in a table.[6] Thomson Reuters names no price.

The market is there. According to a report led by the bar association OAB-SP (March, over 1,800 respondents), 77% of lawyers in Brazil often use AI, up from 55% before.[6] The risks are there too: in May, a judge at the Superior Court STJ found a made-up precedent in a legal brief.[6]

Methodology The 77% come from a voluntary survey, as relayed by Exame. People who take part in such a survey are more likely to be interested in AI. Other figures in the article (1 million users in 107 countries) are vendor statements.

The best sentence comes from the vendor itself: "A IA assume mais etapas do trabalho, mas não assume a responsabilidade por ele." AI takes on more steps of the work, but not the responsibility.[6]

My take: Law is the area where "agent" moves fastest from buzzword to product, because the work consists of reading, searching and writing. The STJ case shows the price, though: a made-up ruling is not a typo, but a professional risk. The tool with the best source checking will win, not the one with the best text. Technically, CoCounsel is built on Anthropic's Agent SDK, according to Exame. The technology behind it is a dev topic for the ai-dev brief and does not belong here.

AI agent: A program that does not just give an answer, but plans and carries out several steps itself: search, read, compare, write. Like an intern who gets a task and comes back only with the result.

AI Act: 25 Days to Go, Now From the Official Journal Reg EU

Research again found contradictory information today. An article from before adoption gave December 2, 2026 for the labeling duty, another gave December 2, 2027. That is why I read Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 myself on EUR-Lex.[7] The text of the law is unambiguous:

  • Wed 12/02/2026, new bans: The new points (ba) and (bb) in Article 5(1), meaning AI for intimate images without consent and for abuse depictions, "shall apply from 2 December 2026" (Article 113).[7]
  • Wed 12/02/2026, labeling: Providers of generative AI whose systems "have been placed on the market before 2 August 2026" must comply with Article 50(2) "by 2 December 2026" (new Article 111, paragraph 4).[7]
  • Thu 12/02/2027: High-risk AI under Annex III, such as candidate selection and credit granting.[7]
  • Wed 08/02/2028: High-risk AI in products under Annex I.[7]
Update Until now, the two December dates came from law-firm texts (Legal500, FPF). Now they are verified in the Official Journal. The dates for high-risk systems are in the Official Journal in Article 113(c): December 2, 2027 (Annex III) and August 2, 2028 (Annex I). The earlier December 2, 2027 date for labeling probably comes from a draft. It is not in the adopted text.
Article 50(2): The duty to label AI-generated images, audio, video and text in a machine-readable way, for example with watermarks or metadata. Humans do not see it, but programs can check it.

My take: For twiceD customers, the labeling is what matters most. The duty falls on the providers of the AI systems, not on every company that uses ChatGPT. But anyone who built a tool themselves that generates images or text and was already running before August has 25 days. A short check is worthwhile.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Requirements Scribe: Turning Workshops Into a Reviewable Specification

ELI5 & Value: In every ERP or software rollout, subject-matter experts and consultants sit in workshops for hours. Afterward, someone writes down the requirements from notes and memory. A lot gets lost in the process, and you often notice only when the system is live. The scribe records the workshops (with consent) and turns them into a draft: a list of requirements, each with a reference to the point in the conversation it came from. A human reviews and signs off. Value: fewer forgotten requirements and faster planning. Vale shows the order of magnitude: up to three weeks earlier, more than 270 requirements.[5] Like a court clerk who notes the time for every sentence, so that anyone can verify who said what.
Prototype (V0): (1) A real customer project with 3 to 5 recorded workshops as a test set. (2) Local transcription with an open speech model such as Whisper, on a server at the customer or in a German data center. (3) A language model extracts requirements from it in a fixed template: process, role, must/can, open question, plus timestamp as evidence. (4) A review step in which the consultant accepts, changes or discards each requirement. (5) Export to Jira, Azure DevOps or Excel. MVP deliberately narrow: one project type (e.g. ERP rollout), German, no live participation in meetings, no automatic loading into the target system.
Market & Gap: The basic pattern already exists. ZBrain offers an agent that turns "unstructured inputs like transcripts, notes, and summaries into structured, actionable user stories."[10] According to search snippets, there is something similar from Deloitte (on AWS) and as a Salesforce app. Many teams build it themselves from Fathom, NotebookLM and ChatGPT, as a Scaled Agile post describes (search snippet only).[11] At Vale, the large consulting house Accenture does it.[5] I did not find a market size, so I name none. The gaps: the tools are generic (user stories for software teams), not for ERP questions like "standard or customization?" or data migration. For ZBrain, the page names neither price nor hosting location. And the DIY solution sends workshop recordings full of company internals to US services, which in mid-sized companies often fails at the works council or on data protection. Gap for twiceD: the middle between Accenture and the DIY solution. Run locally, in German, with evidence per requirement, fitted to the customer's templates.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. The hard parts (transcription, language model) exist off the shelf. twiceD builds the template, review interface and export. A V0 is doable solo in 3 to 4 weeks. The offer takes two forms. First, as a service within its own project: twiceD uses the scribe in its consulting workshops, so there is no maintenance at the customer. Second, as an installation at the customer or at an ERP partner: then about 2 to 3 hours per customer and month are added for updates and model switches. That supports one person for about 5 to 8 installations. The honest risk is not the technology, but consent: recordings need clear rules with the works council and participants. This template is part of the offer. The variant with an open model on own hardware is the selling point here, not an extra. zbrain.ai · scaledagile.com · exame.com
