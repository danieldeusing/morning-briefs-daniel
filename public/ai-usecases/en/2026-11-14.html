Glia: $100 million with AI for banks only, says Glia Deploy Finance US

Glia sells an AI platform for customer contact at banks and credit unions. On Nov 10 the company announced: "Glia has surpassed $100 million in annual recurring revenue".[1] The platform reportedly runs at "700 institutions". In the third quarter of 2026, sales of the AI products reportedly rose 93% year over year.[1] The growth was "achieved while maintaining profitability"; the release gives no profit figure.[1]

What Glia offers: chat and phone with AI, a branch version, a customer management system for banks and "Agentic Workflows" that run fixed work processes across several bank systems.[1] Harvard Federal Credit Union is named as a customer. It reportedly replaced manual legacy processes with automated workflows; no metric is given.[1]

ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue): Revenue from ongoing subscriptions, projected over a year. Like a gym saying: all our current members together pay us 100 million a year. That is not profit and not money already collected, but the state of the contracts today.
Vertical AI: AI built for just one industry instead of all of them. A general practitioner versus a dentist: the dentist can do less, but knows the little they do exactly, including the rules of their field.
Methodology A pure vendor press release, published via ffnews.com. No independent check. The 93% refers to sales of the AI products, not total revenue. The baseline is missing. The release says nothing about the effect at the banks (for example, fewer calls to a human).

My take: The impact figure is missing, but the revenue figure still says something. 700 banks pay for an AI that only knows banks. The sales argument is not the best model, but rules, security and connection to the bank systems. That is the same lesson as with Bitkom: customers in regulated industries buy trust and fit, not raw performance. For twiceD this means: narrow and industry-deep beats broad and general.

The week in three patterns Study Finance DE BR

1. Show the value in euros. McKinsey reports in its "State of AI": "Thirty-seven percent of respondents do attribute at least some EBIT impact to AI use." The share of top companies with at least a 5% profit effect stays "flat at about six percent".[4] Among companies with more than $1 billion in revenue, 40% are scaling AI agents, up from 27% last year. Among smaller companies the figure stays at 22%.[4] Santander named €194 million in "business value" (Nov 13 brief). According to its president, Banco do Brasil calculates by its own account that about 2,300 AI agents delivered an efficiency gain equal to the work of 20,000 employees within 12 months.[3]

Methodology McKinsey: read only via diginomica. The report does not state the sample or the survey period; it is self-reporting by managers. Banco do Brasil: the bank's own estimate at a congress on 2026-10-07; the calculation method is not public. Santander: self-reported, with no comparison value.

2. AI from Europe, but not more expensive. Bitkom reported on Nov 2: "96 Prozent der Unternehmen würden bevorzugt KI-Anwendungen deutscher Anbieter nutzen."[2] The preference for US AI fell from 51% to 41%, while that for France rose from 38% to 46%. China fell from 18% to 12%.[2] But 64% are "nicht bereit, dafür Abstriche in Kauf zu nehmen".[2] (Covered in detail in the Nov 9 brief.)

Methodology Two phone surveys by Bitkom Research: 603 companies (weeks 28 to 33 of 2026) and 602 companies (weeks 33 to 39 of 2026), each with 20 or more employees.[2] The questions were about preferences, not purchases. Bitkom is an industry association whose members also sell AI.

3. Banks lead the way. Glia (700 institutions), Banco do Brasil (2,300 agents), Santander (€194 million), plus Australia's NAB with the assistant "Ruby" for 40,000 employees (Nov 13 brief). The most concrete numbers of the week came from the financial sector. That makes sense: banks have lots of text, lots of rules, lots of customer contacts and enough money for pilot projects.

My take: The three patterns are connected. Companies want AI that demonstrably earns money, from a source they trust, and at no premium. These three wishes do not always fit together. Whoever can check whether a European model is good enough for a specific task resolves exactly this conflict. The idea below builds on that.

AI Act: 18 days left Reg EU

On 2026-12-02 the grace period for labeling under Art. 50(2) ends. Generative AI systems that were already on the market before 2026-08-02 must then mark their outputs in a machine-readable way. The law firm Lewis Silkin writes: "compliance required by 2 December 2026".[5] New systems have had to do this since 2026-08-02.[5]

The basis is the AI Omnibus package. It was published in the Official Journal on 2026-07-24 and "enters into force today, 27 July 2026".[5] The high-risk rules under Annex III are postponed "from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027". For AI in products under Annex I "the deadline shifts further to 2 August 2028".[5]

Note All dates come from law firms, not directly from EUR-Lex. Law firms give the regulation number as (EU) 2026/1744. We did not verify it in the Official Journal.

For mid-sized companies: The obligation mainly falls on providers of systems that generate images, audio or text, not on every company that uses a chatbot. A checking aid for this was in the Nov 2 brief.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The trade-off test: does our AI workflow also run on an EU model?

ELI5 & Value: Almost all German companies would rather take AI from Germany, but two thirds do not want to give anything up for it.[2] Whether they would have to give something up, usually nobody knows. The test answers that for one concrete workflow of the customer, for example sorting emails or summarizing quotes. The same 100 real tasks run once through today's US model and once through a model running in a European data center. Then you compare quality, speed and price. Like a test drive with two cars on the same route. Value: a decision based on evidence instead of gut feeling, and a ready migration plan if the EU model is enough.
Prototype (V0): (1) Choose one workflow with the customer and collect 50 to 100 real, anonymized examples with good answers. (2) Load these examples as a test set into an open evaluation tool, such as promptfoo. (3) Run the test set against today's model and against two to three EU-hosted models. (4) Score the results: automatically where possible, and with spot checks by a subject-matter employee. (5) A two-page report: quality, monthly cost, risks, recommendation. MVP deliberately narrow: one workflow, no rebuild of the software, no own platform. Existing building blocks: the evaluation tool and the programming interfaces of the hosting providers.
Market & Gap: The EU models themselves already exist at low prices. STACKIT offers, in the "EU01" region, among others gpt-oss-120b, Llama 3.3 70B and Gemma 4 31B, for €0.45 per million input tokens and €0.65 per million output tokens.[8] STACKIT advertises a "data-sovereign STACKIT Cloud" on the page.[8] IONOS offers similar open models in its AI Model Hub, with an "OpenAI-compatible REST API", for example gpt-oss-120b at $0.17 / $0.71 per million tokens.[7] Both pages are undated, so prices may change. So the offer is there. What is missing is proof for your own task: the providers sell compute time, not the answer to "Is this enough for our complaint emails?". The gap for twiceD: a neutral test at a fixed price, in German, with the customer's data and an honest recommendation, even if it says "stay with the US model". We found no market size for such tests. Whether German agencies already sell this as a package, we did not research today. That is the next step.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. It is consulting with a clear end. A test takes about four to six working days solo. Most of that goes to the test set, not to the technology. The compute cost at these prices is in the low euro range per run. Maintenance: none, as long as twiceD does not operate the migration itself. Follow-up business: the migration itself and a re-test when new models arrive, a few hours per quarter. Scaling via one test template per industry and a contractor. The honest risk: the hardest part is getting good sample answers from the customer. Without them the test measures nothing. bitkom.org · stackit.com · cloud.ionos.com
