Sunday question: Who counts what AI access delivers? Study Deploy Global

The pattern of recent weeks is clear: big companies roll AI out to everyone. According to TechCrunch, Uber is giving "its entire global workforce — some 30,000 people" access to ChatGPT Work.[2] What that delivers or costs, Uber does not say (brief of Oct. 26).

The McKinsey figures fit that picture. "Only 37% of organizations report any positive EBIT contribution, essentially flat compared with last year," diginomica sums up.[1] At the same time, 40% of companies with more than $1 billion in revenue are scaling AI agents, up from 27% last year. For smaller companies the figure stays at 22%.[1] So the big players are expanding even though the profit effect is not growing on average.

One figure from the same report matters for Germany: 39% of respondents expect fewer employees next year because of AI, up from 32%. 43% expect no change.[1] According to diginomica, the declines expected last year were about twice as high as those later reported.[1]

EBIT: Earnings before interest and taxes, meaning what the core business earns. "AI contributes to EBIT" means the AI lowers costs or brings in revenue, and you can see it on the balance sheet, not just in how users feel.
Methodology Read only via diginomica (2026-11-04); the McKinsey page itself did not load. We do not know the sample or the survey period. It is self-reporting by managers, not measured impact. The agent figures are taken from a summary, not quoted verbatim.

My take: I read two things here. First: the gap between 40% and 22% is the story of mid-sized companies. Smaller firms are not making progress on scaling, and not because of the technology. Second: if four in ten managers expect AI-driven job cuts, the employees expect it too. In a German company with a works council, that worry lands straight on the negotiating table. "Access for everyone" as at Uber is not a click in the admin console there, but a negotiation.

Germany: No AI rollout without the works council Policy B2B SaaS DE

The German Works Constitution Act (Betriebsverfassungsgesetz) gives the works council a co-determination right over the "introduction and use of technical devices designed to monitor the behavior or performance" of employees (§ 87 Abs. 1 Nr. 6).[4] The law firm CMS writes: "Entscheidend ist bereits, dass ein KI-System grundsätzlich geeignet ist, Leistungs- oder Verhaltensdaten auszuwerten" (what matters is that an AI system is in principle suited to analyzing performance or behavior data).[3] And because AI systems are "regelmäßig objektiv geeignet" (regularly objectively suited) for this, introducing them requires "in aller Regel eine Betriebsvereinbarung" (as a rule a works agreement).[3]

Then there is § 80 Abs. 3: "Muss der Betriebsrat zur Durchführung seiner Aufgaben die Einführung oder Anwendung von Künstlicher Intelligenz beurteilen," then "gilt insoweit die Hinzuziehung eines Sachverständigen als erforderlich" (calling in an expert is deemed necessary).[5] So the works council does not have to justify why it needs an AI specialist. It agrees the details with the employer.[5]

Works agreement (Betriebsvereinbarung): A contract between the company and the works council that applies to all employees. Like house rules that a landlord and a tenants' council write together. For an AI tool it states, for example: what you use it for, what is stored, and that nobody is monitored with the usage data.
Co-determination (Mitbestimmung): On certain topics the company cannot decide alone. Without agreement with the works council, things do not move forward. That is not a say, but a right of consent.

CMS recommends "Rahmenbetriebsvereinbarungen" (framework works agreements) that regulate the process for new IT or AI applications.[3] But that "funktioniert aber verständlicherweise nicht bei grundlegenderen KI-Transformationen" (understandably does not work for more fundamental AI transformations).[3] A second piece of advice: "Unternehmen sollten KI-Einführung und organisatorische Folgeprojekte möglichst voneinander trennen" (companies should separate AI introduction from follow-up organizational projects where possible).[3] So anyone who wants to introduce a tool and restructure jobs at the same time makes both conversations harder.

Note This is a law firm's assessment (CMS, 2026-07-21), not a ruling and not legal advice. Whether a works agreement is needed in a specific company is for a specialist employment lawyer to check. Statute quotes are from gesetze-im-internet.de.

My take: This is not a brake, but a reason why AI in mid-sized companies may arrive more slowly, yet more properly. The negotiation rarely fails on the question "do we want AI?". It fails on questions nobody at the table can answer: What data does the tool store, for how long, and can the boss analyze individual users? The lawyers write the contract. Nobody often supplies the technical facts for it. That is the gap for the idea below.

AI Act: 17 days left Reg EU

According to the law firm Garrigues, the AI Omnibus package was "published today, July 24, in the Official Journal" and took effect "three days later, on July 27."[6] On 2026-12-02 two points from it kick in:

  • New bans: AI systems that generate nude or sexual images of real people without consent, and systems for depictions of child abuse. "These prohibitions will take effect on December 2, 2026."[6]
  • Labeling: For generative systems that were on the market before 2026-08-02, "a transitional period is introduced until December 2, 2026, to comply with the obligation to label their outputs".[6]

One change affects almost every company that uses AI: the AI literacy obligation. According to Garrigues, it is being cut back: "The obligation now focuses on adopting measures to support the development of literacy, without requiring a specific level of literacy from each individual."[6] The high-risk rules under Annex III do not come until 2027-12-02, and for AI in regulated products on 2028-08-02.[6]

AI literacy: Anyone who uses AI at work should make sure employees know what the tool can do and where it makes mistakes. After the Omnibus this means: the company has to do something about it, for example training. But it does not have to prove a specific knowledge level for each individual.
Note All dates come from a law firm, not directly from EUR-Lex. The regulation number, (EU) 2026/1744, is given by other law firms. We have not checked it in the Official Journal.

For mid-sized companies: The bans hit almost no ordinary company. The labeling rule mainly hits providers of systems that generate images, audio or text. A checking aid for this was in the brief of Nov. 2.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The works council fact sheet: technical facts on the AI tool before negotiations start

ELI5 & Value: A company wants to give its employees an AI tool, for example Microsoft Copilot, ChatGPT or Claude. Because AI systems could almost always analyze performance or behavior, it usually needs a works agreement for this.[3] The negotiation then hinges on questions like: What does the tool log? For how long? Who in the company can see per-person reports, and can that be switched off? twiceD reads the admin console and the vendor's documentation and writes the answers down in two to four pages in German. Like an inspection report on a used car: both sides negotiate over the same facts instead of assumptions. Value: weeks less negotiation, and a rollout that does not get stuck in committee.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick a tool, for example ChatGPT Enterprise or Microsoft 365 Copilot. (2) In a test account, go through every setting on logs, retention, usage reports and admin rights, and document it with screenshots. (3) Turn that into a fixed grid: data type, storage location, duration, who sees it, switch to turn it off. (4) For each customer, check the grid against their real configuration and flag the gaps. (5) A "technical annex" chapter that the lawyer can drop directly into the works agreement. MVP deliberately narrow: one tool, no legal advice, no contract text. Existing building blocks: the vendors' admin documentation and freely available model agreements.
Market & Gap: The legal part already exists. Law firms like CMS advise on co-determination and framework agreements.[3] Model works agreements are freely available online. One of them (skill-sprinters.de, April 2026) gives the works council access to "die Nutzungsprotokolle der KI-Systeme" (the usage logs of the AI systems), but says nothing about retention periods and nothing about admin settings.[9] The template calls itself "ein Orientierungsrahmen" (a guidance framework) and recommends review by a specialist lawyer.[9] This is exactly where the gap lies: the lawyer knows the law, but not the switches in the admin console. The mid-sized company's IT knows the console, but does not write for the works council. On top of that comes § 80 Abs. 3: the works council may also bring in an expert on AI questions.[5] So twiceD can appear on both sides, just not in the same company at the same time. We found no market size for such expert reports. Whether IT experts already sell this as a fixed package, we did not research today. That is the next step.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. It is consulting with a clear end. The first grid for one tool costs about one to two weeks solo, after that each customer is two to three working days. Maintenance: vendors change their settings often. The grid needs a few hours per tool each quarter, otherwise it becomes wrong. Scaling: one grid per tool (Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini), one contractor for the customer meetings. The honest risk: the line to legal advice must stay clear. twiceD delivers facts, not contract clauses, and works best with an employment law firm for that. cms.law · skill-sprinters.de · gesetze-im-internet.de
