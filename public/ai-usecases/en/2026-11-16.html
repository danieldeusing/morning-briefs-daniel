Bitkom: AI from Germany, gladly, but please at the same price Study B2B SaaS DE

At the Digital Summit, the industry association Bitkom presented two surveys of German companies. The core figure is clear: "96 percent of companies would prefer to use AI applications from German providers."[1] For 87%, the country of origin matters when choosing an AI provider.[1] Then comes the sentence that puts the number in perspective: "However, 64 percent are not willing to accept trade-offs for it," meaning no higher prices and no weaker features.[1]

The shift between countries is interesting. The question was where respondents would prefer to source AI from:[1]

Provider origina year ago2026
France38%46%
other EU countries40%43%
USA51%41%
China18%12%

The US now ranks behind France and the rest of the EU.[1] Bitkom president Ralf Wintergerst, however, warns against reading this too simply: "Digital sovereignty is not decided by where we buy AI."[1] In line with that, 64% think Germany needs more hyperscalers.[1]

Digital sovereignty: A company can decide for itself where its data is stored and which provider processes it. And it can switch providers without operations grinding to a halt. Like a house where you can change electricity providers without laying new cables.
Hyperscaler: A very large cloud provider with data centers in many countries, for example Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure or Google Cloud. German providers such as STACKIT or IONOS are much smaller.
Methodology Two phone surveys of companies with 20 or more employees: 603 companies (calendar weeks 28–33/2026) and 602 companies (calendar weeks 33–39/2026).[1] The report does not say clearly which figure comes from which wave. Read via it-finanzmagazin.de, not directly on bitkom.org. It is a stated preference, not buying behavior. We deliberately leave out another Bitkom figure on AI use in Germany: different reports give 36%, 41% or 57%, and we have not checked the primary source.

My take: 96% versus 64% is the real finding. German companies want sovereignty, but as a free add-on. That is not a contradiction but a clear buying condition: a German offering only wins if it is equally good for the specific task and not more expensive. Whether that holds, usually nobody knows. The company compares brand names, not results on its own documents. That is exactly the measurement that is missing, and the idea below is for that.

BMO: AI helper in the credit card call center, with targets instead of results Deploy Finance CA

According to FStech, the Canadian bank BMO is deploying an AI assistant in its credit card call center. The team gets "more than 500,000 calls each year," and a call takes on average "12 to 14 minutes to resolve."[2] It is an internal tool for the employees on the phone, not for customers. How exactly it helps during the conversation, the report describes only roughly.

The numbers BMO gives are targets. For eligible requests, handling is meant to be "between 20 and 30 per cent" faster.[2] And the AI is meant to handle "around 40 per cent of eligible credit refund requests automatically," that is, refunds on the credit card.[2] Overall, BMO wants to reach benefits of more than 1 billion Canadian dollars from AI by 2030.[2]

Agent Assist: The AI does not talk to the customer itself. It sits next to the employee, like an experienced colleague who opens the right page in the manual during the call and pre-fills the form. The human decides and speaks.
Handling time: The time from picking up the call until the case is resolved, including follow-up work on screen. Call centers almost always calculate their costs from this number.
Note All impact figures are targets from the announcement, not measured results. BMO does not say how many of the 500,000 calls are "eligible." Our own calculation as an upper bound: if all calls were eligible, 25% of 13 minutes across 500,000 calls would come to about 27,000 hours a year. The real figure is smaller.

My take: This is the right shape for AI in customer service. The bank does not replace the human on the phone but gives them a tool for the routine parts of the conversation. And it starts with a narrowly defined task, the refunds. I remain skeptical about the announcement culture: "up to 30%" appears in the press release, and the measured result rarely follows. I am noting BMO and will check in half a year whether there are real numbers.

AI Act: 16 days left Reg EU

According to the law firm Garrigues, the AI Omnibus package entered into force on Jul 27, 2026.[4] Two points from it take effect on Dec 2, 2026:

  • New bans: AI systems that generate nude or sexual images of real people without consent, and systems for depictions of child abuse. "These prohibitions will take effect on December 2, 2026."[4]
  • Labeling: Generative systems that were on the market before Aug 2, 2026 must label their outputs from Dec 2, 2026.[4]

The high-risk rules under Annex III come only on Dec 2, 2027, and for AI in regulated products on Aug 2, 2028.[4] For a normal company that only uses AI, almost nothing changes on Dec 2. Mainly affected are providers of systems that generate images, audio or text. More in the brief from Nov 15.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The sovereignty test drive: the same AI task, once with a US provider, once hosted in Germany

ELI5 & value: According to Bitkom, 96% of companies want AI from Germany, but 64% do not want to make trade-offs for it.[1] The question "is the German offering just as good for our task?" is answered with data by almost nobody today. twiceD takes a real task from the customer, for example summarizing quotes or extracting invoices. This task runs in parallel on the current US tool and on an open model running in a German data center. At the end there is a report with three numbers: quality, cost per month, effort of switching. Like a test drive with two cars on the same route, instead of comparing brochures. Value: the company decides on sovereignty with facts, not gut feeling, and knows whether it can switch "without trade-offs."
Prototype (V0): (1) Choose a task with the customer and 50–100 real examples, with the correct answer for each example. (2) Send the same requests through an OpenAI-compatible interface to two targets: the current tool and a German hosting provider. IONOS explicitly cites "an OpenAI-compatible API" for its AI Model Hub.[8] (3) Score the answers against the correct answers, automatically and by manual spot check. (4) Project the costs from the real token volumes and the public price lists. (5) A three-page report with a clear recommendation: switch, mix or stay. The MVP is deliberately narrow: one task, two providers, no migration, no ongoing operation. Existing building blocks: open evaluation tools for LLM outputs and the providers' interfaces.
Market & gap: German hosting already exists, and it is cheap. STACKIT promises "easy access to the latest GenAI models in a secure environment on the data-sovereign STACKIT Cloud" and lists, for example, Llama 3.3 70B at €0.45 input and €0.65 output per million tokens.[7] IONOS advertises "100% GDPR-compliant and securely hosted in Germany" and lists the same model at €0.65 per million tokens, input and output alike.[8] Both sell an interface, though, not a comparison. Whether their model reads a specific customer's invoices as well as the familiar US tool, the customer has to find out themselves. Alongside, partner companies are building whole "sovereign" chat platforms on STACKIT. That is a product sale, not a neutral test. The gap for twiceD: the neutral, short comparison on the customer's data, before the purchase decision. Exactly what the 64% group from the Bitkom survey needs. We found no market size for such comparisons. Whether consultancies already offer this as a fixed package, we did not check today.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. It is consulting with a clear end. The first test framework costs about a week solo, after that each customer is three to five working days, mostly for collecting and checking the examples. No ongoing maintenance, no own server, no GPU. A few hours per quarter to keep price lists and model selection current, since they change often. Scaling: a contractor for the evaluation work. Honest risk: customer data leaves the building for the test. The test therefore has to run on German hosting itself or with redacted examples, otherwise it contradicts its own purpose. A follow-up engagement, the migration itself, is bigger and needs its own review. it-finanzmagazin.de · stackit.com · cloud.ionos.de

It's Monday, November sixteenth. Another quiet day: for the past week, our research turned up hardly any new reports on AI in real-world use with a named company and a verifiable number. So today, two pieces from the last two weeks that deserve a second look.

First, the Bitkom survey. Ninety-six percent of German companies would rather use AI from German providers. But sixty-four percent don't want to pay more or give up features to get it. Sovereignty, yes, but at the same price.

Meanwhile, the U.S. is losing favor. Only forty-one percent would now prefer AI from the United States, down from fifty-one a year ago. France climbs from thirty-eight to forty-six percent. The figures come from two phone surveys of about six hundred companies each, read through a trade report rather than directly from the association.

Then the Canadian bank BMO. It's rolling out an internal AI assistant in its credit card call center to help employees on the phone. According to FStech, the team gets more than five hundred thousand calls a year, and each one takes twelve to fourteen minutes on average. The bank wants to cut handling time on eligible calls by twenty to thirty percent and automatically resolve about forty percent of eligible refunds. The key point: those are targets, not measured results.

On the AI Act, sixteen days remain until December second. That's when the ban on AI that creates nude images of real people without their consent takes effect. And older AI systems that generate images or text will have to label their output. For companies that simply use AI, little changes.

The project idea for twiceD is a sovereignty test drive. A real task from the customer runs side by side on the current U.S. tool and on a model hosted in Germany, for example at STACKIT or IONOS. In the end, quality, cost and switching effort come together in a three-page report. That's exactly the answer the big group that wants to buy German, but without trade-offs, is looking for.
