United Airlines: No entry for third-party agents Policy Consumer US

United Airlines announced on Wednesday, Nov 11, that it would restrict access for third-party AI agents. According to Skift, the statement says United is restricting access "to prohibit use by unauthorized, third-party personal AI agents or assistants".[1] This applies to its own channels, meaning website and app, and according to Skift also to the route via online travel agencies and comparison portals.[1] That makes United, per Skift, "the first carrier in the U.S. to explicitly say it will restrict access to third-party AI agents".[1]

Personal AI agent: A program that acts on its own on the web on behalf of a person: opening pages, filling in forms, paying. Like a butler to whom you hand your credit card and the password to your frequent-flyer account and say: "Book me the cheapest flight to Frankfurt."

The reasons according to Skift: "regulatory and consumer safety concerns" and the risk that sharing login credentials with a third-party agent exposes sensitive data.[1] The article's summary also lists unwanted purchases, changes to the account, redeeming miles, and conflicts with US FAA and DOT rules at check-in. The second reason is more openly on the table: United CEO Scott Kirby said, per Skift quoting the Wall Street Journal: "I don't want to create another middleman."[1] Delta CEO Ed Bastian is, per Skift, "cautious" on agents in booking.[1]

Middleman: Someone who sits between customer and provider and earns money from the relationship. In travel, those classically are booking portals. An AI agent could become the next one: it decides which airline the customer gets to see at all.
Note Read at Skift (Nov 11, 2026), not in a statement from United itself. The article does not say from which day the block applies, how United detects third-party agents, or what happens to flights already booked. Other reports say Delta allows registered agents. We could not verify that. That is why only the Skift statement "cautious" appears here.

My take: Kirby's sentence is more honest than the security reasons, and both are true. Whoever owns a customer relationship does not want to hand it to an agent that books with a competitor tomorrow. And whoever runs accounts with monetary value (here: miles) has to know who is clicking. This is not an airline question. Every online shop and every booking portal in the German Mittelstand will soon have to answer it: which agents do we let in, and for what? Right now usually nobody decides, and the agent simply shows up. The idea below grew out of this.

AI Act: 14 days left Reg EU

The AI omnibus package "was published in the Official Journal of the EU on 24 July 2026", writes the Future of Privacy Forum.[2] On Dec 2, 2026, two points from it take effect:

  • Labeling: Generative systems that were on the market before Aug 2, 2026 got, according to Gibson Dunn, "a four-month grace period (until 2 December 2026)". After that they, too, must label their outputs in a machine-readable way.[4]
  • New bans: AI for sexual images of real people without consent and for depictions of child abuse. The transition period also ends on Dec 2, 2026.[4]

The high-risk rules under Annex III arrive on Dec 2, 2027, "previously 2 August 2026", and for AI in regulated products on Aug 2, 2028.[3]

Labeling (Art. 50(2)): An image, video or audio clip generated by AI carries an invisible tag that software can read. Like the watermark on a banknote: you do not see it right away, but you can check whether it is there.
Who is affected The obligations fall mainly on the provider of the generating system. A company that uses ChatGPT or Copilot only for internal text has to do hardly anything on Dec 2. Anyone who has AI generate images or audio and offers that as a product should know by then whether their tool labels.
Sources All from law firms and the FPF, not directly from EUR-Lex. The Gibson Dunn statement dates from May, before the adoption. Pinsent Masons confirms the high-risk dates in July. Law-firm sources name the act as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744; EUR-Lex not checked.

Bitkom, checked at the source: 57% use AI Study B2B-SaaS DE

On Monday we deliberately left out the Bitkom figure on AI use. Reports gave 36%, 41% or 57%, and we did not have the primary source. Now we do. The press release of Sept 14, 2026 says (translated from the German): "For the first time, with 57 percent, the majority of companies use AI." And: "A year ago it was only 36 percent, two years ago just 20 percent."[5] So the 36% is the prior-year value. The 41% does appear in the release, but as the share who said "AI is not an issue" two years ago, not as a usage rate.

Share of companies with 20 or more employees that use AI (Bitkom, in %)

More interesting than the headline figure is what companies pay for. AI users name these as a major cost item:[5]

Cost itemShare of AI users
Infrastructure (cloud, compute)51%
Data preparation50%
Integration into existing systems41%
Subscriptions and licenses for AI software21%

The biggest hurdles among users: data protection (66%), legal uncertainty (56%) and the worry "that company data falls into the wrong hands" (45%).[5] Those who do not yet use AI mainly lack know-how. Added to that are legal uncertainties (66%) and costs that are hard to calculate (63%).[5] 45% of companies are spending more on AI in 2026 than the year before.[5] And: 45% expect a slight and 12% a significant decline in headcount in two years. Among companies that already use AI, only 9% expect a significant decline.[5]

Data preparation: Before an AI can work with company data, the data must be found, cleaned and brought into a readable form: merging duplicate customers, turning old PDFs into text, clarifying rights. Like chopping vegetables before cooking: it takes longer than the cooking itself, and nobody photographs it.
Methodology Telephone survey of 603 companies with 20 or more employees in Germany, fieldwork calendar weeks 28 to 33/2026.[5][6] Bitkom is an industry association of the digital economy and has an interest in high numbers. "Using AI" in such a survey often already means: part of the workforce uses ChatGPT. The jobs question measures expectations, not layoffs. We did not read the exact figure on missing know-how word for word and therefore do not give it.

My take: The cost table is the most important finding of the whole survey. Only one in five AI users names the license as a major cost item, but one in two names data and a good two in five name integration. So the money flows into the work around the model, not into the model. That is exactly where a small service provider earns. A second point: users are calmer than non-users on the jobs question. That may be experience, or companies with AI simply have different structures. The survey does not separate the two.

Methodology The base is only the AI-using companies among the 603 respondents, so roughly 340 companies. For values from 0 to 3% that is statistical noise, as Bitkom itself says. We have prior-year values only for the first three rows. The single-digit values come from a summary of the release, not from a verbatim quote.

My take: Together with Monday this gives a clear picture. German companies say "sovereignty" but buy US tools that already sit in the office suite. ChatGPT and Copilot keep growing, and the European alternative has zero users in this sample. That has little to do with model quality. It is about convenience: Copilot is already there with the Microsoft contract. Anyone who wants to sell a sovereign solution therefore does not sell a model but the path to it: the same task, equally good, with no extra effort. That was Monday's idea, and it still holds.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The agent house rules: who may browse an online shop, and who may buy?

ELI5 & Value: AI agents already visit online shops and booking portals today, on behalf of their users. United shows that a provider needs a stance on this.[1] Most Mittelstand companies have none: the agent simply shows up, or a bot shield turns it away across the board even though it would be a paying customer. twiceD helps the shop define "house rules" and enforce them technically. For example: agents may read prices and availability, but account login and payment only with verification. Like a doorman with a guest list instead of a locked door. Value: no lost revenue from wrongly blocked agents, less fraud and account trouble, and a clear answer when the customer asks "can my agent order from you?".
Prototype (V0): (1) Analyze two weeks of server or CDN logs: how many requests come from known AI agents, and to which pages? (2) Set up a table with the customer: which flow (search, cart, login, payment) is open, verified or blocked for agents. (3) Implement the rules technically. If the shop sits behind Cloudflare, via its detection of signed agents with Web Bot Auth.[9] If payment runs through a card provider, check whether its agent scheme is already usable.[8] (4) A simple overview for the customer: agent requests per week, blocked versus let through. MVP deliberately narrow: one shop, one shop platform, no custom detection software.
Market & gap: The building blocks exist. Cloudflare describes Web Bot Auth as an "authentication method that leverages cryptographic signatures in HTTP messages", used "as a verification method for verified bots and agents".[9] Visa, according to its own release of Jul 2, 2026, is working with "over 30 European issuers" so that AI agents can shop for cardholders. Merchants such as lastminute.com and Frasers take part via Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol and Agent Directory. Among the banks named are Commerzbank, comdirect and DKB.[8] What is missing: these are tools for infrastructure and payment, not a decision. Nobody sits down with the owner of a shop with 20 employees to settle which flows should be open to agents, and then builds that in. We did not find a reliable market size for "agent commerce" and therefore give none. Gap for twiceD: translating "what do we want as a company?" into rules at the CDN and in the shop, in German and with data protection in mind.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium. The build is easy: log analysis, a rules table and the configuration of existing tools, solo in 2–3 weeks per customer. Maintenance is low, one to two hours a month for the overview and new agents. Demand is uncertain: many Mittelstand companies do not see the topic yet, and the standards change fast. So offer it as a fixed package (review plus setup), not as an ongoing service, and only for shops with visible agent traffic. Scaling via one contractor per shop platform (for example Shopware or Shopify). skift.com · visa.co.uk · developers.cloudflare.com
