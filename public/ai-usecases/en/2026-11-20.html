Wells Fargo: Whoever Hands the Agent the Key Is Liable Policy Finance US

Wells Fargo has revised its “Online Access Agreement.” The new version takes effect Tuesday, Nov 24, 2026.[1] Three sentences matter for AI. If a customer uses a third-party AI tool or agent: “requests submitted through the tool may be treated as if they came directly from you.”[1] More generally, anyone who gives others access is “responsible for actions taken through that access,” including with the bank's own AI tools.[1] And the bank reserves the right “to restrict or block access from certain third-party AI tools.”[1]

Customer gives AI agent access
→
Agent acts in the account
→
Requests count as the customer's
→
Bank may block the tool
AI agent in a bank account: A program that logs in with your credentials and gets things done on its own: reading your balance, paying bills, moving money. Like a power of attorney for an acquaintance who goes to the bank. Whatever the acquaintance signs counts as signed by you.

American Banker also reports that the customer is supposed to prove when they revoked an agent's access. They remain liable if they cannot prove the revocation.[2] Whether this applies only to personal customers or also to business accounts, the sources describe differently.

Citi takes a different path. Naveed Anwar of Citi said in October: “As of right now, no one is connecting to Citi agentically.”[3] The bank first wants a “know your agent” process: it wants to know who is behind an agent before it gets in.[3]

Note One digest attributed to Citi a clause making customers liable for agents. We could not confirm that claim. The clause is documented only at Wells Fargo. We read the Wells Fargo page; we saw the American Banker article only in a search summary.

My take: This is the most important item of the day, because it carries the question from yesterday's BGH ruling into the bank. Banks are not building an agent brake into the technology but into the contract. The risk shifts to the customer. For a company this means: every access an employee gives an AI tool is a power of attorney. And hardly any Mittelstand firm knows how many of these are open right now. We did not search for a German bank with a similar clause. That would be the next question.

European Commission: Attack, AI Tool Under Suspicion Security Public EU

The European Commission's HR systems have been disrupted for more than ten days. Affected are Sysper (payroll, working time, leave), MyPMO (reimbursement of medical costs) and the translation service.[5] According to Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier, the first signs appeared on Oct 22.[4] The Commission speaks of an “AI-generated cyber campaign” without naming a tool. According to Politico, quoted by The Record, CERT-EU established the use of ARTEX, an open AI platform for attack testing.[4] The FBI warned, per the same report, that with ARTEX one can go “from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours.”[4]

Pentest tool: A program that security testers use to find gaps in a system before an attacker does. Like a locksmith who tests whether your front door opens. Here a language model runs the test itself. The same tool in the wrong hands is a burglar's kit.
Unproven None of the sources says whether ARTEX helped with the break-in itself. Nobody names an attacker. The Commission has not confirmed whether payroll or health data was exfiltrated. We know the dates of a CERT-EU warning (Nov 5) and an FBI warning (Nov 13) only from a search summary.

My take: The technology is covered in the software brief. What counts here is the use-case side: the European Commission's administration has not been able to run normal payroll and leave management for more than ten days. If AI makes attacks cheaper, the pressure rises on everyone who leaves access open. It is the same theme as Wells Fargo, from the other side.

Harvard: AI Makes the Big Ones Bigger, on the Stock Market Study US

Harvard Business School presents a working paper on Nov 18 (Working Paper 26-083).[6] The researchers studied 2,596 US firms. “Superstars” are the top 10% by adjusted Tobin's Q. They measure how exposed a firm is to generative AI through the occupations of its employees (data from Revelio Labs).[6] Result: strongly AI-exposed superstars were 19 points ahead of comparable firms one year after the launch of ChatGPT. After two years the gap had “doubled to 39 points.”[6] Before ChatGPT this advantage did not exist. Firms with a lot of general software but no GenAI exposure trailed by 9.8 points.[6]

Tobin's Q: A firm's market value divided by what its assets would cost to buy new. A high value means the market expects much more from the firm than what sits in its machines and buildings. Like a restaurant worth more than its kitchen and chairs because the name draws crowds.
Methodology What is measured is stock returns, not productivity and not profit. The study shows a correlation, not a cause. “AI exposure” means many occupations that GenAI can affect, not that this firm actually uses AI. US firms only. The article does not say who paid for the study. A working paper, published without peer review.

My take: The study says something about expectations, not about work. The market believes that the big firms with good data and lots of money will use AI best. That fits yesterday's McKinsey survey: adoption is rising, operating profit stays flat. For the Mittelstand the lesson is still serious. If the gap on the stock market doubles, it will soon show up in purchasing, sales and wages too.

Netherlands: Many Users Like AI, Just Not on the Service Line Study Consumer Retail NL

On Nov 17, Deloitte presented the “Digital Consumer Trends” for the Netherlands, with 2,000 respondents aged 18 to 75. Among respondents who use generative AI, weekly use rose from 52% to 58%.[7] At the same time, 58% of respondents familiar with GenAI say they are less likely to use customer service when an AI answers.[7]

The same day, the photo retailer Cameranu reports, according to Emerce: its assistant “Flits” handles a quarter of customer contacts. About 60% of those it resolves without humans. For order-status questions the success rate rose from about 40% to 67%, and response time fell from 6 to 3 seconds.[8]

Sources The Deloitte figures come from Deloitte's own page. They are stated attitudes of respondents, not measured behaviour. We know the Cameranu report only from the English digest “AI Pulse Daily Brief.”[9] We could not open the Emerce article. The Cameranu figures are the company's own claims, with no outside audit.

My take: The two figures only seem to contradict each other. People like using AI when they ask it themselves. They dislike it when a company puts it between them and the humans. Cameranu shows the good path: narrow questions like “where is my parcel” go to the AI, the rest to humans. Whoever builds a bot as a wall loses customers. Whoever builds it as a shortcut gains time.

Belastingdienst: 100 Algorithms, Three Audited Reg Public NL

The Dutch data protection authority AP presented its report on the tax administration on Nov 11. According to the digest, 10 of 12 areas examined are deficient. The authority counts about 100 algorithms used to select cases for audit. By August 2026 only three of them had been audited. The Belastingdienst wants to audit all of them by the end of 2027.[10]

Sources We could not open the PDF report (access blocked). The figures come from the digest. That the report appeared on Nov 11 is confirmed by a letter to the Dutch parliament.

My take: That almost all selection algorithms of a large agency are unaudited shows that an inventory is harder than a new model. This is exactly the inventory the AI Act demands from December 2027 for high-risk systems.

Banco do Brasil: 2,300 Agents, Its Own Math Deploy Finance BR

Not new, but the fitting counterpart to Wells Fargo: Banco do Brasil's president, Tarciana Medeiros, said on Oct 7 at the IBGC congress that the bank operates “cerca de 2,3 mil agentes de Inteligência Artificial.” Their efficiency over twelve months is “equivalente ao trabalho de 20 mil funcionários.”[11] Back in July the bank reported that an agent cut the time for foreign-exchange transactions by 90%. A human still does the final check.[12]

Methodology The “20,000 employees” are the bank's own estimate, not an outside audit. How it was calculated is unknown. We know the 90% only from a search snippet.

My take: Here the bank builds the agents itself and keeps control. At Wells Fargo the agents come from outside, and the bank pushes the liability away. Both are sensible answers. For customers this means: the bank's agent is safer than your own agent in your bank account, at least contractually.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Agent Authorization Register: Who Gave Which AI Tool Which Access?

ELI5 & Value: In a company of 30 people, everyone connects their own tools: a chat assistant to the mailbox, an n8n flow to accounting, a browser agent to the bank portal. Each of these connections is a power of attorney. Wells Fargo shows where this is heading: the customer is responsible for what happens through an access they granted, and according to American Banker must prove when they revoked it.[1][2] twiceD runs an audit: a list of all agent accesses, who is responsible for them, what they may do, and a log for revoking. Like a key book at the caretaker's: who has which key, since when, and who returned it. Value: fewer open doors after cases like the European Commission's, and proof when a bank or insurer asks.
Prototype (V0): (1) Interview with management and IT: which accounts and programs are critical (bank, accounting, mailbox, customer data)? (2) Read the granted OAuth accesses automatically, via the admin interfaces that already exist: Microsoft Graph for Microsoft 365, the Admin SDK for Google Workspace. (3) Everything into a simple table: agent, account, permissions, responsible person, last use. (4) A rule for each access: keep, restrict or revoke, and log the revocation with a date. MVP deliberately narrow: read and assess only, no blocking tool of its own, no bank portals via script.
Market & Gap: For large companies this already exists. Microsoft Entra Agent ID gives AI agents their own identity with owner, sponsor and log. But the security features for it need a Microsoft Agent 365 license, included in Microsoft 365 E7 or as an add-on to E5 and Business Premium.[18] Alongside it sits a whole “non-human identity” market with vendors such as CyberArk, SailPoint, Silverfort, Aembit and Delinea. Polaris Market Research estimates it at $10.94 billion in 2025 and $29.61 billion by 2034.[19] These tools are built for corporations with their own security department and assume that all agents are listed in one directory. Gap for twiceD: the Mittelstand firm with a mixed landscape (some Google, some Microsoft, a bank portal, n8n) has nobody who even makes the list in the first place and explains it in its own language. The audit is the first step before such a tool pays off.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High. An audit is consulting with little maintenance: 3–5 days per client, of which one day is reading and four days are conversations and report. The script for reading is small and the same for every client, solo in 1–2 weeks. Ongoing load: one review per quarter as a maintenance contract, about half a day per client. Scaling via a contractor who runs the audits from a template. Limit: agents that log in through a browser with a password are invisible to any interface. Only conversation finds them. Honestly open: we did not find a German bank with a Wells Fargo clause, because we did not search for one. Today the sales pressure comes more from security and insurance than from the bank contract. wellsfargo.com · learn.microsoft.com · polarismarketresearch.com
