Texas: The AI Detector as an Exhibit Public Legal US

In United States v. Khalwinder Bhengura in federal court in the Northern District of Texas, prosecutors ran the defense's briefs through Pangram, a commercial AI detector. They filed the results as exhibits.[1] Their conclusion: "virtually nothing of substance in these filings seems to have been drafted by a human." They spoke of "defense by ChatGPT" and argued the government does not have to respond to such arguments on the merits.[1]

The defense lawyers do not deny using AI. They cite a medical emergency, after which attorney Mario Herrera had only four days to prepare. Herrera stresses that the prosecutors found "no false citations." Judge Mark Pittman was nonetheless "disturbed" by briefs that "subtly bend" the law.[1]

AI detector: A program that reads a text and estimates how likely it is that an AI wrote it. It does not compare against a database; it scores style, word choice and sentence structure. Like a handwriting analyst who infers the writer from the handwriting: often right, but never proof.

Pangram itself cites a false-positive rate of "1 in 10,000" and an accuracy of "over 99%." By its own account, the tool also shows which parts are "AI-edited," meaning revised by an AI.[3]

Sources & Methodology We know the case from the aiweekly.co digest, which cites 404 Media.[1][2] We did not read the 404 Media article or the court filings ourselves. The Pangram figures are vendor claims, measured on public datasets, not on legal briefs.

My take: This is the most important story of the day, because it shows where the dispute over AI-written text is heading. The question is no longer "may you use AI?" but "who proves what a human reviewed?" The prosecution uses a probability as a weapon, and the defense has nothing in hand except "we have no false citations." Even an error rate of 1 in 10,000 helps little when the detector only says "AI-edited" and every lawyer today uses AI for revising. Anyone who uses AI therefore needs their own proof of how the text came about. That leads to the twiceD idea below.

University of California: ChatGPT for Everyone, Price Unknown Deploy Public US

On Nov 19, the University of California announced an expanded collaboration with OpenAI. Students, faculty and staff get "ChatGPT and Codex," and "at no cost to individuals, with clear guardrails."[4] According to the announcement, the system has more than 300,000 students and 265,000 faculty and staff. It does not say how many of them will use the access.[4] The university promises "stronger data protections" and stresses that it does not mandate use "in or outside the classroom."[4]

What's Missing No contract value, no term. The announcement only mentions "lower costs for UC campuses," with no figure. The page does not say whether OpenAI may train on the university's data. Read on the university's own site.

My take: For OpenAI this is a customer channel for the next twenty years: people who study with a tool buy it later at work. For the university it is convenient, but a contract with no public price and no clear training rule is a dependency you only notice at renewal. German universities, which lean toward their own interfaces with rented models, have the better argument on sovereignty but the weaker one on convenience. Codex is a coding tool; the technical side belongs in the ai-dev brief.

Gallup: A Third of Young People Have an AI Companion Study Consumer Global

Gallup, with the Digital Wellness Lab at Boston Children's Hospital, surveyed young people aged 15 to 22 in 16 countries.[5] According to 404 Media, "33% … they had an AI companion."[5] According to a digest, the share rises to 50% among daily AI users. People who use AI as a companion have 70% higher adjusted odds of feeling lonelier. The study cannot say which direction the effect runs.[6] And around 93% have at least one close friendship in real life.[5]

Median across countries: You compute a share for each of the 16 countries, sort them and take the middle value. That is not "a third of all young people worldwide," but "in a typical country in the survey, about a third."
Methodology We did not open the Gallup page itself. The figures come from 404 Media (Nov 18) and from digests. Digests give the sample as 15,925 people, 404 Media as "more than 16,000." Fieldwork ran from May to August 2026. According to search summaries, the survey ran online in 14 of the 16 countries without a random sample. The 50% and 70% figures are not in the 404 Media text.

My take: This is not a panic number, but it is a market number. Anyone building chatbots for customers will increasingly be talking to people who already have a relationship with AI. For companies that means a bot is no longer read as a form but as a conversation partner, with every expectation about tone and honesty. For parents, the good news is the 93%: the AI friend mostly does not replace the real one.

AI Act: 11 Days to Dec 2 Reg EU

The AI Omnibus package appeared in the EU Official Journal on July 24, 2026 and has applied since July 27.[7] On Wed, Dec 2, 2026, two things happen. First, new bans apply to AI that generates non-consensual intimate content or child sexual abuse material: "These prohibitions will take effect on December 2, 2026."[7] Second, the transition period for labeling under Art. 50 ends for systems that were on the market before Aug 2, 2026.[7] The high-risk rules are postponed: Annex III to Dec 2, 2027, Annex I to Aug 2, 2028.[7]

Art. 50 (labeling): Anyone offering an AI that generates images, audio or text must mark that output as AI content in a machine-readable way. Anyone talking to a chatbot must know it is not a human. Like the "contains sweetener" label on a soda.
Source Law-firm source (Garrigues), not EUR-Lex. New since yesterday: nothing. The details were here on Nov 18 to 20.

Bunnings: 230 IT Jobs Gone, but Not Because of AI Work Retail AU

The Australian hardware chain Bunnings is cutting "roughly 230 permanent roles" in IT, according to ABC. 120 contractor agreements end. About 500 people remain in the in-house IT team in Australia. Tata Consultancy Services takes over the rest, with teams in Australia, India and the Philippines. The transition runs from December to June 2027.[8]

Headline Correction A digest frames the case as "AI-era outsourcing."[9] The ABC article itself does not mention AI. We read ABC directly.

My take: Not every IT job cut is an AI case. This one is classic offshoring to a large service provider. Anyone who counts such cases as proof of AI job losses makes the debate on jobs and AI worse, not better. I am including it because the headline is circulating right now.

Itaú Buys Its Own Model Lab M&A Finance BR

For context, not new: on Oct 26, Itaú Unibanco announced it will buy all shares of NeoSpace A.I. Technologies. The price: "BRL 2,925,000,000.00," part at closing, the rest over five years.[10] NeoSpace builds "foundational models trained on large-scale datasets" and has worked for about two years for Itaú using the bank's own data. CADE and the central bank still have to approve.[10]

Foundation model: A large base model trained on a great deal of data and then adapted for many tasks. Here: trained on bank data instead of the open internet.

My take: A bank is paying almost three billion reais to own its models instead of renting them. That is the counterpart to the university in California: whoever holds the data at the core of the business wants the model in-house. It is too big for mid-sized companies, but the pattern holds on a small scale too.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Provenance Log: Your Own Proof Instead of a Stranger's Detector Guess

ELI5 & Value: Law firms, expert-witness offices and engineering firms use AI for drafting and revising. The Texas case shows the risk: the other side runs the text through a detector and says "no human wrote this." The defense could only answer that no citations were false.[1] A provenance log turns this around. While the work is being done, it records which model suggested which section, which sources were checked and which human signed off. Like a site diary on a construction site: whoever asks later who laid which wall gets a file instead of a guess. Value: less argument over credibility, proof for liability insurers and clients, and a clean answer when a client asks "did you do this with AI?".
Prototype (V0): (1) All of the office's AI calls run through a small proxy that logs time, model, user and document reference. Building block: an open LLM proxy such as LiteLLM, no custom build. (2) The document's version history (Word track changes or a Git repository for text) is linked to this log. (3) A review step checks every citation and reference against the source and flags open points. (4) At the end, a script generates a one-page PDF: sections, AI share, sources checked, sign-off by person X on date Y. The MVP is deliberately narrow: one office, one document type (e.g. a legal brief or an expert report), no detector function, no legal advice on admissibility.
Market & Gap: The opposing side is well staffed. Pangram, GPTZero and Originality.ai estimate from the outside whether a text came from AI. Pangram cites a false-positive rate of "1 in 10,000" and, by its own account, also detects AI-edited parts.[3] For images and video, C2PA is an open provenance standard, "Content Credentials," backed by Adobe, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI, among others.[14] Gap for twiceD: detectors give a probability to the accuser, and C2PA targets media files. For the small office with Word documents and mixed AI tools, our search found no simple protocol that documents its own work from the inside, in German and with data kept in-house. Honestly open: whether German law-firm software (such as the big legal-practice programs) already plans such a feature, we did not check today. That is the first question before any offer.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): Medium to high. The proxy and the PDF script can be built solo in 2–3 weeks, because the heavy parts (proxy, version history) already exist. Rollout per office takes 2–3 days, mostly for habits: people must use AI through the proxy instead of in their own browser tab. That is exactly the weak point; anyone working outside it is missing from the log. Ongoing load: updates when an AI provider changes its interface, about half a day a month across all customers combined. On-prem variant on a small server in the office, the obvious route for client data. Scaling through a contractor who does rollouts from a template. No large team is needed as long as twiceD does not promise its own detector or legal-review function. pangram.com · c2pa.org · aiweekly.co
