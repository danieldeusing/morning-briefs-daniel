AI Act: In 10 days, AI content must carry a label Reg EU

The AI Omnibus package appeared in the EU Official Journal on 07/24/2026 and has applied since 07/27.[2] It reshuffled the AI Act's deadlines. Two things happen on Wed 12/02/2026. First, a transition period ends: systems that were on the market “before August 2, 2026” must label their generated content as synthetic by 12/02.[1] Second, new prohibitions apply to AI that generates non-consensual intimate content or depictions of child abuse: “These prohibitions will take effect on December 2, 2026.”[1]

Important for planning: according to Garrigues, the remaining transparency obligations under Art. 50 have applied since 08/02/2026, with no transition.[1] The high-risk rules are postponed, for Annex III “from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027”, and for AI in regulated products (Annex I) to 08/02/2028.[2]

Art. 50 (labeling): Anyone who offers an AI that generates text, images or audio must mark the output as AI content in a machine-readable way. It's like an invisible watermark in a banknote: the eye can't see it, but the checking device can. Anyone talking to a chatbot must also be told it is not a human.
Sources Two law firms, not EUR-Lex. We read Garrigues ourselves today; it contains the prohibition date and the transition period.[1] Lewis Silkin confirms the transition period (“compliance required by 2 December 2026”) and the high-risk dates, but gives no date for the new prohibitions.[2] We do not cite a regulation number because we only know it from a search summary.

My view: The prohibitions hit almost no ordinary company. The labeling, however, hits many that don't realize it: the agency with its own image generator for clients, the software house with a text feature in its own product, the online shop that creates product photos with AI and passes the tool on to retailers. Anyone who offers such a system themselves is quickly considered a provider under the law. Ten days are not enough for a clean project, but they are enough for an inventory. Whether a company is a provider or only a user must be settled by a law firm case by case; this is not legal advice.

Medicare WISeR: AI review in everyday use, with bumps Deploy Health US

For context, not new: since early 2026, Medicare, the US health insurance for older people, has been testing a model called WISeR. In six states, six companies use AI “to help human clinicians determine whether Medicare will cover select services”.[3] On 11/02, Nextgov analyzed internal documents. Four companies are named: Genzeon (New Jersey), Virtix Health (Washington), Zyter (Arizona) and Innovaccer (Ohio).[3]

The numbers come from a vendor. At Virtix Health, “63.7% of requests” pass the first review. After resubmission by the practice, the share rises to 84.3%. According to Virtix, the average processing time fell to 1.18 days.[3] The agency CMS itself is blunt. CMS deputy administrator Kimberly Brandt says: “There are a lot of things about WISeR that are not working.” And: “I don't have an answer for you in terms of what's going to fix it.”[3]

Prior authorization: Before a doctor performs an expensive treatment, they ask the insurer: will you pay for this? The insurer checks the request against its rules. With WISeR, an AI reads the request first, and a human decides. Like a case worker who gets a pre-sorter.
Methodology The rates and the 1.18 days are self-reported by Virtix Health. A CMS spokesperson stresses that “not confirmed” does not mean “denied”, and that the data do not show the current state.[3] In Congress, the Appropriations Committee passed an amendment against funding in June; it is not law. We know this only from a search summary.[4]

My view: WISeR is an honest test because the agency states its problems openly. The lesson also applies to German health insurers, other insurers and any company that reviews applications. First: a good model helps little if the applicants' documents are incomplete. The jump from 63.7% to 84.3% after resubmission shows this; the problem often lies in the files, not in the AI. Second: anyone who uses AI to decide on money for people is under political pressure, even if a human signs off. Anyone introducing something like this needs an auditable trail from day one showing why an application got stuck.

57% use it, 22% scale it: two numbers, two questions Study DE Global

Bitkom reports on 09/14/2026: “For the first time, with 57 percent, the majority of companies use AI.” A year earlier it was 36%. 603 companies with 20 or more employees were surveyed by phone, in calendar weeks 28 to 33.[5] Gartner paints a very different picture. Only 22% of companies have “successfully scaled … across multiple business units” AI or adopted an AI-first approach. According to a search summary, 1,303 people from companies with at least US$ 50 million in revenue were surveyed, from January to April 2026.[6]

Methodology The numbers measure different things. Bitkom asks “do you use AI?”; often it is enough that individual employees use a tool. Gartner asks about a rollout across multiple areas, and Gartner itself defines what “successful” means. Both are self-reports. We read the Bitkom release today. The Gartner page returned an error (403); the 22% comes from a search summary. Older reports still give 41% for Bitkom; that was an earlier survey.

My view: The gap between “use” and “scale” is the business of the next two years. Most companies have a chat access or a pilot project. What is missing is the second and third use case, connected to real data and real processes. That is exactly where small service providers have work: not yet another workshop about possibilities, but a process that runs in production and is measured. The Gartner number, however, is a large-company number; for the German Mittelstand with 50 employees it says little.

What this means for the week

Three patterns carry the quiet week. First: the calendar drives more than the technology. 12/02 is the only hard deadline, and it hits companies that put AI content out to the world. Second: where AI helps decide on money for people, as with WISeR, the problem often lies in the documents and in traceability, not in the model. Third: “we use AI” is now the majority; “we have rolled out AI in operations” is not. For a small service provider, the second sentence is the market.

Project ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Art. 50 labeling check: Does our AI content carry the label?

ELI5 & Value: Many small companies generate AI content for clients: agencies images, software houses text inside their own product, shop operators product photos. From 12/02/2026, older systems of this kind must mark their outputs as AI content in a machine-readable way.[1] The check answers three questions: Where in the company does AI content arise that goes outside? Does each piece carry a marking that a checking tool can read? And is there proof of it? Like a vehicle inspection appointment for a car: you don't go because you want a new car, but because you need the sticker. Value: avoided risk with regulators and clients, and a clear answer when a large client's procurement asks “are you AI Act compliant?”.
Prototype (V0): (1) A questionnaire and a short conversation capture every point where AI content leaves the company (product, website, client delivery). (2) A script checks sample files from each point for a readable marking. Building block: the open tools of the C2PA standard for images and videos, no custom technology.[13] (3) For text outputs, it checks whether a label exists in metadata or in the interface. (4) The result is a two-page report: point, status, next step, with a traffic light. MVP deliberately narrow: Art. 50 only, no high-risk assessment, no legal advice. The question “are we a provider?” is settled by a partner law firm.
Market & Gap: AI Act compliance software exists. A vendor blog gives rough prices of “EUR 5,000-30,000/year (focused tools) to EUR 100,000+/year (enterprise GRC)”. These tools list Art. 50 as a module for “Evidence of disclosures”, that is, as an evidence folder.[12] For the marking itself, C2PA is an open standard, “like a nutrition label for digital content”, backed by Adobe, Google, Microsoft, OpenAI and Meta, among others.[13] Gap for twiceD: the software collects evidence, but it does not build in the marking and does not check files. The standard exists, but nobody adapts it for the office with ten people. A fixed price for a check of a few days is far below an annual license. Honestly open: we do not know how many small German companies are really providers under Art. 50. The price range is the blog's estimate, not a market study.
Feasibility (Solo + AI): High for the check, medium for the implementation afterwards. Questionnaire, check script and report template can be built solo in about a week because the checking tools are open. One check then takes 1–2 days per company and has no ongoing load. The implementation (building the marking into a product) is normal software-project business, well handled with a contractor. Honest catch: before 12/02 you will hardly sell this anymore. The real market is the companies that missed the deadline, and suppliers whose large clients ask in procurement. No large team needed. legiscope.com · c2pa.org · garrigues.com
