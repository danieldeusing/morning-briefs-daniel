KIMVG: AI in Asylum Procedures, and the Fight over Data Policy Public DE

On 07/29/2026 the federal cabinet approved the draft AI Migration Administration Act. According to the federal government, the goal is to make asylum and residence procedures “more efficient,” more uniform and safer.[2] Two points are at the core. First, programs may “analyze procedural workflows and decision patterns.” Applicants' statements may be compared with publicly available internet information, but only “if there are justified doubts that the statements are correct.”[2] Second, the authorities are to be allowed to use personal data from these procedures “to develop systems with artificial intelligence and other automated programs.”[2] The government stresses that review and decision remain “entirely the responsibility” of its staff.[2]

On Monday, 11/23/2026, at 2 p.m., the Interior Committee held a hearing on it (Drucksache 21/8275).[1] Written statements came from, among others, the Society for Civil Rights (Gesellschaft für Freiheitsrechte), the German Institute for Human Rights, Caritas, the Paritätische, the municipal umbrella associations, the Hessian interior ministry and Prof. Alexander Steen for the German Informatics Society.[1] We do not yet know what was said there. In the evening heise ran the headline: “Experts fear more lawsuits instead of shorter procedures.”[4] We have not read the article; the committee page shows only the names, no content.

The criticism is older and loud. On 11/16, 37 civil-society organizations published an appeal. netzpolitik.org reports: the draft creates “far-reaching AI powers” without defining concrete rules, transparency and clear accountability.[3] “Large amounts of data on persecution history, sexual orientation and gender identity” could be processed, plus origin, religion and disability. A binding, independent evaluation is missing. Steen calls the draft “ill-conceived,” another critic warns of the “human as a fig leaf for the machine.”[3]

Source situation The government side is self-description; the appeal comes from advocacy groups that oppose the draft. We have read both. Neutral figures are missing: we know neither which concrete systems are planned nor what they cost. The government side names no authority.
Automation bias: People's tendency to believe a computer's suggestion because it comes from the computer. Like a driver who follows the GPS into the lake even though he sees the water. If a caseworker signs off 200 files a day and the AI is almost always right, at some point he stops really checking. Then “the human decides” is in the law, but no longer in daily practice.
“Developing” with data: An AI learns from examples. Anyone who wants to build a system for asylum files needs real asylum files as learning material. It is like a cooking apprentice practicing in a stranger's kitchen with the guests' diaries. That is why the question of which data gets released for this is the real dispute.

My take: The most important line in the draft is “the human decides.” Almost everyone writes this sentence into their AI rollout, the private sector included. Almost nobody measures whether it is true. In asylum decisions that is a fundamental-rights problem. At an insurer or in invoice checking it is a liability problem. The gap the critics name is a measurement gap: how often does the human contradict the machine? The twiceD idea below comes from that.

KPMG: Almost Everyone Finds AI Important, Hardly Anyone Is Done Study DE B2B

For the study “Generative AI in the German Economy 2026,” KPMG surveyed 480 decision-makers.[8] 97% say AI is relevant to their business model. 79% expect a major impact on their industry. But “fewer than 1%” have fully integrated AI into their processes. Only 33% manage trustworthy AI company-wide. 82% say the AI Act strongly affects their use of AI. And 84% consider their workforce well prepared.[8]

Methodology The study page gives no publication date (only “© 2026”), no survey period and no method. These are self-reports from decision-makers. KPMG sells AI consulting; a gap between “relevant” and “integrated” is also a sales argument. The figures are a mood reading, not a measurement.

Set beside the two studies of recent weeks, a picture emerges. Each study asks a different question, so they do not contradict each other:

StudyQuestionResult
Bitkom, 09/14/2026 (n = 603)Does the company use AI?57% yes (previous year 36%)[9]

Bitkom and PwC are carried over from recent issues; the PwC figure comes only from search snippets (the page returned 403).[10]

My take: The most interesting value is the contradiction within the KPMG study. 84% consider their staff well prepared, but under 1% are done. If people are ready and the company still does not arrive, the problem lies in the processes, not in the heads. That matches PwC. The 82% on the AI Act show: regulation steers decisions more than technology does.

What Google Tells Us about Its Customers Deploy Finance DE BR

The only named deployments with figures that we find this week are three months old and come from the seller. On 08/27/2026 Google Cloud compiled case studies from the financial industry.[11] The German insurer SIGNAL IDUNA rolled out Gemini Enterprise to 11,000 employees and reached “more than 80% monthly active usage” in under two months. A “Health Agent” checks insurance coverage against old tariff data for the service team; customers get their answer “37% faster,” according to Google.[11]

From Brazil, Google names the credit fintech Core AI from São Paulo. It helps companies launch their own credit programs in 25 days, instead of “a market average of around 300.” An agent reviews lease contracts in under 15 seconds.[11]

Vendor claims All figures are self-reports in a Google promotional text dated 08/27/2026 and thus outside our seven-day window. There is no baseline measurement, no method and no independent check. We show them as an example of the kind of claim, not as evidence.

My take: “80% monthly usage” is a usage figure, not an impact figure. It says how many people log in, not what it brings the company. That is exactly the gap PwC and KPMG describe. The “37% faster” comes closest to an effect, but without a method it remains advertising.

AI Act: 8 Days Left Reg EU

On Wed 12/02/2026 the transition period ends for the machine-readable labeling of AI content in systems that were already on the market before August 2026. On the same day the ban on “nudifier” apps takes effect.[5] The AI Omnibus package appeared in the Official Journal on 07/24/2026 and has applied since 07/27/2026.[6] The high-risk rules come later: for standalone systems “from 2 December 2027,” for AI in regulated products on 08/02/2028.[5] We explained the date dispute over a law-firm table that supposedly gives 2027 for labeling in yesterday's issue. We keep planning with 2026. We found no Commission guidelines on labeling.

One detail fits the asylum debate. Art. 14 requires that, for high-risk AI, the people providing oversight remain aware of the tendency to rely too much on the AI, “(automation bias).”[7] So the term is already in the law. It becomes mandatory only from 12/02/2027.[7]

High-risk AI: Systems that help decide important things in a person's life, for example credit, job, education or residence. The strictest rules of the AI Act apply to them. As with cars: a toy needs no inspection sticker, a bus does.

Brazil: A Free Model from Goiás Open Mfg BR

The AI center of excellence Ceia at the Federal University of Goiás has presented “Ceia Lumi,” a model with 4 billion parameters in Portuguese. The Embrapii announcement dates from 11/09/2026.[12] According to the report, it is based on Nvidia's Nemotron 3 Nano 4B, further trained with around 50 billion tokens. Companies are supposed to be able to run and adapt it on their own servers, for example as an assistant for manuals or maintenance reports. The files are “gratuitamente” downloadable. The license is not entirely clear, though: Ceia uses MIT, while the Nvidia base model has its own terms.[12] There is not yet a deployment in a factory or any measured savings.

Small open model: A language model so small that it runs on a company's own server, and whose files you can download. Like a cookbook you buy instead of ordering from the delivery service every evening. The data never leaves the house. In return, you have to cook yourself.

My take: Anyone who does not want to hand customer data to US providers under the LGPD now has a Portuguese base. The license must be clarified before a customer project.

What This Means for the Week

Three patterns. First: the state is becoming the largest AI user in Germany, and it is doing so in the most sensitive files. The debate about it decides which rules companies will later hear cited as the benchmark. Second: all studies say the same thing in different words. Usage yes, impact hardly, integration almost never. Third: “the human decides” is the most popular safeguard, and the least checked. Whoever can measure it has something to sell.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with a market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The Sign-Off Test: Does the Human Still Check the AI's Suggestion, or Just Nod It Through?

ELI5 & value: Many companies put a human in front of an AI who signs off on every suggestion: the damage classification, the invoice coding, the preliminary check of an application. The critics of the KIMVG call this human a possible “fig leaf for the machine.”[3] The sign-off test measures whether the check is real: how often does the human change the suggestion? How long do they look? Do they find a deliberately wrong suggestion? Like a smoke-detector test: you press the button so you know it will beep in an emergency. Value: the company can back “the human decides” with a number, toward auditors, works council and court. And it sees where sign-off only costs time, because the AI is almost always right there anyway.
Prototype (V0): (1) Pick a workflow in which the AI suggests and a human confirms. (2) Export the existing logs: AI suggestion, final decision, timestamps. No new software in the workflow. (3) Compute three metrics: change rate, mean review time, share of sign-offs within a few seconds. (4) In a test environment with anonymized real cases, mix in 20–30 deliberately wrong suggestions and count how many get noticed. (5) A two-page report with a recommendation. Building blocks: Python with pandas, a notebook or Metabase for the charts, the export of the existing tool. MVP deliberately narrow: one workflow, one company, no model evaluation, no continuous monitoring, no platform.
Market & gap: The market for AI governance platforms is still small: according to Nemko, Gartner expects $492 million in 2026 and over $1 billion by 2030 (forecast from February 2026; the Gartner page itself gave us a 403 error).[17] The known vendors build registries and rulebooks. Credo AI wants to catalog “every AI system, agent, vendor and model” and translate policies into code.[15] Holistic AI tests models for safety and bias and mentions “Approvals, deployment gates and human oversight,” that is, sign-offs for the AI.[16] Both sell to large enterprises, prices on request only. On their home pages we found nothing on how well the humans in the workflow check. The law knows the problem: Art. 14 AI Act names “automation bias” explicitly, mandatory from 12/02/2027.[7] Gap for twiceD: measure the human side, in one workflow, for mid-sized companies, with a method the works council can support. Honestly open: we found no market size for exactly this check, and until the end of 2027 the need is voluntary.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium to high. The evaluation scripts can be built solo in one to two weeks. A project needs about 4–6 days of work spread over three to four weeks. Ongoing load afterwards: none; a repeat every quarter is the natural follow-up engagement. Scaling via one contractor per parallel project. Two honest catches. First: many tools do not store the original AI suggestion separately from the final decision. Then V0 needs a small log adapter, and that costs one more week. Second: measuring review behavior requires co-determination in companies with a works council, because § 87 Abs. 1 Nr. 6 BetrVG covers technical devices intended “to monitor the behavior or performance of employees.”[18] So measure only at team level, never per person, and involve the works council from the start. That fits the works-council data sheet from the 11/15 issue. A large team is not needed. credo.ai · holisticai.com · nemko.com · artificialintelligenceact.eu · gesetze-im-internet.de
