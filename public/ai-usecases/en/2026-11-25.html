KIMVG hearing: two in favor, most against Policy Public DE

On Monday, Nov. 23, 2026, the Bundestag’s Interior Committee held a hearing on the draft (Drucksache 21/8275). Written statements came from seven expert witnesses, plus the municipal associations and the Paritätischer welfare federation.[2] There is no transcript yet. Our source for how it went is a report by Hartmut Gieselmann in heise/c’t from the same evening.[1] The law would allow agencies to use AI in asylum and residence procedures and to develop AI systems with the data from those procedures.

The supporters. Constitutional law scholar Matthias Friehe (EBS) calls the draft a “first step toward modernizing administration.” He considers the intrusions justifiable because humans decide in the end. Joscha Rasch of the Hessian interior ministry says, according to heise: “Given the heavy workload and staff shortages, AI applications were necessary for the immigration offices.” He warns against national rules that go beyond the EU.[1] The municipal associations mainly want centrally provided and funded AI. Individual immigration offices can hardly manage development, operation and legal review on their own.[1]

The critics. Computer scientist Alexander Steen (University of Greifswald, Gesellschaft für Informatik) warns of “excessive trust by caseworkers in automated recommendations.” That a human makes the final decision is “not sufficient as a safeguard.”[1] Franziska Görlitz (GFF) points to the Dutch childcare-benefits scandal, in which discriminatory risk scoring ruined many families. Stricter rules are “not an obstacle but a precondition for the hoped-for efficiency gains.” Without documentation, longer court proceedings loom.[1] Eric Töpfer (German Institute for Human Rights) demands binding fundamental-rights assessments, reports to the Bundestag and an independent evaluation.[1]

What companies can learn here. Yana Gospodinova (Caritas) proposed three concrete checks. First: develop AI systems “preferably with synthetic training data.” Second: targeted tests of whether the system rates “identical cases with different names” differently. Third: caseworkers should form their own judgment before they see the AI’s suggestion, plus independent second reviews.[1]

Synthetic training data: Artificially generated example cases that resemble real cases but belong to no real person. Like practice dummies in first-aid training: you learn the moves without endangering a real patient. The AI learns the pattern, but no real asylum file is opened for it.
Name-swap test: You give the AI the same case twice. Only the name differs, for example “Anna Schmidt” and “Aylin Yılmaz.” If the AI rates the two differently, it has learned something it should not have learned. Like two identical job applications to a bakery that carry different names.

What happens next. There is still no date for the second/third reading. The Bundestag sits in 2026 only “from Dec. 7 to 11 and from Dec. 14 to 18.” If the committee’s recommendation comes too late, the debate continues in 2027.[1] The Bundesrat even wants to expand the automated internet comparison, for example to identity and nationality. It can call the mediation committee, and that would delay the law.[1]

Source situation Everything about the course of the hearing comes from a single report (heise/c’t). The statements themselves are available as PDFs from the Bundestag; we did not read them individually. By our count, two expert witnesses support the draft, four oppose it, and the municipalities are neutral. On the statement by Prof. Sarah Rachut (TU Braunschweig), the report says nothing. This ratio is not a picture of majority opinion in the field.

My take: Both sides agree on one point without saying so: AI in administration will come centrally, not agency by agency. The municipalities want it that way, and with KIPITZ the platform already exists (see right). So the question is no longer “AI yes or no,” but: who audits the central platform? Gospodinova’s three checks are the best answer from the hearing because they are cheap and measurable. They fit an insurer or an HR department just as well. The twiceD idea below grows out of this.

KIPITZ: the government AI already exists Deploy Public DE

One detail from the heise report matters more than the whole debate: “BAMF now uses the central AI platform KIPITZ, which ITZBund operates with about 20 open-weight models.”[1] ITZBund is the federal government’s IT service provider. It also makes the platform available to other agencies. So the federal government runs its own AI on its own servers, with freely available models instead of a US subscription.

Open-weight model: An AI model whose “weights” you may download and run on your own machines. The weights are the knowledge the model has learned. Like a cookbook you buy and use in your own kitchen, instead of ordering from a delivery service every evening. The data never leaves the house.

heise does the math. The AI Act’s high-risk obligations apply only from Dec. 2, 2027, “for systems already deployed by agencies even only from Aug. 2, 2030.” Systems like KIPITZ could thus be operated for almost four years without having to meet the stricter requirements of the AI Act.[1] What KIPITZ concretely does at BAMF today is not in the report.

My take: For Daniel, KIPITZ is the most interesting signal of the week. The state is implementing exactly the model that mid-sized companies are looking for for cost and data-protection reasons: many open models, run centrally, data kept in-house. But the gap until 2030 applies only to agencies. A company that uses AI in recruiting faces the full obligations from Dec. 2, 2027.

CADA: right to terminate government clouds that lack sovereignty Reg EU B2B

The rapporteurs’ draft for the Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA) is now out in the European Parliament. Falk Steiner reports this at heise on Nov. 24, 2026.[4] Lead is Diego Solier (Industry Committee ITRE), together with Reinier van Lanschot (Internal Market Committee IMCO, Volt). The Commission had presented its proposal “in early June.”[4] The key points:

  • Scope: The proposal contains “366 amendments.”[4]
  • Tenders: Strengthening European cloud and AI services is to count “for at least 20 percent” in public tenders.[4]
  • Termination: Public-sector cloud contracts that fail the sovereignty check are to be subject to a special termination right “without a claim to compensation.”[4]
  • Critical companies: Entities under NIS2 are to name their important cloud workloads under Art. 31 and apply a “cloud security level.” There are four levels; from level 3, “things get tight for non-EU companies.”[4]
NIS2: An EU directive that obliges important companies and institutions to improve IT security, for example energy suppliers, hospitals and many larger mid-sized companies. Like a fire-safety code: not every building has to meet it, but hospitals and factories do.
Status This is a draft by two MEPs, not a Parliament position. After that, Parliament, Council and Commission have to agree. heise writes that the text is unlikely to stay unchanged, and that it is open whether the termination right survives.[4] The report gives no number of affected contracts.

My take: The termination right without compensation is radical and will probably be watered down. The 20 percent rule in tenders is quieter but has bigger consequences. It turns “runs in the EU” into a scoring advantage that even a small provider with EU hosting can write into its bid. Anyone selling AI to agencies should have an EU variant ready now.

Health: lots of “game changer,” no number Policy Health DE

At the Bitkom Digital Health Conference in Berlin on Tuesday, Federal Health Minister Carsten Linnemann (CDU) called AI in connection with personalized therapies a “game changer.”[5] He announced the GeDIG law, with regulatory sandboxes for faster innovation; it is also meant to implement the European Health Data Space (EHDS). As examples, heise named the Berlin platform Amboss, with a model for medical expert questions, and the voice assistant Voize for care documentation.[5]

Regulatory sandbox (Reallabor): A permitted test space where a new technology may run under real conditions before all the rules for it are finished. Like a test track for a new car: real road, but with supervision and an end date.

My take: A speech, not a deployment: no budget, no measured effect.[5] Only the sandboxes matter. With the GeDIG, small providers would get a legal way to test AI with real health data. When, is open.

AI Act: 7 days left Reg EU

On Wed, Dec. 2, 2026, the grace period ends for labeling AI content in systems that were already on the market before August 2026. Parliament wrote on June 16, 2026: the labeling obligation is postponed “until 2 December 2026.” For “nudifier” apps: “Companies will have until 2 December 2026 to bring their systems in line.”[6] The AI omnibus package appeared in the Official Journal on July 24, 2026, and has applied since July 27, 2026.[7] The high-risk rules come later: for stand-alone systems “from 2 December 2027,” for AI in regulated products “from 2 August 2028.”[6]

New from the hearing: for high-risk systems that agencies already use, heise even names Aug. 2, 2030.[1] Art. 111(2) AI Act confirms Aug. 2, 2030 for systems placed on the market before Aug. 2, 2026 without substantial modification. We carry it over with a pointer to the source.

High-risk AI: Systems that help decide important things in a person’s life, for example credit, a job or residency. The strictest rules of the AI Act apply to them. Like with cars: a toy needs no inspection sticker, a bus does.

What this means for the week

Three patterns. First: “A human decides” is losing its value as a safeguard. Even lawmakers are now hearing from experts that this sentence alone is not enough. What’s needed are tests you can measure. Second: AI in government is becoming centralized and open, with KIPITZ as the model. Third: Brussels is turning “runs in the EU” into an advantage in tenders. That helps small providers more than large ones.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · The name-swap test: does the recruiting AI rate “Aylin” differently than “Anna”?

ELI5 & value: Many mid-sized companies already let AI pre-sort applications, in the applicant portal or with a chatbot tool. The name-swap test runs the same application through several times, each time with a different name. If the rating changes, the AI sorts by origin or gender. This is exactly the test Caritas proposed in the Bundestag on Monday for asylum files.[1] The risk is real. In a University of Washington study, three AI models preferred résumés with white-sounding US names “in 85.1% of cases,” and female names only “in 11.1% of cases.”[10] Value for the customer: in Germany, anyone who presents indications of discrimination reverses the burden of proof. Then “the other party bears the burden of proof” that there was no discrimination (§ 22 AGG).[11] A documented test beforehand is cheaper than a lawsuit afterward.
Prototype (V0): (1) With the customer, build a real job posting and 20–30 synthetic résumés, without data from real applicants. (2) Copy each résumé into 6–8 name variants (origin, gender), changing nothing else. (3) Run all variants through the customer’s tool, via the test environment or the interface, and note the score or rank. (4) Evaluate differences per group, as a ratio of selection rates as in the New York audit procedure. (5) A two-page report with result and recommendation. Building blocks: an LLM for the synthetic résumés, Python with pandas, and the free tool Fairlearn for the analysis.[14] The MVP is deliberately narrow: one job, one tool, only the name as a variable. No continuous monitoring, no testing of other attributes like age or gaps in the résumé.
Market & gap: In New York the market already exists because the law forces it. Local Law 144 requires “a bias audit performed by an independent auditor” for automated applicant tools, and the results must go on the careers page.[13] Deloitte offers preparation for this; going by its content, the page dates from around 2023.[13] Holistic AI and Credo AI sell governance platforms to large corporations (see the Nov. 24 issue). Germany has no such obligation. But from Dec. 2, 2027, AI for “the recruitment or selection of natural persons” is high-risk AI under Annex III of the AI Act.[12] Gap for twiceD: a fixed, small test for mid-sized companies that buy a ready-made tool and have no data-science department. Honestly open: we found no market size for such tests in Germany. Until the end of 2027 the need is voluntary, driven by AGG risk and by reputation as an employer.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. This is consulting with a reusable set of scripts, not a service in continuous operation. You can build V0 solo in one to two weeks. A customer project costs about 3–5 working days. Ongoing load: none. Follow-up work: a repeat after every update of the tool. Scaling via one contractor per project. Three honest catches. First: not every purchased tool allows test runs. Then it needs the vendor’s cooperation, or the test runs slowly by hand through the interface. Second: the test finds only one kind of bias. The New York audits are criticized for exactly this, that they only measure group differences. Third: a finding is uncomfortable for the customer. The contract has to settle beforehand what happens to the report. The test is not legal advice. arxiv.org · gesetze-im-internet.de · artificialintelligenceact.eu · deloitte.com · fairlearn.org
