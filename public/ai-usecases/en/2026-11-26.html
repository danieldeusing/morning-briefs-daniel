AMPEL: Leipzig Releases Its Hospital AI Deploy Health DE

Leipzig University Hospital has "published the source code of its AMPEL platform on GitHub under the MIT license." Marie-Claire Koch reported this at heise on 2026-11-25.[1] AMPEL is a platform a hospital can use to build its own AI-supported medical devices. "LAMPE, a medical device that has been in routine care at UKL since April 2026, is already based on it."[1] One algorithm ships at launch: "a combined algorithm for acute kidney injury and acute kidney injury under vancomycin therapy."[1]

This is not a chatbot. "UKL describes LAMPE as a rule- and model-based system and distinguishes it from generative AI such as language models."[1] It continuously checks patient data and raises an alarm when it sees a warning pattern.

Clinical decision support: Software that gives doctors hints, such as "the kidney values are starting to slip." The doctor decides. Like a smoke detector: it doesn't put out the fire, but it wakes you up in time.

What the numbers say. UKL has "developed 14 algorithms for different clinical pictures." It reports false-alarm rates per algorithm. For acute kidney injury there are "virtually none." For the refeeding syndrome algorithm, they "are at 32 percent."[1] According to the report, no figures on treatment outcomes have been published, that is, whether patients do better because of the warnings.[1]

Refeeding syndrome: A potentially life-threatening metabolic disorder. It can occur when severely malnourished people start eating normally again. Like an engine that sat empty for a long time and gets revved up too fast.
32% false-alarm rate: Out of 100 warnings, about 32 are unfounded. Like a smoke detector that often beeps while you cook: eventually nobody listens anymore.

The catch. "What was published is not the ready-to-use medical device." The "module that reads data from a hospital information system" is deliberately missing. Each hospital has to build this connection itself and meet "the requirements of the EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR)" for its own product.[1] UKL puts a number on the effort in its wiki: a hospital should assume "at least 24 person-months" if it is the first self-manufactured medical-device software at that site. According to UKL's Daniel Steinbach, anyone with prior experience in in-house medical-device software gets off considerably cheaper.[1]

MDR (Medical Device Regulation): The EU law that governs when a device or software may be used on patients, with proof of safety and risks. Like a vehicle inspection: anyone can build a car, but it only gets on the road with an inspection seal.
Hospital information system (HIS): A hospital's central software holding patients, findings and lab values, customized differently in every house. Like power outlets in different countries: the device (AMPEL) is the same, but everyone needs their own adapter.

Who else uses it. So far it only runs in patient care at UKL. "An implementation was recently started at the university hospitals in Lübeck and Kiel (UKSH)." According to Steinbach, it is likely to take "at least a year" before it is used on patients there.[1] The MIT license "permits use, modification, distribution and sale of the code, but excludes any warranty and liability."[1]

Source situation Everything comes from one report (heise) that relies on UKL and its wiki. The 24 person-months are an estimate by UKL itself, not an independent figure. We did not check the GitHub code. We did not find a press release from UKL.

My take: The most important health-AI story out of Germany in weeks, because it is honest. Leipzig also shows the math: the code is free, the certification costs two person-years. So the bottleneck is not the algorithms but integration and regulation. Hospitals without their own medical informatics team won't manage this alone. More on that in idea 1 below.

Tidal Kicks Out Fully AI-Generated Music Policy Consumer Global

The streaming service Tidal is stemming the flood. "From December 9, such tracks will be rejected and removed from the catalog." This means music generated entirely by an AI. Daniel Herbig reported this at heise on 2026-11-25.[2] Until now, Tidal only "labeled such tracks and excluded them from royalties."[2] The numbers behind the decision:

  • Catalog: About 27 million tracks are flagged as AI music, "which corresponds to 6.3 percent of the music available on Tidal."[2]
  • Nobody listens: "96 percent of them had zero streams in the past three months."[2]
  • New deliveries: Over the last 90 days, Tidal detected 43% of all delivered tracks as fully AI-generated. "On November 6, the share peaked at over 60 percent of deliveries."[2]
  • Timeline: The flagged tracks are due to be gone "by early 2027."[2]

AI-assisted music stays allowed, "as long as it does not come exclusively from an AI tool."[2] At Deezer, "only 1 to 3 percent of streams" go to AI music. "Up to 85 percent of those streams were classified by Deezer as fraudulent in 2025, and it paid no royalties for them."[2]

Source situation All figures are statements by Tidal and Deezer, via heise, with no second source. The report does not say how Tidal detects AI music or how often it gets it wrong.

My take: The interesting number is 43%, not 27 million. Almost every second new track is machine-made, and hardly anyone listens. This is a spam problem, like email around 2005. Tidal chooses the ban, Deezer the label. But the detection itself is an AI, and nobody publishes its error rate.

Pax: Police AI Expands to 230 Cities in Brazil Deploy Public BR

The Brazilian startup Pax lets police officers search approved databases in plain language. According to Exame, it links data "de bases autorizadas, como imagens de câmeras e registros de ocorrências," that is, camera images and incident-report registers.[3] AI agents look for connections and suggest leads. Pax already runs at the police forces of São Paulo, Goiás, Paraná and Ceará. Now it is expanding to 230 cities, "com projetos-piloto na Bahia, em Mato Grosso do Sul e no Distrito Federal."[3] The funding: a $40 million seed round led by Benchmark and Greenoaks, announced in May.[3]

The impact figures come from the company. Pax reportedly contributed to "mais de 7 mil casos entre maio de 2025 e outubro de 2026." In Luziânia (Goiás), the share of solved crimes rose "de 18% para 34%," and crime in the monitored area fell by 27%.[3] Exame itself adds that these values "não permitem determinar quanto da evolução decorreu da plataforma."[3]

Methodology One case, chosen by the company itself, with no comparison city. The article names no criticism on data protection (LGPD); it only describes the company's safeguards, such as a required justification for each query and logs.[3]

My take: The technology is unspectacular: natural-language search across distributed data, like a corporate knowledge assistant. The audit design is what's interesting. Required justification and a log per query are also what KIMVG critics demand in Germany. Whether the clearance rate is due to the AI will only be shown by an independent evaluation.

Deloitte Brazil: Everyone Wants Agents, Hardly Anyone Has Rules Study BR

Exame reports on 2026-11-23 from Deloitte's "State of AI in the Enterprise 2026" report. 3,235 executives in 24 countries were surveyed, 115 of them from Brazil. 95% of Brazilian companies plan to deploy agentic AI within two years. Only 27% have mature governance.[4] According to Exame, Jefferson Denti, Chief Disruption Officer at Deloitte, says there is a race to adopt, but governance isn't keeping pace.[4]

Agentic AI: AI that doesn't just answer but carries out steps itself, such as creating an order or sending an email. Like the difference between a GPS that shows the way and a car that drives itself.
Methodology 115 respondents in Brazil is few; the article names neither the margin of error nor the selection. Deloitte itself sells governance consulting. We did not read the original report.

My take: 95% "plan" is sentiment, 27% "mature" is self-assessment. Only the gap is useful: anyone introducing agents needs rules first for what they are allowed to do (governance).

AI Act: 6 Days Left Reg EU

On Wed 2026-12-02, the transition period ends for labeling AI content in systems that came to market before 2026-08-02. On 2026-06-16, Parliament wrote that the obligation applies "until 2 December 2026." For "nudifier" apps: "Companies will have until 2 December 2026 to bring their systems in line."[5] The AI omnibus package that postponed these deadlines has been in force since 2026-07-27.[6] The high-risk rules apply "from 2 December 2027" for standalone systems and "from 2 August 2028" for AI in regulated products.[5]

My take: For hospital AI, 2028-08-02 matters: the high-risk obligations of the AI Act will then come on top of the MDR obligations.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · AMPEL Feasibility Check: Does the Leipzig Hospital AI Fit Our Hospital?

ELI5 & value: Leipzig gives away the engine, but not the adapter and not the inspection. Before a hospital puts two person-years into certification, it wants to know: do we have the data AMPEL needs? How often would the kidney alarm fire falsely at our site? The check answers this in a few weeks using the hospital's historical data. Value: the hospital decides on an investment of "at least 24 person-months"[1] based on its own numbers instead of a heise article. If the answer is "no," it has saved almost all of those 24 person-months.
Prototype (V0): (1) Run AMPEL from GitHub locally with synthetic test data. (2) Work out with the hospital IT which lab and medication data the HIS exports, and in what format. (3) Build a read adapter only for a pseudonymized export. (4) Run the kidney algorithm retrospectively over one year of data and compare alarms against the actual diagnoses. (5) Report: data gaps, false-alarm rate compared to Leipzig, rough effort for real deployment. The MVP is deliberately narrow: one algorithm, retrospective only, no alerts to doctors, no work on the medical device itself. Whether the retrospective analysis needs clearance from data protection or an ethics committee is for the hospital to settle beforehand. Building blocks: AMPEL (MIT license), the existing HIS's export path, Python for the analysis.
Market & gap: The big players sell closed decision-support software. A MarketsandMarkets report (July 2026) names Siemens Healthineers, Wolters Kluwer, Philips and Oracle, among others, for Germany. It estimates the German market at "$117.9 Million in 2025" and "$187.3 Million by 2030."[8] Caution: the same page gives different totals in a table (188.6 and 300.2). So the number is only good for an order of magnitude. AMPEL is now the open alternative, but apart from Leipzig nobody uses it on patients yet, and in Lübeck/Kiel it takes "at least a year."[1] Gap for twiceD: the step before the decision. Hospitals without their own medical informatics team can neither evaluate nor connect AMPEL today, and the big vendors won't promote free competition. Honestly open: we found no vendor selling exactly this check, and no figure for how many hospitals want to evaluate AMPEL.
Feasibility (solo + AI): Medium. The check is consulting with an end date: V0 of the tooling solo in two to three weeks, two to four weeks of work per hospital, no ongoing maintenance. Three catches. First: hospitals buy slowly; without a contact in a hospital's IT there is no first customer. Second: evaluating the alarms requires a physician at the hospital. Third: twiceD should not take on live operation and the MDR documentation as the manufacturer. That stays with the hospital; twiceD works at most as a supplier within its quality process, with liability questions for a lawyer. heise.de · marketsandmarkets.com

2 · The Sovereignty Bill of Materials: What Is Really Inside the "German" AI?

ELI5 & value: Many German AI vendors advertise "data stays in Germany," but under the hood a US model often runs. The bill of materials shows which models and cloud services a product really uses and where the data flows, like the ingredient list on a package. Value for the vendor: when the CADA draft comes, European vendors get at least 20% weight in public tenders.[7] Those who can prove their sovereignty score. Those who only claim it risk a contract that can be terminated without compensation.[7]
Prototype (V0): (1) Build a template for the bill of materials: model, vendor, headquarters, hosting location, data flow. (2) At the customer, inspect the configuration, dependencies and network traffic of a test run and assign each external call to a vendor. (3) Mark gaps: where a US service sits and which EU or open-weight alternative exists. (4) Write a two-page result for tender documents. Building blocks: network capture, dependency lists. The MVP is deliberately narrow: one product, one result document, no certification and no legal statement.
Market & gap: The need is shown by an analysis from the AI platform GreenPT via t3n (2026-07-13). It examined 514 German AI companies. "According to GreenPT, 173 of the 514 companies examined actively market EU or Germany data residency." "Of these, 72 nonetheless use a proprietary model from the US."[9] Caution: GreenPT is itself a European vendor and has an interest in this result. We did not research competitors for such an audit. Gap for twiceD: a technical, small-scale proof for vendors who want to sell to government agencies and can't pay a large auditing firm. Honestly open: CADA is only a position draft in Parliament; without a law, the need stays voluntary.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High. Pure consulting, close to Daniel's everyday developer work. V0 of the template in one week, two to five working days per customer, no ongoing maintenance apart from a repeat on major updates. Scale via one contractor per project. Catch: without a finished CADA law, selling is hard. t3n.de · heise.de

It's Thursday, November twenty-sixth. Today there's finally a real deployment again, with names and numbers: Leipzig University Hospital is giving away the code of its hospital AI.

The platform is called AMPEL, and it's online under an open license. A system built on it has been warning of acute kidney injury in day-to-day care in Leipzig since April. According to the hospital, there are virtually no false alarms. For another algorithm, aimed at a dangerous metabolic disorder, the false-alarm rate is thirty-two percent. Whether patients actually do better because of it hasn't been published.

Here's the catch: Leipzig is not releasing the building block that reads data from the hospital's information system. Every hospital has to build it itself and get its own medical-device certification. Leipzig estimates at least twenty-four person-months for that, which is two person-years. The code is free. The certification is not.

The streaming service Tidal will remove music made entirely by AI starting December ninth. That affects about twenty-seven million tracks. Nearly half of all newly delivered tracks are now machine-made, and ninety-six percent of the flagged tracks have no listeners at all.

In Brazil, Pax's police AI is expanding to two hundred thirty cities. Officers use it to search camera images and incident reports in plain language. The company reports a clearance rate in one city in Goiás rising from eighteen to thirty-four percent. But the magazine Exame stresses that the effect can't be cleanly attributed to the AI.

A Deloitte survey says almost all surveyed companies in Brazil want AI agents, but only a little over a quarter have clear rules for them. Only one hundred fifteen companies were surveyed, though. On the AI Act, there are six days left until December second. That's when the transition period ends for labeling AI content in older systems.

The first project idea for twiceD comes straight from Leipzig: a feasibility check that lets a hospital find out, before the expensive certification, whether AMPEL works with its own data. The second idea is a sovereignty bill of materials for AI vendors who advertise German data but use US models. Tomorrow we'll see whether anyone confirms the Leipzig numbers, and we'll keep counting down to December second.
