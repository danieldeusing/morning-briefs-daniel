Copyright: Whoever generates the AI content should pay Reg Creative DE

“The federal government is proposing a new compensation claim that applies at the point where AI content is generated.” That is from dpa, published by heise on Fri 11/27/2026 at 8:31 a.m.[1] Payment is due “when their systems produce texts, music or videos that compete with works created by humans.”[1]

How the money flows. “The money is to flow to the rights holders through collecting societies.” There is one condition: “Only rights holders who make their works available free of charge for AI training are to benefit.”[1] This is a trade: training stays free, and the output is paid for instead.

Collecting society: A body that collects and distributes money for many authors together, for example GEMA for music or VG Wort for texts. Like a roommates’ kitty: everyone pays into one pot, and at the end of the month everyone gets their share, without each person having to settle up individually.

What it is, and what it is not. It is not a law. It is “a position paper of the federal government on the EU consultation on copyright.”[1] Justice Minister Stefanie Hubig (SPD) had outlined the approach in a speech “as early as November 9.” On November 20, “Germany and 14 other EU states” called on the Commission to present “a legislative proposal as quickly as possible.” The impetus came from Minister of State for Culture Wolfram Weimer, supported “among others by France, Poland and Spain.”[1]

The timeline. “The EU Commission has completed its consultation but has not yet presented a draft law.” According to its work program, “a legislative package is planned for the second quarter of 2027.”[1]

The government’s own warning. The government sees the risk itself. It “warns that new rules could make AI foundation models more expensive or restrict their availability.”[1]

Source situation One dpa report via heise. We did not read the position paper itself. The article contains no opposing voice, neither from AI providers nor from industry associations.

My take: Targeting the output instead of the training is smart. The training is long done, and nobody can cleanly prove which work ended up where. The output, by contrast, can be counted. The weak point is the word “compete.” Does an AI-written product text in an online shop compete with a copywriter? Whoever decides that decides the bill. For small and mid-sized businesses nothing changes today. If it comes, the levy lands in the API price, in 2027 at the earliest.

Consumer advocates send cease-and-desist letters to OpenAI and Anthropic Consumer Legal DE

The Lower Saxony consumer advice center has sent cease-and-desist letters to OpenAI and Anthropic. Malte Kirchner reports this at heise on Fri 11/27/2026 at 1:13 p.m.[2]

Cease-and-desist letter: A formal written demand to stop a behavior. Anyone who does not respond risks a lawsuit. Like a yellow card in soccer: not yet a red card, but the last warning before one.
  • Anthropic: The accusation is that the company states “subscription prices incompletely.” According to the consumer advice center, “the total price must be stated,” that is, including taxes. The terms reserve the right to “change prices and plans at its discretion.” The legal notice “at no point names a person authorized to represent the company.”[2]
  • OpenAI: In the Plus subscription, the chat and work areas are to be merged. OpenAI wants to “pool the usage quotas of ChatGPT and Codex” and “switch usage metering from message count to tokens.” The consumer advice center calls the consequences “completely unclear.” Users might “have to buy paid extra credits.”[2]
Token: The small chunks of text in which a language model computes, often just part of a word. Like a taxi: before, a flat fare applied per ride (per message), now the meter runs per kilometer (per token). A long question then costs more than a short one.

Advice to users. Affected users should demand “a return to the originally agreed plan.” Digital products may “only be changed for good cause.”[2] It is not the first case. “A lawsuit by the Brandenburg consumer advice center is already pending against OpenAI,” over difficult cancellation and “a unilateral price adjustment clause.” The company had “already received a cease-and-desist letter in July 2026” and gave “no binding declaration to cease and desist.”[2]

Open The article names no deadline for a response. It contains no reaction from OpenAI or Anthropic. This is about consumer subscriptions. We did not check the terms for business contracts and the API.

My take: This is a consumer story, but it hits small companies just the same. Many businesses with ten people have no business account. They simply hand out Plus subscriptions to their staff. For them, the switch to tokens means: the cost of AI will soon depend on the length of the work, not on the number of questions. More on that in the idea below.

Signal blocks screenshots against AI agents Privacy Consumer EU/UK

“On iOS 27, Signal blocks screenshots across the board.” On Android, “the block kicks in as soon as an AI assistant tries to access the screen content.” Stefan Krempl reports this at heise on Fri 11/27/2026 at 6:04 p.m.[3] According to the article, AI assistants from Apple, Google and Microsoft could “pull messages, photos and emails straight off the screen.”[3]

AI agent in the operating system: An assistant that does not live in a single app but in the phone or PC itself, and can read along with what is on the screen. Like a sealed letter with someone reading over your shoulder as soon as the recipient opens it. Encryption protects the route, not the screen.

The voices. Signal strategist Udbhav Tiwari says billions of devices could permanently spy on their users. He says that would make the Snowden revelations “look like a joke.”[3] Niamh Sweeney of the Irish data protection authority DPC says uninvolved third parties would have “hardly any chance of being informed or objecting.”[3] “Apple, Google and Microsoft declined to comment.”[3]

Meta Muse. Meta launched its AI agent Muse in the UK on Tuesday, 11/24. According to the British data protection authority ICO, Meta committed to a “voluntary monitoring and reporting procedure.” At the end of October, the ICO had warned that such products could “amplify and accelerate risks for consumers.”[3]

My take: Sweeney’s point is the important one for companies. Anyone who chats with customers on a company phone has their data on the screen. If an AI reads along there, the customer never agreed to it. For businesses with managed devices, that means: the question of which AI assistants may read along on the company phone belongs in the device policy. So far it mostly is not there.

The driver. According to the article, IaaS growth is “fueled by the growing production operation of AI models (“inference-first” workloads).” Gartner analyst John-David Lovelock expects spending to “shift toward agent-based systems.” He speaks of the “biggest infrastructure expansion in history.” For server prices, Gartner expects them to “soon reach three times the 2024 level.”[4]

Inference: Using a finished AI model, that is, every single answer it gives. The counterpart is training. Training is writing a cookbook, inference is cooking every day afterward. Gartner is essentially saying: the daily cooking now costs real money.
Methodology A forecast, not a measurement. Gartner sells analyses to exactly the companies that spend this money. The Gartner page itself returned a 403 error to us. All Europe figures therefore come from the heise report. The article gives no figure for AI agents alone.

My take: The headline is the 1.57 trillion. The story is the gap: infrastructure almost 30 percent, IT services 4.6 percent. The money goes into machines, not into people. That fits SAP, which according to the report is cutting jobs and betting on AI. For small service providers, it means: if servers get three times as expensive, every consultant who shrinks the bill pays off. The catch for sovereignty: your own AI hardware in the basement gets more expensive, not cheaper.

Project Ideas for twiceD

Researched proposals — with market gap and honest solo feasibility

1 · Provider exit plan: protect AI features against price shocks

ELI5 & value: Many mid-sized companies have built an AI feature directly on one provider, such as a support assistant or reading delivery notes. If the provider changes price or billing, the company can only pay or rebuild. Exactly such changes are under criticism right now: Anthropic reserves the right to “change prices and plans at its discretion,” and OpenAI is switching metering from messages to tokens.[2] On top of that, Gartner expects server prices soon at “three times the 2024 level.”[4] A gateway between the company and the provider works like a power strip with an electricity meter: you see what each device draws, and you can plug in a different device without rewiring the house. Value for the customer: cost per use case is visible, a switch takes days instead of months, and in price negotiations they have a real alternative.
Prototype (V0): (1) Inventory: Which feature calls which model, under which contract, at what monthly cost? (2) Put a self-hosted open-source gateway in front of the calls, with its own key per use case. (3) Collect 50 to 100 real requests per use case with expected answers as a test set. (4) Run the test set against today’s model and one or two replacement models, for example an EU-hosted or an open model. (5) Set up a fallback rule in the gateway and write a one-page report: cost per case, quality gap, switching effort. MVP deliberately narrow: one use case, no new feature, no running your own models. Building blocks: LiteLLM as the gateway, Cloudflare AI Gateway as a hosted alternative.[10][11]
Market & gap: The tools exist. LiteLLM describes itself as “Open Source AI Gateway for 100+ LLMs. Self-hosted.”, with “spend tracking” and fallback logic across several providers.[10] Cloudflare offers “analytics, caching, rate limiting, and model fallback,” “Available on all plans” according to its docs.[11] The market is moving upmarket: Palo Alto Networks completed its acquisition of the gateway provider Portkey on 2026-05-29, “a pioneer in AI Gateways.”[12] Gateways thereby become part of large security platforms for enterprises. Gap for twiceD: The tool is not the problem. What is missing is someone who, at a company with 200 people, does the inventory, builds the test set, proves the quality gap and reads the contract. Honestly open: We found no market size for this segment. Which consultancies already offer this in Germany, we did not research. And the cease-and-desist letter concerns consumer subscriptions, not API contracts. Whether business contracts have similar clauses must be checked per customer.
Feasibility (solo + AI): High as a project, medium as ongoing operation. The building blocks are ready. twiceD builds the test set, configuration and report. A project takes solo about two to three weeks per use case. If twiceD then runs the gateway itself, updates, monitoring and incidents come on top, roughly two to four hours per week per customer. Recommendation: build, hand over, and offer a maintenance contract only on request. Scale through one contractor per new customer. Sovereign variant: the gateway runs on the customer’s server, the replacement is an open model. Because of rising server prices, however, this variant gets more expensive.[4] github.com/BerriAI/litellm · developers.cloudflare.com · paloaltonetworks.com · heise.de

It’s Saturday, November twenty-eighth. Friday brought four stories, and all of them come down to one question: who pays for AI, and who controls it?

The German government wants AI providers to pay when their systems produce text, music or video that competes with human work. The money would flow to creators through collecting societies like GEMA. This is not yet a law, just a position paper sent to the EU. A legislative package from the Commission isn’t expected until the second quarter of twenty twenty-seven.

The Lower Saxony consumer advice center has sent cease-and-desist letters to OpenAI and Anthropic. Under its terms, Anthropic reserves the right to change prices and plans at its own discretion. OpenAI wants to stop billing Plus subscribers by message and bill by token instead, meaning by the length of the text. What that will cost is, in the consumer advocates’ words, completely unclear.

Signal is blocking all screenshots on iOS twenty-seven. The reason is AI assistants built into the operating system that could read what’s on your screen. Ireland’s data protection authority warns that third parties whose messages get read along have hardly any chance to object. Apple, Google and Microsoft declined to comment.

Gartner expects European IT spending next year to reach one point five seven trillion dollars, up just under ten percent. Infrastructure is growing by almost a third, IT services by only four and a half percent. The driver is the day-to-day running of AI models. But keep in mind, that’s a forecast, not a measurement.

Two studies out of Brazil seem to contradict each other. Bain says ninety-five percent of companies use AI. Amcham says fifty-two percent of executives see no measurable result. Both can be true, because using something isn’t the same as measuring it.

On the AI Act, there are four days left until Wednesday, December second. That’s when the new ban on AI-generated nude images without consent kicks in. And on the SAP report about ten thousand job cuts, there’s still no second source.

The project idea for twiceD follows from those price clauses: an exit plan for companies whose AI feature depends on a single provider. A gateway shows the cost per use case, and a tested replacement model makes switching possible.
