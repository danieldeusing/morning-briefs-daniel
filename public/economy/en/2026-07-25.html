37.5 Percent — and the Real Doesn't Flinch

Why the stock market paid for the tariff immediately and the FX market didn't

Since Friday, most Brazilian goods entering the US carry a tariff of 37.5%. It's built in two layers: the 25% confirmed since Jul 15, plus a surcharge of 12.5% announced Jul 23 and effective Jul 24, which Washington justifies with allegedly insufficient enforcement against forced labor.[1][2] Steel and aluminum run separately at 50%. Roughly 471 products are exempt — coffee, crude oil, fertilizer, orange juice, beef, pulp, civil aviation — and by the government's own estimate, about 2,000 line items escape the full stack.[2] Honest caveat: my citable sources are a market live blog and a tax-advisory analysis, not a US official gazette — the order of magnitude holds, but I'd only treat the exact product list as firm once it's officially published.

The reaction split cleanly. The Ibovespa lost 1.52% on Friday to 174,041.95 points — the week still closed up +0.19%.[1] The FX market, by contrast: nothing. The commercial dollar closed at R$5.081, 0.06% lower.[1] Stocks paid, FX didn't.

Tariff pass-through to the exchange rate: textbook logic says a punitive tariff should weaken the exporting country's currency — if Brazil sells less to the US, fewer dollars flow into the country. Like a lemonade stand whose street gets closed off: fewer customers, less revenue. That it isn't happening this time has three reasons — the exemption list protects exactly the big foreign-currency earners (oil, coffee, pulp), the rate spread keeps pulling in capital, and a tariff hits company profits faster than it hits a country's currency flows.

My take — and the counter-bet I read the FX calm not as an all-clear, but as a delay. Trade effects take quarters, stock prices take seconds; the channel to the real is the trade balance, and that only shows up in the July and August statistics. The counter-bet, to be fair: if the exemption list really does cover most of the export value and Brasília negotiates instead of retaliating, the tariff ends up being noise for the exchange rate. That's exactly why the forecast below shows a range and not a point.

FX in Detail — One Fix, Two Clocks

Where we stand, why, and what Monday's next anchor will be

This weekend's anchor is Friday's ECB reference fix: EUR/BRL 5.7826, +0.09% above Thursday's fix.[3] That's the second-lowest fix since mid-May, 2.79% below the monthly high on Jul 3 (5.9486) and 11.4% below where it stood a year ago. The twelve-month trend runs clearly against Daniel's exchange — whether it turns is the only question that matters here.

The timing trap. The fix is pulled around 14:15 CET, i.e. mid-morning Brasília time. USD/BRL stood at 5.0827 there, +0.22% versus Thursday[4] — the real was weaker in the first hours of the tariff shock. By the close in São Paulo it had turned to R$5.081. Read only the fix and you see a weaker real; read only the close and you see a firmer one. For Daniel, the fix is what counts, because bank conversions key off it.

Cross-rate check — clean The check only works if the EUR/USD leg is pulled independently, otherwise you're checking a number against itself. With an independently sourced EUR/USD 1.1377[5] and USD/BRL 5.0827, the implied cross-rate comes out to 5.7826 against the published fix 5.7826: 0.00% divergence, warning threshold 0.5%. A second, independent piece of evidence: the close of R$5.081 from the Brazilian spot market — a 0.06% gap, i.e. noise.

What else is fresh. The DXY closed at 101.47, +0.02% — a dollar that's waiting.[6] And oil is easing: Brent $98.38, −2.29% on Friday[7], back in double digits for the first time in days — after roughly +30% for the month on the Middle East escalation. That's the most important change heading into midweek: if oil falls, US inflation pressure eases, and the Fed has to sound less hawkish on Wednesday. Double-edged for the real — it loses a commodity tailwind, gains a calmer rate environment.

What this means for exchanging money At 5.7826, €5,000 gets exactly R$28,913 — about R$830 less than at the monthly high on Jul 3. Anyone exchanging today would still be locking in close to the worst level since mid-May. My base case sees the rate higher over 30 days and beyond, supported by three independent arguments: tariff pass-through, the fiscal hole, one-sided positioning. This describes the situation, not a recommendation — forecasts aren't exchange rates.

Everyone Is Sitting on the Same Side of the Boat

Positioning, sentiment, and what the sell side is rebuilding right now

The most interesting number of the week isn't on the price sheet — it's in the US futures regulator's positioning report. In real futures on the CME, speculative accounts hold 55,346 long contracts against 1,791 shorts, out of 106,059 open contracts (as of Jul 14).[8] That's roughly 31 longs for every short.

CFTC positioning ("COT"): the US regulator publishes weekly who's betting on rising (long) and who's betting on falling (short) prices in the futures markets. Extreme one-sidedness isn't a buy signal, it's a risk signal: if almost everyone stands on the same side of the boat, there's no one left to buy more — but plenty of people who'll want to get out at the same time once it turns. The numbers are from the prior Tuesday, so about ten days old.

And the inflow is fading. The first half was exceptional: +$17.78 billion net inflow, the best figure since 2018. In July, almost nothing of that is left — through Jul 17, just +$189 million, practically a zero.[9] Reserves stand at $369.8 billion (Jul 17), a strong cushion — but the engine that carried the real this year is idling.

The sell side is turning faster than the consensus. The Focus median from about 170 institutions has held the year-end dollar at R$5.20 for five weeks, the Selic at 14.00%, IPCA at 5.15%.[10] The individual houses have moved further: Itaú raised its target in June from 5.15 to 5.30 — citing the external scenario and a higher domestic risk premium.[11] BTG Pactual moved in its July macro report from 4.90 to 5.40, having still cut to 4.90 in May.[12] Anyone who reverses 50 centavos in the opposite direction within two months is telling you more about the uncertainty than any point estimate.

Risk premium: the 5-year CDS stood at roughly 124 basis points at Friday's close — historically calm.[13] Caveat: no clean second source for the same day; an independent series saw 126–127 bp in early July. At the long end, the market still demands a lot: the Tesouro IPCA+ 2035 yielded IPCA + 8.33% real in July — simultaneously the reason capital flows into the real, and the price a state with a R$145 billion hole pays.

The Fiscal Hole Has Become Real

Until now, Brazil's budget problem was a forecasting debate. With the first-half result, it's a booked entry: from January to June 2026, the central government's primary deficit adds up to R$92.3 billion — up from R$9.8 billion in the same period of 2025, nearly tenfold.[14] The rolling 12-month balance flipped from a +16.2 billion surplus to a −145.2 billion deficit. Drivers: primary spending up a real +12%, net revenue up only +5.5% — plus court-ordered payments (precatórios) front-loaded in March.

Primary deficit: the government's balance before interest payments. Like a household budget where the loan interest hasn't been entered yet: if there's already a minus there, you have to borrow even more just to cover the interest. So debt grows even when you strip out the interest burden entirely.

Two numbers, one caveat. For the month of June, two figures are circulating — roughly R$44.3 billion in the widely reported "central government" scope and R$49.0 billion in another summary. That's a scope question, not a disagreement; I'm citing both rather than a falsely precise single number. Equally honest: the Tesouro Nacional's portal wasn't reachable today (404), so the first-half and 12-month figures come from the IPEA preliminary estimate.[14]

For the exchange rate, what matters is this: a government that spends 12% more and takes in 5.5% more, in real terms, cements exactly the interest burden that still makes the real attractive today — and pushes the problem into an election year.
