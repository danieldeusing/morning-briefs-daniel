From 12 to 38 Percent

How an oil price in the Middle East is flipping the rate debate in Washington — and what that does to the real

The most important number of the weekend isn't a price quote, it's a probability. CME FedWatch, which derives what traders expect from Fed funds futures, showed a 38% probability of a rate hike as soon as Wednesday on Jul 24 — versus under 12% a week earlier. Consensus remains "hold" within the target range of 3.50–3.75%; the decision comes Wed Jul 29, 14:00 ET = 15:00 BRT.[1]

Priced-in probability: not a survey of economists, but a back-calculation from real bets. In Chicago, contracts trade whose value depends on the future US policy rate; their price reveals what the market considers likely — the same way you can back out a team's win probability from betting odds. "38%" means: real money is riding on this.

There's a single reason: oil. Brent crossed the $100 mark on Thursday[1] and closed Friday at $98.38 (−2.29%) — even after the pullback, still 30.3% above last month and 43.8% above a year ago.[2] Expensive oil makes transport and electricity more expensive — exactly the prices that flow through fastest into US inflation statistics. A central bank that was just talking about cutting can't ignore that.

Why this matters more to Daniel than the tariff. The real has lived off its rate spread for a year and a half — and that spread is now shrinking from both sides. The Selic sits at 14.25% (since Jun 17),[3] and consensus expects 14.00% by year-end.[4] In the US, the only open question used to be how fast rates would come down — now a hike is on the table.

Selic 14.25%− Fed midpoint 3.625%= 10.6 points today→ 10.1 points after hike + cut
Carry (rate spread): anyone who borrows in dollars and invests in Brazil pockets the rate difference — today roughly 10.6 percentage points a year. Like a savings account that pays out more than the loan you funded it with. If the difference shrinks, part of the demand for reais disappears.
My take — and the counterargument Half a percentage point less carry sounds harmless, but these positions are leveraged: anyone working with 5x leverage doesn't lose 0.5 points of return, they lose 2.5. The fair counterargument: 38% also means the market puts 62% odds on "hold" — and one calm day out of the Middle East can reverse this number as fast as it climbed.

37.5 Percent — Now a Campaign Issue

How a tariff turns into a domestic political boomerang

The tariff has stood at its full level since Friday, built in two layers: 25% under Section 301 since Wednesday, Jul 22,[10] plus 12.5% since Friday, justified by allegedly insufficient controls against forced labor. 3,985 products worth $10.8 billion now pay 37.5%; overall, some form of additional tariff hits 48.7% of all Brazilian exports to the US.[11] Coffee, beef, certain fruits, and aviation parts stay exempt — exactly the items with the largest foreign-currency volume.[10]

Section 301: a provision of the 1974 US trade law that allows Washington to impose tariffs against "unfair" trade practices after a formal investigation. The detour matters: since the Supreme Court struck down the broad emergency tariffs in February 2026, Section 301 is the remaining legal route.[10] Like a blocked main entrance where someone finds the service door instead: it takes longer, but it works.

The political calculus. Brasília calls the measures impermissible, wants to activate the reciprocity law, and plans to go to the WTO; Lula's office stated there is "no justification for unilateral measures against our country."[10] In parallel, the government is rolling out a credit line for affected industries, while the industry association CNI is pushing for dialogue.[11] Three months before the election, a foreign tariff lets the incumbent be both national defender and emergency relief provider at the same time — Bloomberg's headline suggests it risks becoming a campaign gift.[12]

Why the FX market yawns anyway. Because the exemption list protects the big dollar earners, because a tariff hits profits first and trade flows only quarters later, and because the rate spread keeps attracting capital. My reading: delay, not an all-clear. The counterargument: if the exemption list really does cover the lion's share of export value, the tariff ends up being noise for the exchange rate.

Risk Premium & Sentiment

The most striking thing about Brazil's risk premiums is how unstriking they are. The 5-year CDS closed Friday at 124.40 basis points, practically unchanged.[13] An independent series saw the same week at 126–127 bp — normal provider spread, not a contradiction.

CDS (Credit Default Swap): insurance against a country defaulting on its debt; 124 basis points means a 1.24% premium per year. Like fire insurance — if the price rises, the market considers a fire more likely. At this level, the market is saying: Brazil has a budget problem, not a payment problem.

The EWZ, the large US fund tracking Brazilian stocks, stood at $36.62 on Jul 23 — 13% below its 52-week high of $42.02, but 39% above its low of $26.30.[14] Foreign investors haven't fled; they've just stopped adding.

On bonds, there's a gap today. Neither the long NTN-B real yields nor the DI curve could be pulled cleanly with a date — and a single short-end point wouldn't be a picture of the curve. Better no number at all than turning one point into a trend.

It's Sunday, July twenty-sixth. Nothing trades today, and yet something fundamental shifted over the weekend. Three weeks ago, the debate was still about when the Federal Reserve would cut rates. Now the futures markets are betting it might raise them instead, as soon as Wednesday.

The number behind that comes from the Chicago futures exchange's FedWatch tool. On Friday, it showed a thirty-eight percent probability of a rate hike at Wednesday's meeting. A week earlier, that number was under twelve percent. Consensus still calls for holding steady, in the target range of three-and-a-half to three-and-three-quarters percent. But the distribution has shifted dramatically.

The trigger isn't the American economy — it's the price of oil. Brent crude crossed one hundred dollars on Thursday and pulled back about two percent on Friday, to ninety-eight forty. Even after that pullback, it's still roughly thirty percent above where it stood a month ago, and nearly forty-four percent above a year ago. Expensive oil makes transportation and electricity more expensive, and those are exactly the prices that show up fastest in American inflation data.

For Daniel, that's a bigger story than the tariff, because of the rate spread. The real has lived for a year and a half on Brazil paying fourteen and a quarter percent while the United States pays only around three and a half. That's roughly ten point six percentage points of advantage. Now it's shrinking from both sides: Brazil is expected to keep cutting, and the US suddenly has a hike on the table. If both happen, the spread would drop to about ten point one points. That sounds small, but carry positions are leveraged, so half a point quickly turns into a full point of lost return.

The anchor for the exchange rate through Monday is Friday's reference rate: five point seven eight two six reais per euro. The cross-rate check is completely clean today, with zero percent divergence between the published rate and what you'd calculate from the dollar-real and euro-dollar rates. At this level, ten thousand euros buy fifty-seven thousand eight hundred twenty-six reais — about sixteen hundred less than at the monthly high in early July.

There's an uncomfortable flip side to the oil story. What helps the Fed costs Brazil money. The benchmark Ibovespa index lost one point five two percent on Friday, closing around one hundred seventy-four thousand points, dragged down mainly by Petrobras. Brazil today is a net oil exporter — when the price falls, export revenue and stock prices fall together. There's simply no scenario this week where both channels pull in the same direction.

On the tariff, the full rate has applied since Friday. Twenty-five percent has been in place since Wednesday, with another twelve and a half percent added Friday. Nearly four thousand products now pay a combined thirty-seven and a half percent, representing about eleven billion dollars of exports. Overall, some form of surcharge hits nearly half of everything Brazil sells to the United States. Brasília wants to file a complaint at the World Trade Organization and activate a reciprocity law, and Lula has announced a credit line for the affected industries.

This is politically notable. Three months before the election, a foreign tariff lets the incumbent play both national defender and emergency responder at the same time. Bloomberg has already run a headline suggesting the tariffs risk becoming a campaign gift for Lula. The currency market shrugs it off, mainly because the exemption list protects the big foreign-currency earners: coffee, beef, fruit, aviation parts. I read that calm as a delay, not an all-clear.

My base case sees the exchange rate higher over thirty days and beyond, in a range of five point seven two to six point zero two. Compared with yesterday, I've widened the ranges and trimmed my confidence slightly, though the point estimates have barely moved. The weekend made the distribution wider, not shifted its center. That remains a description of the situation, not a recommendation.

Monday brings the first fresh reference rate and the Brazilian central bank's weekly consensus survey. The real event comes Wednesday: the Fed's decision at three p.m. Brasília time, with no new projections, so tone alone will decide. That's what the next briefing will focus on.
