Six Percent Oil in a Day

How a single barrel is turning back the rate debate in Washington — and why Brazil only half benefits

Brent stands this morning at $92.11 a barrel, 6.37% below the previous close[1]; thin Sunday-evening trading briefly printed $90.95.[2] The math checks out cleanly: $98.38 from Friday minus 6.37% is exactly $92.11 — two separately dated pulls from the same source confirm each other. On Thursday, the same contract was still above $100.

That's not a detail for commodity traders — it's the unwinding of the story that carried all of Friday: the jump above $100 had, within a week, created a bet on a Fed rate hike on Wednesday. Today the market aggregate is back to roughly 60–80% for "hold", depending on the provider, within the 3.50–3.75% range.[3]

Priced-in probability: not a survey of economists, but a back-calculation from real bets — from the price of contracts whose value hinges on the future US policy rate. The same way you back out a win probability from betting odds. A caution about comparing to Friday: the 38% hike bet came from CME FedWatch, today's roughly 60–80% depends on which news aggregate you use — the direction holds, the provider doesn't.

My take — and the counterargument I read the drop as a return to normal, not a new direction: last week's premium was a war premium, not a supply shortage — and premiums decay fast. The fair counterargument: precisely because nothing physically happened, a single headline out of the Strait of Hormuz can pull the price right back within hours. One quiet news day isn't a resolved conflict.

The Hole in the First Half

Why the fiscal number says more about the real than the tariff

Brazil's central government closed the first half of 2026 with a primary deficit of R$92.3 billion — in the same period of 2025 it was R$9.8 billion.[9] A deterioration of more than R$80 billion in twelve months.

Primary result: government revenue minus spending, without interest on existing debt. Like a household ledger that doesn't yet include the loan payments: it shows whether the government makes ends meet on its day-to-day operations. A primary deficit means the debt is already growing before a single interest payment is made.

The market stays calm: the Tesouro Nacional's Prisma Fiscal, the monthly analyst survey, actually trimmed its median for the full year slightly — from R$59.016 (June) to R$58.077 billion in deficit (July).[10]

Both numbers only fit together with one strong assumption. If R$92.3 billion in deficit accumulated in the first half and the year is supposed to end at R$58.1 billion, the second half needs to deliver a primary surplus of roughly R$34 billion. Plausible explanations: one-off payments pulled forward — or too optimistic a median. I lean toward the second reading and treat the consensus fiscal forecast as the weakest link in the bull case for the real. Caveat: the first-half figure comes from aggregated reporting, not a primary document.

Risk Premium & Positioning

What's notable about Brazil's risk premiums is how unremarkable they remain despite the fiscal number. The 5-year CDS closed Friday at 124.40 basis points, practically unchanged (+0.12%).[11]

CDS (Credit Default Swap): insurance against a country defaulting on its debt; 124 basis points means a 1.24% premium per year. Like fire insurance — if the price rises, the market considers a fire more likely. At this level, the market is saying: Brazil has a budget problem, not a payment problem.

The EWZ, the large US fund tracking Brazilian stocks, closed on Jul 24 at $35.73 (−1.22%), with $9.17 billion in fund assets, a 52-week range of $26.30–42.02.[12] About 15% below its yearly high, a good third above its low: foreign investors haven't fled, they've just stopped adding.

On positioning, only half a statement is possible: open real futures on the CME stood at 106,059 contracts (Jul 14), but the net-long/net-short split couldn't be pulled.[13] A level without direction is a fever thermometer without a scale — which is why it's presented here without interpretation.

The effect shows up immediately in Washington. The jump above a hundred dollars had, within a week, created a bet that the Federal Reserve would actually raise rates on Wednesday. For Daniel's exchange rate, the chain is simple: cheaper oil, calmer inflation expectations, a smaller hike bet, a weaker dollar, a firmer real. The dollar index is already down a quarter percent today, to one hundred one point two one. That's the only genuinely fresh quote in today's brief, and it lines up exactly with Friday's level of one hundred one point four seven, minus zero point two five percent.

The last link in that chain is the weakest one, though, for a Brazilian reason. Brazil is a net oil exporter. Cheaper oil relieves the Federal Reserve and, at the same time, costs Petrobras revenue. So the real gets a tailwind through the rate channel and a headwind through the trade channel. I think the effect is real, but muted.

The anchor for exchanging money is still Friday's: five point seven eight two six reais to the euro. The first fresh reference rate arrives today around quarter past eleven, Brasília time. The most uncomfortable number of the day comes out of Brazil's budget. The central government closed the first half with a primary deficit of ninety-two point three billion reais.
