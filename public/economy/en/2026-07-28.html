Almost a Full Percent of Real in One Night

The path from Washington's rate projection to Daniel's exchange rate — step by step

The real lost about 0.94% against the dollar overnight: from 5.0825 (ECB-backed cross on Jul 27)[6] to 5.1305 live this morning[2] — a second live source confirms with 5.1264.[4] The euro didn't rise along with it: EUR/USD slipped from 1.1389 to 1.1363, or −0.23%.[3][7] The dollar index sits at 101.53 accordingly.[8]

That's exactly what produces Daniel's rate. One percent of real weakness minus a fifth of a percent of euro weakness works out arithmetically to +0.71% in EUR/BRL — the measured figure is +0.73%. The breakdown checks out, and it says something: the euro is today's drag. Had EUR/USD stayed stable, EUR/BRL would sit around 5.85.

Cross rate: EUR/BRL isn't traded directly on any exchange — it's assembled from two prices: euros per dollar times dollars per real. Like converting meters to feet by way of centimeters: two steps, one result. That's why Daniel's rate can rise even when nothing has happened in Brazil at all — it's enough that the dollar strengthens against half the world.
My read — and where it wobbles I read the move as pure pre-Fed positioning: the dollar is being bought because the June dots project a hike and nobody wants to be on the wrong side tomorrow. Supporting that: Brazil's risk premium fell (CDS −0.53%) — with genuine Brazil worry, it would have risen. The fair objection: the timing coincides with Friday's budget unfreeze, and three days is exactly the lag with which fiscal news tends to seep into the rate.

Rate spread (carry): anyone who borrows in dollars and invests in Brazil pockets the difference — today around 10.6 percentage points a year (Selic 14.25% against a Fed midpoint of 3.625%). Like a savings account that pays out more than the loan you funded it with. What's new: until now, the spread only shrank because Brazil was cutting. If the Fed projects a hike, it shrinks from both sides — and because these positions are leveraged, half a point hits harder than it sounds.

Tomorrow, 15:00 BRT — the Tone Is the News

Why a meeting with no new numbers is the most important one of the month

The Fed meets today and tomorrow; the decision and statement land Wednesday 14:00 ET = 15:00 BRT, with the press conference at 15:30 BRT.[9] The target range has stood at 3.50–3.75% since Jun 17, confirmed unanimously (12-0) at the time.[10] There are no new projections this time — those only appear in March, June, September, and December.

That's why what was decided in June — and still stands today — is what counts: the median rate projection for year-end 2026 jumped to 3.8%, up from 3.4% in March.[11] That's above the 3.625% midpoint of the range — meaning the center of the committee expects a step up by year-end.

Dot Plot: each committee member anonymously marks a dot where they see the policy rate at year-end. Like everyone in the family writing down their guess for the vacation budget, and you take the middle one. No commitment, just the gravity of opinion. If that median tips from "cut" to "hike," the FX market prices it in immediately — that's exactly what's carrying the dollar today.

Brasília Thaws the Budget

R$5.7 billion freed — and a target the government itself doesn't believe in

On Jul 25, the government released R$5.7 billion (~$1.1 billion) from the budget freeze; R$17.9 billion remains blocked, down from R$23.7 billion.[12] Small on its own — the context matters: the 2026 primary target calls for a +R$34.3 billion surplus (0.25% of GDP), while the economic team's own forecast is +R$10.8 billion.[12]

Budget freeze (contingenciamento): the state voluntarily freezes part of its planned spending to hold the annual target — like putting vacation money into a second account so you don't spend it. An unfreeze means the money can go back out. In an election year, the market reads this as a sign of how seriously the government takes its own target.
A gap I couldn't resolve The government's projection (+R$10.8 billion) and the analyst median from the Jul 15 Prisma Fiscal (−R$58.1 billion) sit about R$69 billion apart. I couldn't confirm today whether both measure the same perimeter — I'm flagging the gap, not selling it as a proven contradiction.

Oil Down Eleven Percent — and the Real Feels Nothing

Brent sits at $87.25 (Jul 28, −0.78% versus the previous day per the source).[13] The same source printed $92.11 yesterday morning, and the contract stood at $98.38 on Friday — by my own intraday, not settlement-based, pulls, about −11% in three sessions. Independent settlement quotes (e.g. Reuters) put Monday's close closer to $88–90 — the direction of the oil slide is independently confirmed, the exact size is not.

Yesterday's chain of reasoning didn't hold up. I'd argued: cheaper oil → calmer US inflation expectations → a smaller hike bet → a weaker dollar → a firmer real. Step one was right, the outcome wasn't. The lesson: ahead of a meeting, positioning beats fundamentals. Oil works on inflation data over weeks; a dot plot works on the futures market in hours. The oil channel isn't dead, just postponed — and it's double-edged for Brazil regardless, since cheaper oil costs Petrobras revenue.

It's Tuesday, July twenty-eighth. Yesterday's thesis was that cheaper oil would ease the Federal Reserve, the dollar would give ground, and the Brazilian real would get some relief. Oil has kept falling since then — and the real lost ground anyway. That's the real story this morning.

Here's the number first. The euro is trading live at around five point eight three reais, against yesterday's reference rate of five point seven eight eight five. That's zero point seven three percent more in a single session. For Daniel's exchange, that means ten thousand euros now buy about fifty-eight thousand three hundred reais, a good four hundred twenty-four more than yesterday. No reference rate has been higher since July twenty-first.

The reason isn't in Brazil. The dollar got stronger against almost everything: the real lost zero point nine four percent against it, the euro lost zero point two three percent. Put those together and you get exactly the zero point seven three percent Daniel's rate climbed. What's interesting is that the weaker euro actually held the rise back. If the euro had stayed steady, we'd be at five point eight five.

A word on the sourcing, because today it's unusually the story itself. For the live rate, there were two conflicting readings from the same site: five point eight three and five point seven nine. I settled the dispute through the cross rate. Two independently pulled live legs — dollar to real and euro to dollar — work out to five point eight two nine. That's two hundredths of a percent from the first reading and almost seven tenths of a percent from the second. I'm running the day with five point eight three and treating the other number as a stale cache.

Tomorrow at three in the afternoon, Brasília time, the Federal Reserve decides. The target range has held at three and a half to three and three-quarters percent since mid-June, confirmed unanimously at the time. There are no new projections this round, so the wording alone will decide. And the starting point is unfriendly for the real: in June, the committee's median rate guess for year-end jumped to three point eight percent, up from three point four in March. That's above today's midpoint. The center of the committee is now expecting a hike, not a cut.

Out of Brazil comes a quiet but uncomfortable headline of its own. On Friday, the government released five point seven billion reais from the budget freeze; what's still blocked is just under eighteen billion, down from almost twenty-four. This year's primary target is a surplus of thirty-four point three billion — the economic team's own forecast is ten point eight billion. A target the government itself doesn't believe in is no anchor in an election year.

What's notable is what didn't happen. Insurance against a Brazilian default actually got cheaper yesterday, to one hundred twenty-three point seven four basis points. The ten-year yield eased slightly too. When the real falls but the risk premium doesn't rise, that's a dollar move, not a Brazil move. That's exactly why I'm reading last night as pure pre-meeting positioning.

My forecast shifts higher accordingly, by about five centavos across all five steps. For today I see five point eight zero to five point eight six, most likely five point eight three. For this week, five point seven six to five point nine four. Out to thirty days, up toward six point zero two, because Brazil's rate-setting committee meets August fourth and fifth and consensus now sees only one more cut. All the probabilities here are my own confidence, not market-implied.
