The Real Gave Ground — the Dollar Didn't

Why tonight looks different from the last one — and what it means for exchanging money

The last auditable anchor is yesterday's ECB fix: 5.8158, +0.47% versus Monday.[1] This morning the rate trades, independently measured, at 5.8557 — +0.69% above the fix[4] and thus above the 0.5% threshold at which we flag a divergence. Explainable, not suspicious: the fix was taken at 16:00 CET, the oil shock came after.

Break down the 0.69% and the night becomes legible. The real lost 0.43% against the dollar (USD/BRL 5.1164 → 5.1384).[2][3] At the same time, the euro gained an implied 0.26% (EUR/USD 1.1367 → implied 1.1396).[2] Multiplied together, that comes out to exactly the measured 0.69%: two-thirds real weakness, one-third euro strength.

Risk-off: when shots are fired somewhere, investors first sell whatever they can offload fastest — emerging-market currencies. Like a schoolyard where a fight breaks out: the smallest kids run first. The real is one of the small kids, even though Brazil has nothing to do with the conflict.
My read — and the honest objection The most important data point is the one that's missing: the dollar index sits at 101.2689, 0.15% lower than at the prior close.[5] A weaker dollar alongside a weaker real means: not a dollar story, an emerging-market story. Sharper still, because Brazil exports oil — pricier oil should support the real. That this channel gets overruled tells me the market is trading the Fed, not Brazil. Fair objection: 0.43% isn't a breakout; it could just as easily be thin pre-decision liquidity.

For exchanging money: at 5.8557, €5,000 gets you about R$29,278 — at the monthly low (Jul 23, 5.7775) it would have been R$28,888, a R$391 difference. A year ago: R$32,255. The euro has lost about a tenth of its purchasing power over twelve months; that's the trend against which any day's mood swing looks small.

15:00 BRT: Warsh's First Real Acid Test

Holding is the consensus — but a third of the market is betting against it

The Fed announces at 2:00 PM New York time = 15:00 BRT, with the press conference 30 minutes later.[1] Economists surveyed by FactSet expect an unchanged 3.50–3.75%, the fifth hold in a row.[1] The CME's FedWatch tool put the probability of a hold at 65.7% as of Jul 27 — read the other way: roughly a third of market pricing does not expect a hold.

Dot Plot (rate projection): four times a year, each Fed member anonymously marks a dot showing where they see the policy rate at year-end. Like eighteen people independently guessing how warm it'll be on Christmas Day — the middle of the cloud is the headline takeaway.

That cloud tipped clearly higher in June: the median projected policy rate for year-end 2026 rose to 3.8%, up from 3.4% in March.[2] Of 18 projections, nine were above today's midpoint of 3.625%, eight sat right on it, only one was below — and 17 of 18 saw inflation risks skewed higher.[2]

This is where the oil price meets monetary policy. Overnight, Iran fired ballistic missiles at a US base; they were intercepted. Brent jumped 3.81% to $87.29[3], clawing back part of the roughly 16% slide from the three prior sessions. The conflict has been running since late February — as recently as early July, the price was below $71.[4]

What the rate really hinges on today Not the decision itself — that's two-thirds priced in. It's two sentences in the statement: whether the oil price gets described as a temporary supply shock or an inflation risk. The dollar index was consolidating below 101.50 ahead of the meeting, with an upward undertone near a monthly high.[5]

On the chair: Kevin Warsh was confirmed 54-45 on May 13, sworn in on May 22, term through 2030.[6][7] He's regarded as a hawk — anyone betting on a soothing choice of words is betting against the basic instincts of the man at the microphone.

Driven by food (−0.66%), held back by housing (+0.97%) with household electricity up 3.03%.

My read, against the reflex: the usual take — "lower inflation → more cuts → a weaker real" — falls short. With the Selic at 14.25% and inflation at 4.52%, the real interest rate sits at nearly 9.7 points; 25 basis points won't change that. What the real needs is credible disinflation — and that's what arrived yesterday. Net: mildly real-friendly.

BR A Better Deficit — for the Wrong Reason

On Jul 24, the government cut its forecast for the 2026 primary deficit from R$60.3 billion to R$52 billion, driven by R$12.3 billion more in net revenue.[2] Excluding precatórios — court-ordered legacy debts that the budget framework is allowed to exclude — the result would even be a surplus of R$10.8 billion. The target is a primary surplus of 0.25% of GDP (about R$34.3 billion), tolerance band zero to R$68.6 billion.

Why I'm not celebrating this as consolidation: according to the government's own explanation, the extra revenue comes mostly from higher income-tax receipts tied to the inflation driven by the Middle East conflict. The state is profiting from rising prices — not spending discipline, but currency debasement with an extra step, and it disappears again as soon as oil falls.

It's Wednesday, July twenty-ninth. This afternoon at three o'clock Brasília time, the Federal Reserve decides — the first real stress test for the new chair, Kevin Warsh, after an overnight escalation in Iran pushed oil prices up by nearly four percent. The currency market already moved ahead of the news.

Start with the anchor. Tuesday's European Central Bank reference rate was five point eight one five eight reais to the euro. This morning, independently measured, the rate trades at five point eight five five seven — a gap of zero point six nine percent, above the threshold where we flag a divergence. The reason is timing, not error: the fix is taken in the afternoon in Europe, and the oil shock hit after that.

That shock came from the Middle East. Iran fired ballistic missiles at a US base overnight; they were intercepted. Brent crude jumped three point eight percent to eighty-seven dollars and twenty-nine cents a barrel, clawing back part of a roughly sixteen percent slide from the three sessions before.

What's more interesting than the move itself is what it's made of. The real lost zero point four three percent against the dollar, while the euro actually gained zero point two six percent against the dollar. Two-thirds real weakness, one-third euro strength. And the dollar index is actually lower today, at one hundred one point two seven. A weaker dollar next to a weaker real means this isn't a dollar story — it's an emerging-market story. That's notable because Brazil exports oil; pricier oil should, in theory, support the real. That it doesn't tells me the market is trading the Fed today, not Brazil.

Brazil, in fact, delivered good news yesterday. The official mid-month inflation preview for July rose just zero point zero six percent, after zero point four one in June. Economists had expected zero point two zero. On a twelve-month basis, inflation falls from four point eight to four point five two percent. The usual reflex is: less inflation, more rate cuts, a weaker currency. I'd push back. With the policy rate at fourteen point two five percent, the real interest rate sits at almost ten points — a quarter-point cut won't change that. What actually keeps foreign bond money in the country is credible disinflation, and that's exactly what showed up yesterday.

On the fiscal side: the government cut its deficit forecast for this year from sixty point three to fifty-two billion reais last Friday. That sounds like consolidation, but it isn't really. The improvement comes from twelve point three billion reais in extra revenue, and that in turn comes from income-tax collections lifted by the inflation the Middle East conflict caused. The state is profiting from rising prices — and that reverses the moment oil falls again. The official target, for the record, remains a surplus of point two five percent of GDP, or about thirty-four billion reais.

My forecast: for today, I see five point eight two to five point nine two, most likely five point eight six. Everything comes down to two sentences in the statement out of Washington — call the oil shock a temporary supply issue and the dollar eases; call it an inflation risk and five point nine is within reach fast. For this week, five point seven eight to five point nine five. Out to thirty days, five point seven four to five point nine eight, because Brazil's rate-setting committee meets August fourth and fifth. The farther-out steps are unchanged — a missile strike moves a trading week, not the rate, fiscal, and election calendar. All these probabilities are my own confidence, not market pricing.

What's missing today, I want to say plainly. Brazil's five-year credit default swap spread, the interest-rate futures curve, real bond yields, net positioning on the futures exchange, and flows into the big Brazil funds were not verifiable. They're left out here rather than estimated. Those are exactly the indicators that usually move before the spot rate does when foreign money is heading for the exits. The next briefing comes down to one question: what was actually in the statement out of Washington — and whether the real gives back tonight's jump.
