This time the push came from Frankfurt

FX in detail — the causal path behind +0.46%

The "fix": every business day around 2:15 p.m. CET, the European Central Bank polls the major central banks for a single official rate and publishes it around 4:00 p.m. Like a school photo: one moment from the whole day gets captured and then counts as "the" rate. What happens afterward only shows up in tomorrow's photo.

Thursday's fix is the first to fully reflect Wednesday's Fed meeting. It rose from 5.8268 to 5.8535 — a gain of 0.46%. Anyone reading that as Brazil coming under pressure is reading it wrong. Break the cross rate into its two legs, and the story flips.

Fed holds, three hawks dissent→ Dollar broadly weakens→ EUR/USD 1.1380 → 1.1476 (+0.84%)→ USD/BRL 5.1202 → 5.1006 (−0.38%)→ EUR/BRL +0.46%

The arithmetic is clear: the euro leg contributed +0.84%, the real leg pushed back with −0.38%, netting +0.46%. The real was actually one of the day's winners — it was just outrun by the euro. The Dollar Index confirms this from the other side: still 100.95 at TradingEconomics on Thursday morning, 99.98 at Thursday's close per investing.com. Almost a full percentage point of dollar weakness in one session — two sources, two capture times, so take the order of magnitude, not the decimal.

What that means for the exchange: a EUR/BRL rise driven by the euro leg is shakier than one driven by Brazilian stress. Euro strength against the dollar can be unwound by a single US data release — Brazilian risk premium builds and unwinds more slowly. And indeed, a third of it is already gone this morning: the xe mid rate stands at 5.8369, 0.28% below the fix.

Risk premium & positioning

CDS (Credit Default Swap): insurance against a sovereign default. 126 basis points means: 1.26% of the insured amount as an annual premium. Like bike insurance — the riskier the neighborhood, the more it costs. The price is the market's most direct vote on Brazil's solvency.

Brazil's five-year CDS sits around 126 bp (investing.com, Jul 30 close; MacroMicro confirms ~126.3 for mid-July). Two independent sources, same level — solid. The provider's stated day-over-day change, by contrast, is internally inconsistent and is deliberately not cited here.

COT report: the US futures regulator publishes weekly who holds how many real contracts on the Chicago exchange — split between fast speculators ("leveraged funds") and long-term managers ("asset managers"). Like peeking into every customer's shopping cart before guessing what the store sells out of next.

As of Jul 21, hedge funds are net +786 contracts — practically neutral (39,303 long against 38,517 short) — no conviction either way. Asset managers, by contrast, hold a net +57,206 contracts (58,969 to 1,763). That's a striking asymmetry: the entire long weight sits with the players who rebalance rarely, and then in large blocks. As long as nothing happens, that's stable; if something does happen, there's no middleman to cushion the move.

Ex-ante real rate: the policy rate minus the inflation the market expects over the next twelve months. What's left after purchasing-power loss — like take-home pay, not gross salary.

With the Selic at 14.25% against a smoothed twelve-month inflation expectation of 4.19% (Focus, Jul 24), the real rate works out to roughly 9.7%. That's near the top globally and explains why asset managers stay put. The equity channel has also been friendly lately: the Brazil ETF EWZ closed at $36.53 on Jul 30, up 2.99% on the day.

Not available: NTN-B and NTN-F yields couldn't be pulled with a clean timestamp today — rather than a guessed real yield, this space is intentionally left blank.

It's Friday, July thirty-first, the last trading day of the month. The euro-real rate is higher than yesterday, and that sounds like good news for anyone earning in euros and living in reais. But the reason behind it isn't quite what the number suggests.

Thursday's European Central Bank reference rate came in at five point eight five three five reais per euro. That's zero point four six percent higher than Wednesday. Break that cross rate down, and the story flips: the euro gained zero point eight four percent against the dollar, while the Brazilian real actually gained zero point three eight percent against the dollar too. So the real wasn't weak at all — it just got outrun by the euro.

The trigger was the US Federal Reserve. The Fed held its policy rate at three point five to three point seven five percent for a fifth straight meeting, but the vote was nine to three — and the three dissenters wanted to raise rates, not cut them. Even so, the dollar broadly weakened. A move driven by the euro side is shakier than one driven by Brazilian stress: by this morning, the mid rate on xe was already back down to five point eight three six nine, meaning a third of yesterday's gain has already evaporated.

The oil argument doesn't convince me, since Brent is nearly nineteen percent higher than a month ago. Still, I lean toward the weaker-real side, and the reason is positioning.

The futures market shows an unusual picture. Fast-moving hedge funds hold a net of just seven hundred eighty-six real contracts — essentially neutral. If something does happen, the cushion isn't there. What holds it together is the interest rate: fourteen point two five percent on the Selic against just over four percent expected inflation works out to a real rate near ten percent.

For today, I expect the fix to come in lower, somewhere between five point eighty and five point eighty-seven, most likely five point eighty-four. These probabilities are my own read, not the market's. Next week gets more interesting: on August fourth and fifth, Brazil's central bank committee meets in Brasília.
