This time it all came from Brazil

FX in detail — the causal path behind −0.75%

The "fix": Every business day around 2:15pm CET, the European Central Bank polls a single official rate and publishes it around 4:00pm. Think of a school photo: one moment out of the whole day gets captured and then counts as "the" rate. No photo gets taken on weekends — so Friday's value stands until Monday.

The fix fell from 5.8535 to 5.8095, a drop of 0.75% — the biggest daily move of the week and the exact mirror image of the prior day, when the euro leg contributed +0.84% and the real pushed back against it.

DXY practically flat (−0.06%)→ EUR/USD 1.1476 → 1.1485 (+0.08%)→ USD/BRL 5.1006 → 5.0583 (−0.83%)→ EUR/BRL −0.75%

The arithmetic is clean: +0.08% from the euro leg, −0.83% from the real leg, net −0.75%. The third number is the decisive one — the dollar index sat at −0.06%, essentially motionless. So this was not general dollar weakness that every emerging-market currency benefited from: the real ran away from the world-market dollar on its own.

And why did it happen? Here honesty gets a bit unsatisfying — there's no cleanly documented trigger. The two most plausible candidates are month-end, when funds and exporters mechanically square their FX holdings, and positioning ahead of Wednesday's Copom meeting. That's a guess, not a finding. What's ruled out is oil: Brent itself edged down slightly on Friday.

What that means for the exchange: A move originating in the real leg is stickier than one from the euro leg. Euro strength against the dollar can get reversed by a single US data release; real strength built on rate advantage and capital inflows builds up more slowly and unwinds just as slowly. For Daniel that's the less pleasant direction: 100 € got 585.35 R$ on Thursday, only 580.95 R$ on Friday.

Oil: the trend is clear, the price isn't

Terms of trade: The ratio between what a country gets for its exports and what it pays for its imports. Like trading cards on a school playground: if your cards suddenly become worth more, you get more back for the same card. Brazil is a net oil exporter — so pricier oil improves its terms of trade and tends to support the real.

Brent climbed sharply over July: +24.4% versus a month ago, per Fortune's Jul 31 figure; TradingEconomics puts it at +26.1%. The trend is doubly confirmed — and good news for Brazil.

Source conflict On the level, sources diverge widely: Fortune quotes $92.27 a barrel for Friday morning (6:45am ET), while TradingEconomics shows $90.24 in its running text and $87.93 in its own data table on the very same page. That's over a 5% spread — too much for a rounding difference. So no point figure for Brent is given here as fact — only the range "high 80s to just over 90 dollars" and the reliable monthly trend.

My take: For the FX forecast, direction is what matters anyway. A quarter-price jump in four weeks is real-positive via the export channel — and at the same time it's the strongest argument for the Fed hawks. What triggered the rise isn't cleanly documented: one circulating explanation cites fresh US-Iran tensions, but no independently confirmed, current-dated source turned up for it. It stands here as an open question, not a cause.

Wednesday evening decides: Copom #280

Copom: The Brazilian central bank's rate-setting committee — the counterpart to a Fed meeting, just every six to seven weeks. It sets the Selic, the policy rate that practically every other rate in Brazil hangs off of. Like the thermostat in a house: one number, but every room feels it.

The 280th meeting runs on August 4 and 5, with the decision landing Wednesday evening after the market close. The Selic has stood at 14.25% since June, and the Focus median puts it at 14.00% by year-end — unchanged for five weeks. Translation: exactly one 25-basis-point cut, then a pause.

"Priced in": When everyone expects the same thing, the expectation is already in the price. Like a Christmas gift you already know the contents of — unwrapping it no longer triggers a reaction. Movement only comes from a deviation, not from the event itself.

That's exactly why the rate number itself is the least interesting piece of Wednesday evening's news. What counts is the wording: if the communiqué signals, in effect, "that was the last one," Brazil's rate advantage is locked in for the next few quarters and the real keeps its tailwind. If it leaves the door open, the calculus flips immediately.

The third possibility — no cut at all — is less far-fetched than the consensus suggests. Focus sees 2026 IPCA inflation at 5.12%, well above target; a central bank cutting rates with inflation above five percent needs to justify that. If the Copom holds still, it would be the week's most real-positive surprise — unlikely, but not out of the question.

Risk premium & buffer

CDS (Credit Default Swap): Insurance against a sovereign default. 126 basis points means: 1.26% of the insured amount per year as a premium. Like bike insurance — the sketchier the neighborhood, the pricier it gets. The price is the most direct market vote on Brazil's ability to pay.

Brazil's five-year CDS closed on Jul 31 at 125.90 — notably calm for a country three months from an election. The bond market reads the political uncertainty far more calmly than the FX forecasts would suggest. The provider's reported day-over-day change is internally inconsistent and is deliberately not cited here; the level itself is reliable.

The second buffer keeps growing: the central bank's net FX position stood at $260.124B on Jul 29, versus $256.302B at the end of 2025, with reserves at $368.899B on Jul 24 (end of 2025: $358.234B). The central bank is building up its cushion rather than spending it — it didn't need to prop up the real recently.

CDI: The rate at which Brazilian banks lend each other money overnight. It follows the Selic like a shadow, usually about ten basis points below it — and it's published daily, so you can see immediately whether a rate change has passed through.

On Aug 1 the CDI stands at 14.15% — exactly the usual gap to the Selic. The interbank market hasn't front-run anything through Saturday. Setting the Selic against the Focus 2026 IPCA forecast (5.12%) gives a real interest rate of roughly 8.7%: near the top internationally, and the real reason long-term capital stays in the country.
