The fix measures the wrong moment

FX in detail — why yesterday's brief had one closing line too many

The "fix": the European Central Bank captures a single official rate every business day around 2:15 p.m. CEST and publishes it around 4:00 p.m. Like a school photo: one moment from the whole day gets captured and then counts as "the" rate. Except: 2:15 p.m. in Frankfurt is 9:15 a.m. in Brasília — the photo is taken shortly after the Brazilian trading day starts, not at its end.

That's exactly what yesterday's brief hinged on. The fix fell from 5.8535 to 5.8095 (−0.75%), and this space said: the real carried the entire move, and a clean trigger couldn't be found. The first half is right, the second half was a search on the wrong day.

Thu 09:15 BRT: fix 5.1006→ Thu afternoon: real strengthens→ Fri 09:15 BRT: fix 5.0583→ Fri close: 5.069–5.079

Both Friday sources see a slightly weaker real: InfoMoney (via Reuters) puts the close at 5.0686 per dollar, up 0.13% on the day; Rio Times cites 5.0793 and a 0.40% real weakness. Working backward from either figure, Thursday's close was around 5.06 — already almost at the level the Friday fix showed the next morning. That's the point, and it holds under both readings: the big real move happened Thursday afternoon, after the fix, and only showed up in the ECB series on Friday. Friday itself ran the opposite way.

Why this is more than bookkeeping: if the last valid fix reflects a move the market has since partly unwound, the next fix mechanically starts higher — without anything new needing to happen. At an unchanged EUR/USD, Friday's close corresponds to a EUR/BRL of 5.821 (InfoMoney) to 5.834 (Rio Times): not a forecast, just working out what has already traded.

Caveat Three separate blur points, and the middle one matters most today. First, the cross rate mixes a Brazilian closing price with a EUR/USD reading from the fix moment — clean would mean both at the same time. Second, that one closing price doesn't actually exist: InfoMoney/Reuters write 5.0686 (+0.13%), Rio Times 5.0793 (−0.40% for the real) — 0.21% apart, which swings the gap to the fix between 0.20% and 0.41%. Third, B3 had a system outage on Friday: after problems with file transmission and opening the Câmara B3, half the morning ran in auction mode — regular trading in all securities and contracts only resumed at 12:30, the DI segment stayed closed through the morning, and without dollar futures, trading desks lacked a price reference. Two sources could simply have caught different moments as "the close" on a day like that. That's enough for direction; not for the third decimal.

For the exchange: €100 bought R$580.95 at Friday's fix, and a calculated R$582.13 to R$583.36 at Brazil's actual Friday close. Anyone checking the fix over the weekend and concluding "it fell, so wait" is reading a value the market has already moved past — by exactly how much, the sources disagree.

Positioning: the central bank pulled back its insurance — as of February

FX swap (swap cambial): a contract the Brazilian central bank sells that pays out if the real falls against the dollar. It gives the market dollar insurance without actually selling dollars out of reserves — like fire insurance: the money only flows if there's a fire. A shrinking stock means the central bank thinks it needs less coverage.

And that's exactly what was observed — in February, not on Friday. Bloomberg Línea reported on Feb 23, 2026 that the swap stock was heading below $100 billion, for the first time since May 2022; the BCB was letting roughly $2 billion in FX credit lines expire, the second straight month without a full rollover. Not a retreat out of distress, but the opposite: with strong capital inflows, the market no longer needs that level of coverage.

Source status — read this as background, not news This figure comes from a Bloomberg Línea article dated Feb 23, 2026 — five months old — and not from the BCB's own "Posição em swap cambial" table itself; the central bank's statistics page couldn't be pulled with a clean timestamp today, and an updated report on this exact figure wasn't findable for July 2026. It's included here as structural context on central-bank behavior, not as a fresh move. Not to be confused with the ongoing FX position ("posição cambial líquida," on the order of $260 billion) — that's a different figure.

My read: the strongest quiet bull point for the real in this brief — with the explicit caveat that I don't know whether the drawdown has continued, stopped, or reversed since February. A central bank pulling back its coverage is implicitly betting it won't be needed. Fitting that picture — and this part is current — is the five-year CDS, the market's insurance against a sovereign default, at 125.90 basis points at the week's close: left unchanged, three months before the election.

The 92 billion that got lost in the rate celebration

Primary deficit: what the government spends minus what it takes in — without interest on old debt. Like a household ledger where the loan payments don't even show up yet: if there's already a shortfall there, the day-to-day money isn't even covering everyday life, let alone the debt. That's why it's the number rating agencies and FX traders look at first.

On Friday at 3:54 p.m. — with the real already the day's talking point — the finance ministry reported: the central government booked a primary deficit of R$48.2 billion in June, bringing the first-half total to R$92.2 billion. Converted at Friday's fix, that's roughly €8.3 billion and €15.9 billion, respectively — and that's before a single interest payment is made.

Under the central bank's different methodology, the June figure comes in even higher, at just over R$55 billion — not a contradiction, just a different scope of measurement. The official reference here is the Fazenda's figure.

My take — and here I disagree with the market: the FX market simply didn't trade this news on Friday. It's pricing the carry, meaning the rate advantage, and tuning out the fiscal path. Short term, that's rational: as long as the Selic sits at 14.25%, waiting pays more than the fiscal risk costs in a matter of days. But that's not a state that survives a campaign. That's exactly why my 3-to-6-month scenario tilts upward — not because of rates, but because of this number.

Wednesday evening: the rate figure is the least interesting part

Copom & "priced in": Copom is the Brazilian central bank's rate-setting committee — the counterpart to a Fed meeting, held every six to seven weeks. "Priced in" means: if everyone expects the same thing, that expectation is already in the rate. Like a Christmas present you already know the contents of — unwrapping it triggers no reaction anymore. Movement only comes from a surprise.

The 280th meeting runs Aug 4 and 5, with the decision coming Wednesday evening after the market close. The Selic has stood at 14.25% since June; nearly 80% of the market expects the move to 14.00%, exactly the Focus median for year-end. Translation: exactly one cut, then a standstill.

That makes the number itself the weakest piece of information all evening. What matters is the wording: if the communiqué says, in effect, "that was the last one," Brazil's rate advantage is locked in for the coming quarters. If it leaves the door open, the math flips immediately. And anyone cutting rates with inflation above five percent and a first-half deficit of this size has to justify it — the justification matters more than the move itself.

Even afterward, the real rate stays extraordinary: a Selic of 14.00% against the Focus inflation expectation of 5.12% works out to a real yield of roughly 8.4%, down from 8.7% today. Still near the top internationally — and the real reason capital stays in the country.

It's Sunday, August second. Nothing moves in the currency markets today — Friday's rate is still the last valid one. Even so, this brief has two things to tell you that got lost on Friday, and one of them is a correction to my own writing from yesterday.

Let's start there. The European Central Bank's reference rate is captured every business day around a quarter past two in the afternoon, Central European time. That's a quarter past nine in the morning in Brasília — just after trading opens, not at the end of the day. Yesterday, this space said the real carried the entire Friday move and that no clean trigger could be found. The reason is simple: I was looking on the wrong day.

Brazilian sources actually report the opposite of what the fix shows for Friday: the real was weaker at the close than it had been in the morning. How much weaker, the sources don't agree. InfoMoney, citing Reuters, puts the close at five point zero six eight six per dollar, a gain of zero point one three percent for the dollar. Rio Times puts it at five point zero seven nine three, with the real down zero point four percent. Both paths point back to a Thursday close of around five point zero six — so the real's big rally actually happened Thursday afternoon, after the fix, which is why it only showed up in the European series on Friday.

For Daniel, that has a very practical consequence, with an honest asterisk. Converting Brazil's actual Friday close into euros gives, depending on the source, either five point eight two one or five point eight three four — that's zero point two to zero point four percent above the official fix of five point eight zero nine five. So Monday should open mechanically higher, without anything new needing to happen — exactly how much higher can't be pinned down today. Part of the reason is likely that the Brazilian exchange had a system outage that Friday: regular trading didn't resume until half past noon, and the interest-rate futures market stayed shut all morning. Two sources could easily have caught different moments as the close. Even so, I'm raising Monday's forecast: a range of five point eighty to five point eighty-nine, most likely five point eighty-four — though with lower confidence, because in the worst case only half of the catch-up move survives. Anyone checking the fix over the weekend and concluding the rate fell, so it's worth waiting, is looking at a number the market has already moved past either way.

The second story comes out of Brasília. On Friday afternoon, Brazil's finance ministry reported that the federal government posted a primary deficit of forty-eight point two billion reais in June. For the first half of the year, that adds up to ninety-two point two billion reais, or roughly sixteen billion euros — and that's before a single interest payment is even counted. Primary deficit means spending minus revenue, with interest on existing debt not yet in the picture.

The currency market simply didn't trade that news. It's pricing the rate advantage and tuning out the budget. Short term, that's rational: as long as the policy rate sits at fourteen point two five percent, waiting pays more than the fiscal risk costs from one day to the next. That posture doesn't survive a campaign, though, and that's exactly why my three-to-six-month scenario tilts higher — not because of interest rates, but because of this number.

On Wednesday evening, Brazil's rate committee meets. Almost eighty percent of the market expects a quarter-point cut to fourteen percent. That cut is fully priced in and, by itself, will move nothing. What matters is the reasoning: anyone cutting rates with inflation above five percent and a deficit this size has to explain why. If the central bank signals that was the last cut, the real keeps its tailwind. If it leaves the door open, that flips fast.

A quieter point in the real's favor comes from that same central bank — as background, not news. Bloomberg Línea reported back on February twenty-third that the bank's stock of so-called FX swap contracts, the dollar insurance it sells to the market, was heading below a hundred billion dollars, for the first time since May twenty twenty-two. That report is five months old, and I couldn't find a more recent dated reading on this same figure. A central bank pulling back its coverage is implicitly betting it won't be needed — whether that's continued since February, I don't know. What is current: the market's insurance against a Brazilian sovereign default, at a hundred twenty-five point nine basis points at the week's close, conspicuously left unchanged three months before the election.

One line on stocks, so Friday's number is read correctly. The Ibovespa gained zero point four seven percent to a hundred seventy-seven thousand nine hundred ninety-nine points, but virtually the entire gain came from a single stock: Santander Brasil jumped thirteen point three percent. Not because of good earnings — because of a takeover.
