Both sides pushing at once

FX in detail — breaking down Monday's jump

Cross rate: EUR/BRL isn't its own market — it's a product of two others: EUR/USD times USD/BRL. Like a translation through a pivot language — German to Portuguese goes through English. That's why EUR/BRL can rise even though nothing happened in Brazil: it's enough for the euro to move against the dollar.

EUR/BRL sits 0.84% above Friday's fix — and that 0.84% splits almost exactly in half between the two legs:

EUR/USD 1.1485 → 1.1534 (+0.43%)× USD/BRL 5.0583 → 5.0764 (+0.36%)= EUR/BRL +0.79%

The arithmetic lands at 5.8552; the quote is 5.8582. The 0.05% gap is timestamp noise between sources. What matters more is what's inside the breakdown: the dollar is broadly weaker today — the Dollar Index falls 0.22%. By the textbook, that should help the real: when the world's reserve currency softens, money flows into higher-yielding emerging markets. Today, the opposite happens.

My read, explicitly as interpretation: the trigger isn't in the FX market but in the oil market. Brent fell 5.31% in a single day, and crude is Brazil's single largest export item. That hits the currency through two channels at once.

Terms of trade: the price ratio between what a country sells and what it buys. Like a kid trading cards: if your own cards drop in value, you get less back for the same stack. When the oil price falls, Brazil earns fewer dollars per barrel exported — fewer dollars flow into the country, and demand for the real weakens.
Brent −5.3%→ fewer export dollars→ real weaker
Brent −5.3%→ inflation pressure eases→ Copom can cut more easily→ rate gap narrows→ real weaker

The second channel is the more uncomfortable one, because it works over a longer horizon. For the exchange: €100 bought R$580.95 at Friday's fix, R$585.82 on Monday morning — nearly R$5 more, over three calendar days without a single full trading day in between.

Positioning: one number, many gaps

CDS (Credit Default Swap): insurance against a country defaulting on its debt. The price in basis points is what the market charges annually for that protection — 125.90 means 1.259% of the insured amount per year. Like bike insurance: if the neighborhood gets rougher, the premium rises before the first bike is even stolen.

Brazil's five-year CDS sits at 125.90 basis points (Jul 31 close, −0.26% on the day) — remarkably calm for a country three months from a presidential election. And it argues against the idea that the real is weakening on political nerves: if this were election jitters, that premium would move with it. It isn't. That supports my oil explanation — a commodity shock, not a country-risk shock.

The oil market reversed direction in four days

On Jul 31, Brent traded at $87.93 a barrel (+1.21% on the day), the provisional peak of a rally of more than 20% within a month. On Aug 3, the same contract stands at $83.26 — down 5.31% in a single day, roughly 8% on the week.

Why a strait sets the price of oil: a fifth of the world's traded oil passes through the Strait of Hormuz, between Iran and Oman. Like the only bridge across a river: as long as it's open, nobody thinks about it; the moment someone threatens to close it, every crossing gets more expensive. The price, in other words, isn't trading scarcity — it's trading fear of scarcity.

That fear premium is exactly what's unwinding now. The ceasefire brokered in April had effectively collapsed by July, and shipping through the strait dropped at times to under ten vessels a day. When the price now gives up 5% in a day, the market is pricing in less escalation — not more oil.

My view: for Brazil, this is the uncomfortable direction. The honest counterargument: the country imports refined products, and cheaper fuel eases the consumer price index and, with it, real incomes. Both are true — it's just that the purchasing-power effect plays out over quarters, while the terms-of-trade effect lands in the exchange rate immediately.

Wednesday evening: oil supplies the alibi

Copom & "priced in": Copom is the Brazilian central bank's rate-setting committee, the counterpart to a Fed meeting. "Priced in" means: if everyone expects the same thing, that expectation is already in the rate — like a Christmas present you already know the contents of. Movement only comes from a surprise.

The 280th meeting runs Aug 4 and 5, with the decision coming Wednesday evening after the market close. The Selic has stood at 14.25% since Jun 17 — the third straight quarter-point cut. The Focus median sees 14.00% by year-end: exactly one more step, then a standstill.

What's changed since yesterday: an oil price that gives up 8% in a week works its way, with a lag, into fuel prices and from there into the IPCA. That hands the central bank a convenient reference to "easing cost pressure" — and makes it easier not to declare the cycle over. That's the unfavorable variant for the real, which is why I consider the communiqué riskier than a week ago.

Even after that, the real rate stays extraordinary: a Selic of 14.00% against the Focus inflation expectation of 5.12% works out to a real yield of roughly 8.4%, down from 8.7% today — still near the top internationally, and the real reason capital stays in the country.

It's Monday, August third. The catch-up move this space walked through yesterday has shown up — and it's bigger than my point estimate. EUR/BRL is trading around five point eight five eight this Monday morning, that's zero point eight four percent above Friday's official reference rate of five point eight zero nine five from the European Central Bank.

What's interesting isn't the size of that number, it's what's inside it. EUR/BRL isn't its own market — it's built from two others, the euro against the dollar and the dollar against the real. And today both legs are pushing the same way. The euro gained zero point four three percent against the dollar, and the real gave up zero point three six percent versus Friday morning. Add those together and you get roughly zero point eight percent — the small remainder is just timestamp noise between data sources.

What's unusual is that both are happening at once. The dollar is broadly weaker today — the Dollar Index is down zero point two two percent, to around ninety-nine point seven. By the textbook, that should help the real: when the world's reserve currency softens, money typically looks for higher-yielding emerging markets. Today, the opposite is happening.

My explanation for that — and this is explicitly my interpretation, not something a source is telling me — sits in the oil market. Brent crude was trading at eighty-seven point nine three dollars a barrel on July thirty-first, the peak of the Hormuz panic. Today the same contract sits at eighty-three point two six dollars. That's down five point three percent in a single day and roughly eight percent on the week. Brazil is a net oil exporter, and crude is its single largest export item.

An oil-price crash hits the real through two channels, and both point the same way. First, the country earns fewer dollars per barrel exported, so fewer dollars flow into Brazil and demand for the real weakens. Second, inflation pressure eases, because cheaper fuel eventually shows up in consumer prices. That makes it easier for the central bank to cut, and every cut narrows the rate advantage the real depends on. The second channel is the more uncomfortable one, because it plays out over a longer stretch.

This isn't a coincidence anymore, it's a pattern: I too often bet on reverting to the recent average and underestimate that a move in a trend tends to keep going. So today I'm deliberately placing my point estimates in the upper half of the ranges. For today, I expect five point eighty-three to five point eighty-nine, most likely five point eighty-six.

Wednesday evening, Brazil's rate committee meets for its two-hundred-eightieth session. The policy rate has stood at fourteen point two five percent since June seventeenth, and a further quarter-point cut to fourteen percent is expected. That cut is fully priced in and won't move anything by itself. What's new is the justification: the collapsed oil price hands the central bank a convenient argument about easing cost pressure — and makes it easier not to declare the cutting cycle over. That would be the bad variant for the real, which is why I consider Wednesday's statement riskier than it looked a week ago.

There's a quieter point moving the other way, out of Brasília. Deputy treasury secretary David Athayde said in late July that the government is reviewing whether to tighten the growth cap on mandatory spending, which today is allowed to run up to two point five percent above inflation. He gave no number and no timeline; some reports mention a range of one point five to two percent. The target remains a primary surplus of zero point two five percent of GDP, roughly thirty-four billion reais. I read this as a signal to the market, not a bill in progress — but it's the first move in months pointing in the right direction.

One thing that argues against a nervousness story: the market's insurance against a Brazilian sovereign default sits unchanged at a hundred twenty-five point nine basis points, remarkably calm three months before the presidential election. If the real were weak because of election jitters, that premium would be moving with it. It isn't. To me, that's the strongest argument that we're looking at a commodity shock today, not a country-risk one.
