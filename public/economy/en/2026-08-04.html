The fix says: it was two-thirds the euro

FX in detail — yesterday's breakdown, corrected

ECB reference rate ("fix"): every business day around 14:15 Central European Time — about 09:15 in Brasília — the European Central Bank polls the major banks for a rate and publishes it as the official daily anchor. Like a class photo: one moment, captured for the whole day. The market keeps moving afterward, the anchor stays put. That's why a live morning quote is never the same as "Monday's rate."

Update since Mon Yesterday this space cited a live spot of 5.8582 and, from that, the claim that the jump split "almost evenly" between euro strength and real weakness. The fix is in now, and it says something different: EUR/BRL 5.8454, that's +0.62% instead of +0.84%. Cleanly broken down on a fix basis, it looks like this:

EUR/USD 1.1485 → 1.1535 (+0.43%)× USD/BRL 5.0583 → 5.0675 (+0.18%)= EUR/BRL +0.62%

Friday's EUR/USD value is back-calculated from the two fixings (5.8095 / 5.0583); Monday's was pulled independently and matches exactly. Result: roughly 70% of the move comes from the euro leg, 30% from the real leg. The real was thus considerably less weak than Monday morning suggested — the spot of 5.0764 sat above what the day ultimately delivered.

Why this is more than nitpicking: if the euro is the main driver, Daniel's exchange rate hinges less on Brasília than on Frankfurt and Washington. A Copom decision then only feeds through by about a third — the rest gets decided on the Dollar Index.

In practical exchange terms: €100 bought R$580.95 at Friday's fix, R$584.54 at Monday's — R$3.59 more. For context: anyone exchanging in January got R$637.43. The 12-month low is 5.8103 (May 4) — the current rate sits only 0.6% above it, so for someone earning in euros, it's still close to the worst level in over a year.

Forecast test: too high this time

Yesterday this space called Monday "5.83–5.89, most likely 5.86," explicitly shifted upward because I'd been too low three days running. The fix landed at 5.8454: range held, point 0.25% too high.

Methodology error Two-thirds of this error isn't a forecasting error, it's a yardstick error: I derived the point from a live spot that already sat above what the fix would later show. Consequence from today on: point estimates get set against the last fix and settled against the next fix — not against the morning spot. I'm walking back the upward overcorrection; the points sit near the middle of the range again.

Risk premium falls while the real gives up ground

CDS (Credit Default Swap): insurance against a country defaulting on its debt. The price in basis points is what the market charges annually for that protection — 122.74 means 1.2274% of the insured amount per year. Like bike insurance: if the neighborhood gets rougher, the premium rises before the first bike is even stolen.

Brazil's five-year CDS closed on Aug 3 at 122.74 basis points. Yesterday this space still had Jul 31's value, 125.90 — so the premium has fallen, while the real gave up ground against the dollar. As a rough trend line: in early July, the same premium stood at around 126 basis points, per MacroMicro. The two sources are a month apart, so they're only good for direction, not a day-to-day comparison.

My read: this is the strongest available counterargument to the "the real is election-jittery" thesis. If it were political fear, the default insurance would have to move with it. It does the opposite. Together with the breakdown above, a consistent picture emerges: the real is currently a passenger, not a driver.

The Brazil ETF EWZ closed Aug 3 at $36.42 (−0.63%, $9.25B in fund assets) — a mild dip, not a flight. Fund flows weren't listed on the page.

Not available: the BCB's swap stock again couldn't be pulled with a clean date — all that's known is that the central bank starts rolling over, from Aug 5, contracts maturing Sep 1, which says nothing about the stock itself. Also out of reach: the CFTC COT breakdown by trader type and the DI curve (DI1F27/DI1F29). Copom tomorrow night: the 21% is the story

Market-implied probability: options on the Copom decision trade on Brazil's B3 exchange. Their prices can be worked backward to show how likely traders consider each outcome — like betting odds in soccer: when the odds are low, the market firmly believes in the win. "75.5%" means: three out of four real staked sit on "a quarter-point cut."

The 280th Copom meeting runs today and tomorrow; the decision comes Wednesday evening after the market close. The Selic stands at 14.25%. The B3 options price in (as of Aug 3):

  • 75.5% — a 25-basis-point cut to 14.00%
  • ~21% — unchanged at 14.25%
  • 2.3% — a 50-basis-point cut to 13.75%

Correction to yesterday: this space said the cut was "fully priced in." That was too strong. 75.5% is a clear majority, but one in five units staked sits on hold — enough for a real surprise. Early June, incidentally, had the ratio reversed: back then, hold was the base case at around 75%. That reversal in two months is a signal in itself: the market has turned its view of Brazil's inflation path around.

Selic −25 bp→ rate gap smaller→ real less attractive→ EUR/BRL higher

What I'm watching: not the number, but the closing line of the communiqué. An expected cut with the message "that was the last step" is better for the real than an expected cut with an open door. And because the announcement comes after the market close, Daniel won't see the reaction until Thursday's fix at the earliest.

Oil: the crash holds only halfway

Brent trades on Aug 4 at $85.01 a barrel, +1.48% versus the day before. On Aug 3, the same contract had fallen 4.68% to $83.82 — attributed to easing tensions around the Strait of Hormuz. Part of that plunge has thus been recovered within 24 hours.

More important than the day's move is the bigger picture: on the month, Brent still stands at +18.1%, on twelve months at +25.7%. The fear premium hasn't disappeared, then — it just twitched for a day.

My read, explicitly as interpretation: this weakens, rather than strengthens, yesterday's oil-driven explanation for the real's softness. If Brent already bounces back the very next day and the real still doesn't firm up again, the commodity channel probably wasn't the whole story. Together with the breakdown — only a third of the jump even came from the real — there's less left to explain than it seemed yesterday.

It's Tuesday, August fourth. The official reference rate for Monday is in, and it corrects yesterday's picture on two counts. EUR/BRL sits in the European Central Bank's fixing at five point eight four five four. Against Friday's five point eight zero nine five, that's zero point six two percent — yesterday, based on a live quote, this space had put it at zero point eight four percent.

The second correction matters more. Yesterday, the story was that the jump split almost evenly between a stronger euro and a weaker real. Worked out properly with the fixings, that's not quite right. The euro gained zero point four three percent against the dollar, while the real gave up only zero point one eight percent against the dollar. That's roughly seventy percent euro and thirty percent real. Monday morning's live quote had overstated how weak the real actually was.

There's a lesson here about my own method. For Monday, I'd called a range of five point eighty-three to five point eighty-nine, with five point eighty-six as the most likely point. The range held, the point came in zero point two five percent too high. But two-thirds of that miss isn't a forecasting error, it's a yardstick error: I derived the point from a morning quote that was already sitting above what the fixing would later show. From today on, I'm setting point estimates against the last reference rate and settling them against the next one.

The real event this week is tomorrow evening. Brazil's rate-setting committee meets today and tomorrow and announces its decision after the market closes. The policy rate stands at fourteen point two five percent. Options traded on Brazil's stock exchange put a price on exactly that decision, and from those prices you can read what the market expects: seventy-five point five percent for a quarter-point cut to fourteen percent, around twenty-one percent for no change, two point three percent for a half-point cut.

A correction is due here too. Yesterday, this space said the cut was fully priced in. That was too strong. Seventy-five percent is a clear majority, but one in five units staked is riding on a hold. That's enough room for a real surprise. And the backstory is notable: in early June, the ratio was exactly reversed, with a hold seen as the base case at around seventy-five percent. The market has completely flipped its view of Brazil's inflation path in two months.

Meanwhile, the oil-market explanation from yesterday is crumbling. Brent had fallen four point seven percent on Monday, to eighty-three point eight dollars a barrel, which this space read as the main reason for the real's weakness. Today the same contract sits at eighty-five point zero one dollars, up one point four eight percent. Part of that plunge has been recovered within a single day, and on the month, Brent is still up eighteen percent. If the oil price bounces back and the real still doesn't firm up, the commodity channel probably wasn't the whole story.

One detail supports that skepticism. The market's insurance against a Brazilian sovereign default fell on Monday to one hundred twenty-two point seven basis points, down from one hundred twenty-five point nine at the end of July. So the risk premium is falling even as the currency weakens. That continues to argue against the idea that the real is suffering from election jitters — and for the idea that it's currently mostly a passenger of the dollar-euro relationship.

The uncomfortable number of the day comes from the fiscal data. Brazil's gross debt rose in June to eighty-one point nine percent of economic output, up from eighty-one point one percent in May. That's the highest level since October twenty twenty-one. Not a jump, but a steady climb over months — and that's exactly the problem, because a jump can be explained by a one-off effect, a trend can't. On the primary balance for this year, meanwhile, two irreconcilable numbers are circulating: a government target of zero point two five percent surplus and a forecast of zero point four percent deficit. I couldn't trace either one to a dated primary source today, so I'm placing them side by side instead of picking one.

For today, I expect a range of five point eighty-two to five point eighty-nine, most likely five point eighty-five. Two forces largely cancel out: the Dollar Index climbed back to around one hundred overnight, which pushes the euro down and with it the rate, but at the same time lifts the dollar-real rate and pushes the rate back up. Every probability in this brief is my own assessment, with one exception: the seventy-five point five percent for the rate cut is a real market price.

Tomorrow's brief comes out before the decision; the reaction to it won't show up in the rate until Thursday. Until then, it's worth watching the Dollar Index — as long as it clings to the hundred mark, Washington is deciding more about Daniel's exchange rate than Brasília is.
