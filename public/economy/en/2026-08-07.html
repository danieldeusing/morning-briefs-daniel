The Fix Stood Still, the Afternoon Didn't

FX in detail — where we stand and why

ECB reference rate ("fix"): Every business day around 14:15 Central European time — about 09:15 in Brasília — the European Central Bank polls major banks for a rate and publishes it as the official daily anchor. Think of a class photo: one moment, frozen for the whole day. Reading the fix isn't reading "Thursday" — it's reading a quarter hour of it.

Update since Thu Between Wednesday's and Thursday's fix, Daniel's rate rose from 5.9102 to 5.9138 — +0.06%. After the prior day's 1.02% jump, that's a standstill. The breakdown explains why:

USD/BRL 5.1153 → 5.1237 (+0.16%)× EUR/USD 1.1554 → 1.1542 (−0.10%)= EUR/BRL +0.06%

Both lost against the dollar, and almost nothing was left in the cross rate. That's the normal case and the reason Daniel's rate is calmer than the headlines: as long as a move is broadly dollar-driven, it eats itself up inside EUR/BRL. It only gets dangerous when the real falls on its own — like on Wednesday.

And then came the afternoon. At 20:52 UTC — 17:52 in Brasília, practically at the Brazilian market close — XE reported a mid-rate of 5.8893, 0.41% below the morning fix. Backed out through EUR/USD, that implies a USD/BRL of around 5.10 instead of 5.1237: the real clawed back in Thursday trading what it had given up in the morning, and then some. The timing lines up with the oil rally (see right). A cleanliness note: the fix and the spot are different measurement points from different sources — the reliable part is the direction, not the third decimal. And it points down.

In practical exchange terms: 100 euros bought R$591.38 at Thursday's fix, 36 centavos more than Wednesday — but only R$588.93 at the evening spot rate. The ECB daily series' trailing 12-month low sits at 5.7449 (May 13), the high at 6.5851 (Dec 23). The current fix stands 2.94% above the low, right at the edge of the bottom fifth of the annual range. Still good ground for a euro seller — but the cushion, which stood at an eighth earlier this week, is used up.

The Risk Premium Is Rising More Slowly

Positioning, sentiment & BR yields

CDS (Credit Default Swap): Insurance against a country defaulting on its debt. The price in basis points shows what the market charges annually for that protection — 121.76 means 1.2176% of the insured amount per year. Like bike insurance: as the neighborhood gets riskier, the premium rises before the first bike is even stolen.

Brazil's five-year CDS closed on Aug 6 at 121.76 basis points, +0.50 bp (+0.41%). The week's series: 122.74 (Aug 3) → 119.42 (Aug 4) → 121.26 (Aug 5) → 121.76 (Aug 6). The second straight rise, but at a quarter of the prior day's pace.

My read: this supports the FX picture. Wednesday's spike was an event, not a regime change — otherwise default insurance would have kept climbing instead of leveling off. Anyone looking to read fiscal panic into the CDS won't find it here — just a market letting the premium sit a few basis points higher while it waits for the Copom minutes.

Not pulled today: BCB swap positions, CFTC COT positioning, the DI futures curve, EWZ fund flows, NTN-B real yields. For the swap positions, the most recent dated value I could verify is about half a year old — so it doesn't appear here; for COT positioning, only open interest was findable, not direction. 09:30 BRT: the Number the Fix Won't See

US jobs report ("Nonfarm Payrolls"): Once a month, the US Department of Labor counts how many jobs were added outside agriculture, and reports the unemployment rate. Like the monthly standings update in a sports league: one number that suddenly makes everyone recalibrate their expectations for the season. For FX, the most important recurring date of the month, because the Fed calibrates its rates around it.

Today's July report lands at 8:30 a.m. New York time — that's 09:30 BRT. The ECB fixing runs at 14:15 Central European time, so 09:15 BRT. Fifteen minutes apart, and they're on the wrong side of each other: the rate Daniel would use to measure today's exchange is taken before the week's most important number even exists. Whatever it triggers only shows up in the Monday fix. Same mechanic as Wednesday's Copom, just predictable this time.

The context makes it sharper: the Fed held at 3.50–3.75% on Jul 29, but three of the twelve voting members — Hammack, Kashkari, and Logan — wanted to hike. A bloc like that only needs a strong jobs report to turn a minority into a majority.

Oil at $84 — and a Correction

Correction Yesterday's brief said "Brent $79.17 (Aug 6), −0.35%," with the read that the slide had stopped but wasn't a rebound. That was wrong. The same source now shows $83.77 for Aug 7, up +1.55% versus the prior day — meaning the prior close was actually around $82.5, not $79.2. The +7.42% monthly change and +25.86% annual change confirm an uptrend. Whether yesterday's figure was stale or a different grade, I can't resolve; so I'm replacing it entirely.

Brent stands at $83.77 a barrel (Aug 7, +1.55% versus the prior day), +7.42% over the month, +25.86% over the year. That's not a side detail — it's the only driver this week pulling EUR/BRL down.

Terms of Trade: The price ratio between what a country sells and what it buys. Like trading cards on a schoolyard: if your cards suddenly become more valuable while everyone else's stay the same price, you get more back for the same card. For currencies: when the ratio improves, more foreign currency flows into the country, and its own currency firms up.

The path, concretely:

Brent +7.4% over the month→ BR: net crude exporter, more export revenue→ EZ: net importer, higher energy bill→ Real firms against the euro

The catch: Brazil exports crude but imports refined products — the net effect stays positive, but smaller than the crude number suggests. And for Copom, expensive oil is a source of inflation, which slows the cutting cycle and keeps the real's rate advantage alive longer. Both effects point the same way: real firmer, EUR/BRL lower. Hence the downward revision to the monthly and 12-month forecasts.

Macro in Brief

  • [US/BR] The US Trade Representative concluded its Section 301 investigation against Brazil on Jul 15 with a 25% tariff on certain imports. Section 301 is the provision in US trade law that lets Washington impose tariffs unilaterally — like a house rule: the host sets it and doesn't have to ask anyone. My take: per reporting, oil and gas, beef, coffee, and oranges are exempt — exactly the categories that bring Brazil foreign currency. I only have these exemptions from news summaries, not the primary text; I'm treating them as likely, not confirmed. If they hold, the exchange-rate risk sits in the danger of escalation, not in the basket of goods.
  • [BR] Three numbers, one budget. The government's report on Jul 24 lowered the 2026 deficit projection from R$60.3 billion to R$52 billion — including court-ordered payments (precatórios). Strip those and the framework exemptions out, and the same government projects a surplus of R$10.8 billion.
    Precatórios: Bills a court has imposed on the state — lost lawsuits that eventually have to be paid. Like a parking ticket that's been sitting in the glove compartment for years: the debt is real, only the payment date keeps slipping. Because they're not plannable, they partly run outside Brazil's spending rule.
    My position stands: it's not the size of the gap that's the problem, it's that only a definition separates deficit from surplus. Markets punish an unclear framework harder than a bad number.
  • [BR/US] The Selic stands at 14.00% after the unanimous cut on Aug 5, the fourth in a row; the market expects the next step on Sep 15/16. The Fed holds at 3.50–3.75% (Jul 29, 9:3) and meets on the same date. As long as Washington and Frankfurt stay still, the move in EUR/BRL comes from Brasília — and lately, from the oil market.

It's Friday, August seventh, and today's brief carries a timing problem worth knowing before you think about exchanging any money.

Start with where things stand. In the European Central Bank's Thursday fix, euro-to-real sits at five point nine one three eight, versus five point nine one zero two on Wednesday. That's a move of just six hundredths of a percent — essentially nothing, after Wednesday's full one percent jump.

Break it down and both currencies actually lost ground to the dollar: the real gave up sixteen hundredths of a percent, the euro gave up ten hundredths of a percent, in the same window. Almost nothing was left over in the cross rate. That's the normal pattern, and it's the opposite of Wednesday, when only the real fell alone.

But then came the afternoon. By evening in Brasília — close to the local market close — the market rate had slipped to five point eight eight nine three, four tenths of a percent below the morning fix. The real clawed back what it had lost that morning, and then some. The fix never saw any of that, because it only measures a quarter hour of the morning.

That same pattern repeats today, only sharper. The fix is taken around a quarter past nine, Brasília time. Fifteen minutes later, at half past nine, the US jobs report for July comes out — the most important recurring number of the month for currency markets. Whatever it triggers won't show up until Monday's fix.

The backdrop makes that gap matter. The Federal Reserve held rates at three and a half to three and three-quarters percent on July twenty-ninth, but three of its twelve voting members wanted to raise them. A strong jobs number is exactly what that camp would need to turn a minority into a majority.

Now, a correction this brief owes its readers. Yesterday it reported Brent crude at seventy-nine dollars and seventeen cents, calling the slide over but not yet a rebound. That was wrong. The same source now shows eighty-three dollars and seventy-seven cents for today, up one and a half percent on the day, seven and four tenths percent over the month, and nearly twenty-six percent over the year. That's a clear uptrend, not a pause.

And that trend matters for the real. Brazil is a net oil exporter, the eurozone a net importer — rising oil improves Brazil's terms of trade and typically firms the real against the euro. It also keeps inflation risk alive for Brazil's central bank, which argues for a slower pace of rate cuts and a longer-lasting rate advantage for the real. Both effects point the same way, which is why this brief is revising its month-ahead and twelve-month forecasts lower.

Risk pricing backs up that calmer picture. Brazil's five-year default insurance closed Thursday at one hundred twenty-one point seven six basis points, up about half a basis point — a second straight rise, but at a quarter of the previous day's pace. That looks like a market absorbing an event, not repricing a new regime.

On the forecast scorecard: yesterday this brief called for a Thursday range of five eighty-nine to five ninety-six, most likely five ninety-three. The fix landed at five point nine one three eight — inside the range, with the point estimate off by just over a quarter percent. A modest miss on direction, a clean hit on the range.

For today, expect a softer range of five eighty-five to five ninety-four, most likely five eighty-nine — the direction turns down not because of fresh news, but because the last reliable market read sits below the last fix. Monday's brief carries the real reckoning: the first fix that actually knows what the US jobs report said.
