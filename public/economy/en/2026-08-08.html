The Fix Closed Before the News Broke

FX in detail — where we stand and why

ECB reference rate ("fix"): every business day around 14:15 Central European time — about 09:15 in Brasília — the European Central Bank polls major banks for a rate and publishes it as the official daily anchor. Think of a class photo: one moment, frozen for the whole day. Reading the fix isn't reading "Friday" — it's reading a quarter hour of it.

Update since Fri Friday's fix came in at 5.8826, −0.53% versus 5.9138 — the sharpest daily drop of the week, and confirmation of what Thursday evening's spot already showed:

USD/BRL 5.1237 → 5.0998 (−0.47%)× EUR/USD 1.1542 → 1.1535 (−0.06%)= EUR/BRL −0.53%

The real gained against the dollar, and the euro lost against that same dollar. Both push the cross rate down, which is why something is left over this time. The rule behind it: a cross rate only moves meaningfully when its two legs aren't working against each other.

The timing is the real point. The fixing ran at ~09:15 BRT, the jobs report landed at 09:30 BRT. The 5.8826 rate contains zero information about the week's most important number — the decline came from Europe, not Washington.

For the exchange in practical terms: 100 euros bought R$588.26 on Friday instead of R$591.38 on Thursday — but over the week, still +1.26% versus R$580.95 on Jul 31. In the ECB daily series' trailing 12-month range (low 5.7449 on May 13, high 6.5851 on Dec 23), Daniel's rate now sits in the bottom sixth, 2.40% above the low: still usable, but no longer good ground.

Currency Up, Stocks Down

Positioning, sentiment & BR yields

CDS (Credit Default Swap): insurance against a country defaulting on its debt; 121.91 basis points means 1.2191% of the insured amount per year. Like bike insurance — as the neighborhood gets riskier, the premium rises before the first bike is even stolen.

Brazil's five-year CDS closed on Aug 7 at 121.91 basis points, +0.15 bp. The week's series: 122.74 (Aug 3) → 119.42 → 121.26 → 121.76 → 121.91. The third straight rise — and by far the smallest.

More interesting is the divergence: the Brazil ETF EWZ closed on Aug 7 at $35.34, −1.31% — roughly −1.8% in real terms, because the currency strengthened. Stocks down, currency up, default insurance flat: the signature of a rate trade, not a growth trade. Someone selling Brazil risk sells currency and stocks together and buys protection. Here the opposite is happening — the market is pricing cheaper money worldwide, not more danger in Brazil.

Not verifiable today, so no estimate: NTN-B/NTN-F real yields (tesourodireto.gov.br only renders in-browser). BCB swaps: only roll activity — daily auctions since Aug 5 for contracts maturing Sep 1 — not a stock figure. CFTC COT: most recent data point is around Jul 14.

Minus 23,000 — What the Number Really Says

Nonfarm Payrolls: once a month, the US Department of Labor counts how many jobs were added outside agriculture. Like the mid-season standings in a sports league: one number that makes everyone recalibrate their expectations for the season. For FX, the most important recurring date of the month.

The US economy lost 23,000 jobs in July instead of gaining the roughly 80,000 expected; the June figure was revised to +20,000. At the same time, the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% from 4.2%.

This contradiction matters. Job losses and a falling unemployment rate only fit together if people are leaving the labor force — those who aren't looking don't count as unemployed. This is a shrinking labor market, not a collapsing one: weak enough for rate cuts, but without the layoff wave of a real recession. For emerging-market currencies, that's the friendliest variant — cheaper money without panic. Wage growth slowing to 3.2% supports that; I only have that figure from news summaries.

The contrast with July 29 is stark: back then the Fed held at 3.50–3.75% — and three voting members (Hammack, Kashkari, Logan) wanted to hike. That position is barely tenable now. Next meeting: Sep 15/16.

The Market Charges for AI Investment

Free cash flow: what's left of revenue after all bills and all investments are paid — like the household budget at month's end, after the new washing machine is paid off too. Harder to dress up than profit.

[US] Meta reported quarterly results on Jul 29: revenue of $60.8 billion (+28%, above consensus) — and the stock still fell 9.64% after hours, from $585.61 to $529.15. The reason is in the cash flow: $31.1 billion in capex ate up almost all of the $31.9 billion in operating cash flow, and free cash flow collapsed from $8.5 billion to $784 million. Earnings per share missed consensus by 13.8%.

[China] On Aug 3, by contrast, Alibaba's Hong Kong shares rose roughly 7% when the company unveiled its new language model. (The technology behind it is covered in the ai-dev brief.)

My read: same industry, two opposite signs. The market no longer pays for AI investment as a growth promise — it treats it as a cost, except where there's a visible product to show for it. For Daniel this is a macro story: a weak labor market plus a market that punishes investment — that's the combination that turns a dollar cycle.

It's Saturday, August eighth, and there's no trading today and no new rate — but there's a number out of America that makes Monday worth watching.

Start with where things stand. The European Central Bank's Friday fix — the last one before the weekend — came in at five point eight eight two six, versus five point nine one three eight on Thursday. That's a drop of just over half a percent, the sharpest daily move of the week. A hundred euros bought five hundred eighty-eight reais and twenty-six centavos, about three reais less than on Thursday.

Broken down, the real gained forty-seven hundredths of a percent against the dollar, and the euro lost an extra six hundredths against that same dollar. Both effects push the cross rate down, so half a percentage point was left over. On Thursday the two legs worked against each other and nearly canceled out. Over the full week, the rate is still up one and a quarter percent. Friday gave back roughly two-fifths of the jump the Brazilian central bank's rate cut had triggered — no more than that.

Now the real news. The US economy lost twenty-three thousand jobs in July. Economists had expected a gain of around eighty thousand. That's not a miss, it's a reversal. At the same time, the unemployment rate fell, from four point two to four point one percent. The contradiction resolves itself this way: people are leaving the labor force, and if you're not looking for work, you don't count as unemployed. That's a shrinking labor market, not a collapsing one.

Timing matters here. The jobs report landed at nine-thirty Brasília time. The ECB fixing runs a quarter hour earlier. Friday's rate contains zero information about the week's most important number — exactly as this page said it would yesterday, and exactly how it played out.

For Monday, two channels are pulling in opposite directions. First: a weak US labor market means lower US interest rates, the dollar falls against everything including the euro, and a stronger euro lifts Daniel's rate. Second: that same effect widens Brazil's interest-rate advantage, strengthens the real, and pushes the rate down. The only hint available favors the first channel: the dollar index fell three-tenths of a percent while the dollar-to-real rate barely moved — the dollar gave ground against the euro, not against the real.

So the call for Monday's fix is a range of five eighty-four to five ninety-six, most likely five ninety. For the month ahead, the most likely point comes down slightly, to five ninety-two. The reason is the same report: the three Federal Reserve members who wanted to raise rates on July twenty-ninth are effectively done for now. That removes a downside risk for the real that was still on the table yesterday.

Two smaller notes round out the picture. Brazil's default insurance rose just fifteen hundredths of a basis point on Friday, to a hundred twenty-one point nine one — a third straight rise, and by far the smallest. Meanwhile the Brazilian stock fund EWZ lost one point three percent even as the currency strengthened. Stocks down, currency up, default insurance flat: that's the signature of a rate trade, not a growth trade. The market is pricing cheaper money worldwide, not more danger in Brazil.

And one more correction to something reported here. Yesterday's brief quoted Brent crude at eighty-three dollars and seventy-seven cents. At the close, the same source showed eighty-two twenty-seven, down just over a quarter percent. Both figures are consistent — the higher one was a mid-morning quote, not the close. The number wasn't wrong; only its label was. Oil is still a tailwind for the real, just a weaker one than yesterday's brief suggested.

Monday brings the first fix that knows all of this. Tuesday brings two Brazilian dates at once: the central bank's meeting minutes and the July inflation reading. Monday's brief will settle the score — and show which of the two channels won out.
