After the Fix, the Real Kept Winning

FX in detail — where we stand and why

There's no new rate on the weekend — but Friday's values are still settling, and that flips yesterday's reading.

Fix vs. closing rate: the ECB reference rate is a snapshot at 14:15 Central European time, about 09:15 in Brasília. The closing rate is the last price of the trading day, many hours later. Like halftime and the final score: same match, two numbers — and whoever only knows the halftime score doesn't know the result.

Update since Sat The fix stood at 5.8826. By the market close, TradingEconomics quoted the dollar at 5.0820 reais (−0.81% on the day instead of −0.47% at the fix) and the euro at 1.1550 dollars:

USD/BRL 5.0820× EUR/USD 1.1550= EUR/BRL 5.8697

That's 0.22% below the fix. In the hours after the jobs report, both legs moved — but unevenly: the real added another 0.35%, the euro only 0.13%. So the rate channel won, not the euro channel. Yesterday this page assumed the opposite, based on a single afternoon quote of 5.0976. The settled close says: wrong call.

No contradiction The ECB fix measures the euro 0.06% weaker on Friday than on Thursday; TradingEconomics measures it 0.22% stronger. Both are correct: the US jobs report sits between them. The fix was taken 15 minutes earlier, the close hours later. Two different times, not two different opinions.

For the exchange in practical terms: 100 euros bought R$588.26 at the fix, only R$586.97 by the market close. In the ECB daily series' monthly window (Jul 8–Aug 7), the low is 5.7775 on Jul 23, the high 5.9138 on Aug 6 — Daniel's rate sits in the upper third of that range, and 1.26% above Jul 31 (5.8095). Whoever waits is betting on the fiscal channel further to the right.

Forecast Test: the Direction Was Wrong

Yesterday this page said, for Monday's fix, "5.84–5.96 (63%), most likely 5.90" — reasoning that the euro channel would take over. The settled Friday close implies 5.8697. The range held, but the point estimate was on the wrong side of the fix: off by 0.51%, roughly R$3 on €100.

What went wrong I took a single quote in the middle of the market reaction as evidence ("USD/BRL practically unchanged"). The close from the same source came in 0.3% lower. The lesson: a quote in the middle of a reaction isn't a signal, it's noise — only the close is reliable, and that doesn't arrive until the next morning at the earliest.

Like a savings account that pays more than the loan used to fund it costs. The catch: if the real falls by more than the difference, the profit disappears. That's why it reacts so sharply when US rate expectations shift.

R$92 Billion — the Worst Half-Year Since 2020

Primary balance: government revenue minus spending, before interest is paid on old debt. Like a household budget without the credit-card interest: whoever is already in the red before interest sees their debt pile grow on its own — even without buying anything new.

Brazil's federal government closed the first half of 2026 with a primary deficit of R$92.227 billion — versus R$11.277 billion in the same half a year earlier, a hole a good eight times as large. June alone brought a R$48.178 billion shortfall, almost 9% more than June 2025 (R$44.216 billion).

The breakdown is the real story. The Treasury and central bank were +R$144 billion in surplus for the half-year, the RGPS pension system −R$236.2 billion in deficit — the difference is exactly the shortfall. The state runs its own accounts at a surplus and the pension system into deficit. Add the growth rates: spending up 12.3% in real terms, revenue up only 5.2% — more than twice as fast. "Real" means after inflation, so genuine growth, not a price illusion.

The Tariff Stack: 25% Becomes 37.5%

Stacking tariffs: two separate US proceedings tax the same crate of coffee twice — the second tariff comes not instead of, but in addition to, the first. Like two fees on the same package: one for oversize, one for express.

[US/BR] Since Jul 15, a 25% tariff has applied from an unfair-trade-practices proceeding; since Jul 23, an additional 12.5% tariff from a forced-labor proceeding; Brazilian diplomats and analysts expect them to add up to roughly 37.5%. That makes Brazil the second-most affected country after China, with an effective average of 18.89%.

The FX channel is slow but steady: fewer exports to the US → fewer dollar inflows → a thinner trade surplus → less natural demand for the real. That doesn't move a daily rate, but it shifts the baseline over quarters — an argument for keeping the 12-month range open to the upside, not a reason to trade differently today.

The lesson is uncomfortable but useful: a quote taken in the middle of a market reaction is noise, not signal. Only the close counts, and that isn't reliable until the next morning at the earliest.

Now the real story of the day — a number this brief hasn't covered before. Brazil's federal government closed the first half of the year with a primary deficit of ninety-two point two billion reais. A year earlier, over the same six months, it was eleven point three billion. The hole is eight times bigger. It's the worst first half since twenty twenty — and that year had a pandemic as an excuse.

The breakdown matters more than the total. The Treasury and the central bank together ran a surplus of a hundred forty-four billion reais. The pension system was two hundred thirty-six point two billion in the red. The gap between those two numbers is exactly the deficit — the government's own accounts are in the black, and pensions are in the red. On top of that, spending grew twelve point three percent after inflation, revenue only five point two — more than twice as fast.

The market hasn't reacted. The real is actually firmer than a week ago. That's not a contradiction, it's a sequence: a half-year report is a statement of fact, not an event, and it doesn't move prices until a date forces the market to price it. That date is October, when Brazil votes.

A second story is moving more slowly, but the same direction. Two US tariffs are now stacked on Brazilian goods: twenty-five percent since mid-July from a trade case, and twelve and a half percent more since July twenty-third from a forced-labor case — together, roughly thirty-seven and a half percent on affected goods. That makes Brazil the second most-tariffed country after China. Fewer exports mean fewer dollars flowing into the country, and less natural demand for the real — it won't move tomorrow's rate, but it shifts the baseline over months.

For the forecast, that adds up to a split view. Short-term, it's friendly for the real: neither the European nor the American central bank meets again until mid-September, and Brazil's not until then either — a month with no rate decisions is a month where the interest-rate advantage of roughly ten points does the work on its own. For Monday's fix, that points to a range of five eighty-three to five ninety-two, most likely five eighty-seven. For the month ahead, five eighty-eight. Three to six months out, the sign flips — that's where the fiscal story takes over, and the most likely point sits at six even.

For comparison, the consensus: the central bank's weekly survey from August third puts the dollar at five twenty reais by year-end. With the euro-dollar rate unchanged, that works out to a rate of about six even — almost exactly my own point estimate, but with a narrower range than these fiscal numbers, in my view, really justify.
