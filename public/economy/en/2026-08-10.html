The Weekend Shifted Nothing

FX in detail — where we stand and why

Friday's market close implied 5.8697. This morning the cross rate stands at 5.8718, the directly quoted spot at 5.8739. Two nights, three numbers, no movement: the real has held onto its gains — and that's the news, not the absence of one.

USD/BRL 5.0838× EUR/USD 1.1550= EUR/BRL 5.8718

Why this is more than a non-event: the Dollar Index is up 0.17% this morning. By the textbook, emerging-market currencies should give way first. The real gave up 0.04%. That's the rate advantage at work.

Rate advantage ("carry"): Brazil pays a 14.00% policy rate, the US 3.50–3.75%. Whoever borrows dollars and invests in reais pockets the difference — a good ten percentage points a year. Like a savings account that pays more than the loan used to fund it costs. The catch: if the real falls by more than that difference, the profit disappears. That's why it can absorb small dollar rallies — and collapses on big ones.
Detail that matters DXY up 0.17%, EUR/USD unchanged at 1.1550 — both at nearly the same timestamp. Since the euro makes up more than half the index basket, the dollar strength arithmetically has to come from the other five currencies. For Daniel, that means today's dollar impulse hits his rate only through the Brazilian leg, not the European one.

For the exchange, concretely: €100 currently buys R$587.39. In the ECB monthly window (Jul 10–Aug 7), the rate ranged between 5.7775 (Jul 23) and 5.9138 (Aug 6) — the spot sits at around 71% of that range. Between the low and the high lies R$13.63 per €100. Whoever exchanges today doesn't catch the high, but does get considerably more than the average.

Correction: the Tariff Stack Was Overstated

Most recently this page said Brazilian goods faced a stack of 25% plus 12.5%, adding up to roughly 37.5%. I fact-checked it — the figure doesn't hold up.

Section 301: a provision in US trade law that lets the government punish another country with tariffs for "unfair trade practices" — without Congress, without the World Trade Organization. Like a shop owner who charges a supplier a surcharge because he thinks they're being dishonest: he doesn't need a court for that, just his own justification.

What's verifiable. A policy tracker that continuously updates US tariffs lists, for Brazil as of Jul 28, exactly one rate: 25% under Section 301, announced Jul 15, effective as of Jul 22, on an import volume of roughly $24 billion, with exemptions for certain food and agricultural products. Three details contradict this brief's earlier version: the date (Jul 22, not Jul 15), the base (a quantified slice of imports, not "most goods"), and the size (no forced-labor surcharge).

What remains open A Brazilian source names 50% for Brazil on Jul 21 — the highest rate in Latin America. But that's not a flat rate on everything: the source describes a 10% baseline, a 40% surcharge tied to Brazil's treatment of US tech firms and unfair trade practices under Section 301, plus sector-specific surcharges that phase in staggered and at different levels: 25% on furniture, ethanol, machinery, footwear, and sugar from Jul 22, 50% on copper from Aug 1, 50% on steel already since Apr 6. The first wave actually matches the tracker's 25%. The same source cites exemptions for beef and orange juice, which matches the tracker's agricultural exemptions. I can't find a US government primary source that reconciles both accounts. So: 25% is the verified lower bound, 50% an unconfirmed upper bound — from today on, I'll write the lower bound.

Why it's worth the effort. The tariff acts on the exchange rate not through headlines, but through trade flows:

graph LR
  A["US tariff on BR goods"] --> B["BR exports to the US fall"]
  B --> C["Fewer dollar inflows"]
  C --> D["Thinner trade surplus"]
  D --> E["Less natural real demand"]
  E --> F["EUR/BRL higher, over quarters"]

How strongly this path pulls depends entirely on the volume affected — 25% on a quantified subset is a different channel than 50% across the board. That's exactly why, next door, I'm pulling back the upper edge of the 3–6-month scenario. A forecast built on an unconfirmed number isn't a forecast, it's an echo.

What still stands: no negotiation track has been reported for Brazil, while Mexico got a 90-day reprieve. That's the unsettling part — not the size of the rate, but the absence of an exit. A tariff with a talking channel is a price risk; a tariff without one is a regime.
