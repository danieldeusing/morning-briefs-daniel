The decisive step is the third one. Had the dollar strengthened globally, the Dollar Index would have to follow suit — it's up 0.04%, effectively flat, and EUR/USD even slipped 0.09%. When the dollar stands still against half the world and gains a full percent against the real, nobody's buying dollars — everybody's selling reais. The parallel move in the Brazil equity fund EWZ (−3.44% the same day) closes the gap: a country exit, not an FX episode.

Risk premium: The extra return investors demand to leave money in an uncertain country. Like the neighbor who lends you his bike: if he hears there's been more theft on your street, he no longer wants five euros, he wants ten. In the FX market, that premium isn't paid in euros — it's paid in a cheaper real.

Forecast check. For yesterday's fix, this page had 5.86–5.93 with a point estimate of 5.89; what came in was 5.8910. The reasoning was wrong anyway: the expectation was a dovish Copom minutes reading and a firmer real heading toward 5.88 — instead it fell all day. The point estimate only held up because the fix is set at 09:15 BRT and the move came afterward. Right number, wrong mechanics; for a brief that sells mechanics, that's the weaker part.

Today at 09:30 BRT: The US Inflation Number

[USA] At 8:30 a.m. New York time — 09:30 BRT — US consumer prices for July land, the first major data point since the Jul 29 Fed meeting.

Heads up The number isn't available at time of writing. So there's no figure here — not even a consensus estimate without a clean primary source. Just the mechanics, not the result.

The context makes it bigger than usual: the Fed held the policy rate at 3.50–3.75% on Jul 29 — but three governors (Hammack, Kashkari, Logan) dissented, specifically for a 25-basis-point hike. A hot July reading would prove exactly those three right.

Dissent at the FOMC: Anyone on the Fed's committee who votes differently is named publicly. Like a family where everyone usually says "yes" to the vacation spot: if three suddenly object loudly, the next decision is pretty likely to go a different way. Three dissents pointing the same direction are the strongest signal short of an actual rate change.
Hot US inflation→Hike expectations→Dollar stronger→Real weaker→EUR/BRL higher

And the timing trap: the ECB fix is set at 09:15 BRT, the number lands at 09:30 BRT — today's fix doesn't see it. Anyone exchanging money this morning is exchanging at a rate that doesn't yet know the day's most important information. That can cut either way; it's not an argument, just a fact about timing.

IPCA: Back in Target, Still Too Hot

[BR] July inflation fell to 4.44% year over year (June: 4.64%) and is back below the 4.50% target ceiling for the first time. That's the good line. The bad one is hiding in the monthly figure: +0.07% against a Reuters poll expectation of +0.03% — more than double.

Central bank target band: Brazil's central bank has an inflation target with tolerance margins — currently 1.50% to 4.50%. Like a speed limit with a grace zone: inside the range it counts as "on plan" and can cut rates, above the ceiling it has to explain itself. The move from 4.64% to 4.44% gives it back that room.

That creates today's contradiction: inflation back inside the target band allows further cuts — and every cut chips away at the rate advantage that carries the real. So good inflation news isn't automatically good currency news for a high-rate currency. Copom cut the Selic unanimously (7-0) by 25 basis points to 14.00% on Aug 5, the fourth cut in a row; the Focus survey sees 13.75% by year-end. The Aug 11 minutes stayed guarded: future decisions would depend on the data.

It's Wednesday, August twelfth. Yesterday the Brazilian real lost more than one percent, and for once that had nothing to do with the dollar — it was an election poll.

The European Central Bank's reference rate for the euro against the real stands at five point eight nine one zero. It was set yesterday at a quarter past nine in the morning, Brasília time. The real kept falling for the rest of the day. This morning the spot rate is running at five point nine six six six, about one point three percent above the reference rate — the highest level since June tenth.

A cross-check through the two underlying legs confirms it. Dollar-to-real times euro-to-dollar comes out to five point nine six seven five — just two hundredths of a percent off the traded rate. So the anchors line up cleanly; the gap to the reference rate is real movement.

Two events triggered it. At nine o'clock, Brazil's July inflation came out. Year over year it fell to four point four four percent, back below the four point five target ceiling for the first time. But the monthly figure rose zero point zero seven percent instead of the expected zero point zero three — more than double. Right after that, the pollster CNT MDA released an election survey: President Lula leads a possible runoff with forty-eight percent against Senator Flávio Bolsonaro's thirty-nine point one percent. Many traders read that as higher fiscal risk and immediately demanded more of a premium.

That the cause sits in Brazil shows up in the Dollar Index. It's essentially flat, up just four hundredths of a percent. And the Brazilian stock fund EWZ lost three point four four percent the same day. When a currency and a stock market fall together while the dollar stands still against half the world, the market is selling a country, not a currency.

For exchanging money, that's the best news in two months. A hundred euros currently buy about five hundred ninety-seven reais. At last month's low, it was just under five hundred seventy-eight. On a five-thousand-euro exchange, that's roughly nine hundred forty-five reais of difference. Today's rate sits above the entire range of the past month.

A word on my own track record. This brief had estimated five point eight nine for yesterday's reference rate, and that's exactly what came in. The reasoning was wrong anyway: the expectation was a firmer real, and instead it weakened. The estimate only worked because the reference rate never even saw the move. Right number, wrong mechanics.

Today that pattern repeats. At half past nine, Brazil time, the United States releases its July inflation figure. The reference rate is set a quarter hour earlier and won't see the number either. This reading matters more than usual, because at the Fed's last meeting in late July, three governors voted for a rate hike. A hot inflation number would prove them right, strengthen the dollar, and weaken the real further. The figure isn't available at time of writing, so deliberately no number appears in this brief.

In the background, the underlying tension remains. Brazil pays a fourteen percent policy rate, the United States three and a half to three and three-quarters. That advantage of a good ten points carries the real every single day. Yesterday it simply proved it's not an airbag.

Tomorrow's brief will mainly answer two questions: how the US inflation number came in, and whether yesterday's election premium stays in the price or unwinds within twenty-four hours.
