The breakdown. Because EUR/BRL is nothing more than USD/BRL times EUR/USD, and all three legs come from the same ECB fixing round, the split is pure arithmetic: EUR/USD 1.1540 → 1.1545 (+0.04%), USD/BRL 5.1049 → 5.1557 (+1.00%). Together, that's the +1.04%. The euro contributed practically nothing; 96% of the move is the real.

And it didn't get better afterward: the tame US number came in, the hike bet fell, stocks rose — and the real kept sliding anyway, to 5.1736 per dollar. What looked like a Tuesday poll reaction is now a two-day exit.

Election premium: the extra return investors demand to keep their money in the country through an uncertain election. Like a landlord getting less rent once construction is announced outside — not because the apartment got worse, but because the noise is priced in.

Forecast check. For yesterday's fix, this page had 5.93–6.00, point estimate 5.96, 64% confidence. What came in was 5.9523: range hit, point estimate 0.13% too high. The more interesting part is the pattern behind it — yesterday's spot at 5.9666 sat exactly 0.24% above the later fix. Applying the same correction to today's spot (5.9916) gives 5.977; that's what the point estimate below is anchored to, rather than a guess.

The US Inflation Number: 3.4% and a Third

[USA] Consumer prices rose 0.1% for the month in July and 3.4% year over year — down from 3.5% in June, in line with expectations. The core rate gained 0.2% for the month and stands at 2.5% year over year.

Core rate: inflation excluding energy and food. Both swing wildly — a cold snap makes tomatoes pricier, a conflict in the Gulf makes gas pricier — and say little about the general price level. Like tossing out the best and worst grade on a class test: what's left shows how the class is really doing. Central banks steer by it because it shows the trend.

The structure is the real story. Energy fell 1.5% for the month — but is still up 14.7% year over year, gasoline even 24.6%. So the oil shock is fully baked into the annual figure and is missing from the monthly figure only because the price recently moved sideways. The biggest single driver was housing: +0.1% for the month, roughly two-thirds of the total increase according to the statistics bureau, +3.2% year over year.

Rate bet (CME FedWatch): on the futures exchange in Chicago, traders bet real money on where the US policy rate stands after the next meeting. Contract prices imply a probability — like a soccer betting line, except banks are placing the bets instead of fans. Not an oracle, but a real-time picture of what the market currently believes.

Following the release, the probability the Fed holds at 3.50–3.75% in September jumped to 61.9% (11:32 a.m. New York time); the hike bet fell to just over 38% and, as the day went on, toward 33%, from around 48% before the number. As recently as Aug 6 — before the July jobs report showing 23,000 jobs cut — the hold bet had stood at just 45%; after the weak number it jumped to around 60%, before easing back to roughly 48–52% by the eve of the CPI print. The S&P 500 rose more than 0.3%.

My view: over the medium term, this is the better news of the day for Brazil, even though yesterday's rate didn't show it. A Fed that doesn't hike leaves the rate advantage of a good ten points intact — and that's the only thing weighing against election jitters.

Oil at $89: Strait of Hormuz vs. the Inventory Pile

[Global] Brent closed at $88.91 a barrel (+$1.19 / +1.4%), the US grade WTI at $83.20 (+1.3%). The market is pricing two stories against each other — the result is a remarkably stable price just under $90.

Supply side: a bottleneck. Only around eight ships a day passed through the Strait of Hormuz on Tuesday — the cited market report puts the pre-war norm at 125 to 140, though other estimates (including IMF PortWatch) put it lower. No direct talks are underway between Tehran and Washington on extending the ceasefire, which expires in roughly five days — though separate, indirect Iran-Oman-US talks on a Hormuz safe-passage deal are reportedly close to done.

Demand side: a pile of inventory. US crude stocks rose by roughly 17 million barrels last week — the largest weekly build since early 2023. OPEC cut its 2026 demand-growth forecast to about 580,000 barrels a day; the IEA expects an outright contraction of roughly 1.6 million barrels for the year, but raised its third-quarter deficit estimate to about 1.8 million barrels.

Inventory build: every week, the US energy agency reports how much crude oil sits in storage tanks. When stocks rise sharply, more was delivered than was processed — like half a pallet of milk still sitting in the supermarket on Saturday night. Normally a price dampener. That the price rises anyway means: the market is more worried about next week's delivery than about today's milk.

Why it matters: Brazil is a net oil exporter, so expensive oil supports the trade balance and the real — but it also drives inflation through fuel and transport, here as in the US. So Washington's all-clear only holds as long as the ceasefire holds. If it breaks, July's tame number becomes moot in August, and the Fed bet reverses within a single meeting.

Brazil's Fiscal Numbers Are Better Than Their Reputation

[BR] The market has been trading a fiscal risk since Tuesday. The most recently published numbers show the opposite: in its budget review of Jul 24, the government cut its estimate for the 2026 primary deficit to R$52 billion (0.38% of GDP), down from R$60.3 billion in May. After allowed exceptions, it even expects a primary surplus of R$10.8 billion, versus R$4.1 billion previously; the spending freeze was eased by R$5.7 billion to R$17.9 billion.

Primary balance: government revenue minus spending, excluding interest on existing debt. Like a household budget that sets the loan payment aside for a moment: it shows whether day-to-day operations pay for themselves.

My view — here I disagree with the price move: the market isn't pricing the fiscal number, it's pricing the fiscal expectation after the election. Legitimate, but a bet on politics, not accounting. Three-week-old numbers don't refute a poll from two days ago — but they're an argument against treating the premium as permanent.

It's Thursday, August thirteenth. US inflation came in tame yesterday, stock markets cheered — and the Brazilian real fell anyway. That's the story of the day.

The European Central Bank's reference rate for the euro against the real stands at five point nine five two three. The day before, it was five point eight nine one zero. That's a gain of one point zero four percent in a single day, and the highest reference rate since June tenth. A cross-check through the two underlying legs lines up to within a thousandth of a percent. The anchors are clean.

More important is where the move came from. Because euro-to-real is nothing more than dollar-to-real times euro-to-dollar, the day splits apart exactly. The euro contributed zero point zero four percent, the real a full percent. So ninety-six percent of the move is Brazilian. It wasn't a dollar event.

At half past nine Brazil time, US consumer prices for July came out. They rose zero point one percent for the month and three point four percent for the year, down from three point five in June. The core rate, which strips out energy and food, stands at two point five percent — the second tame reading in a row.

The bet on a September rate hike from the Federal Reserve then fell from around forty-eight to thirty-three to thirty-eight percent. The probability that the Fed simply holds steady rose to nearly sixty-two percent. The S&P five hundred gained more than zero point three percent, and in Asia, South Korea's Kospi rose four percent.

That's exactly the setup in which emerging-market currencies usually ride along. The real didn't. The reason shows up in the Dollar Index: it measures the dollar against a basket of six major currencies and today stands at ninety-nine point nine seven, slightly higher than the day before. The dollar didn't translate the all-clear into weakness, so the real lacked a tailwind. After the reference rate was set, it kept sliding, to five point one seven three six per dollar.

In oil, the market is pricing two stories against each other right now. Brent closed at eighty-eight point nine one dollars, up one point four percent. Only around eight ships a day passed through the Strait of Hormuz on Tuesday, instead of the usual hundred twenty-five to hundred forty. At the same time, US crude stocks rose by roughly seventeen million barrels, the largest weekly build since early twenty twenty-three. The ceasefire between Tehran and Washington expires in roughly five days, with no direct talks underway on an extension — though separate, indirect Iran-Oman-US talks on a Hormuz safe-passage deal are reportedly close to done.

A word on Brazil's fiscal position, because it contradicts the rate move. In its review on the twenty-fourth of July, the government cut its estimate for this year's primary deficit to fifty-two billion reais, down from sixty point three billion previously. After allowed exceptions, it even expects a surplus of ten point eight billion. So the market isn't trading the number — it's trading a guess about politics after October's election.

For exchanging money, that's still the best window of the month. A hundred euros buy five hundred ninety-five reais at yesterday's reference rate. At the month's low point, it was just under five hundred seventy-eight. On a five-thousand-euro exchange, that's a difference of roughly eight hundred seventy-four reais. A good window, not a historic one — in January, the rate stood at six point three seven.

For today, I expect the reference rate to land between five point nine four and six point zero two, most likely around five point nine eight. Overnight, the spot rate stood at five point nine nine one six, and yesterday the spot rate ran exactly zero point two four percent above the later reference rate. That correction is baked in. Tomorrow's brief will mainly answer two things: whether today's reference rate finally translates the US all-clear into a firmer real, and what becomes of the expiring ceasefire in the Gulf.
