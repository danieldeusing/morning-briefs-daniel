The Essentials in 30 Seconds

Three sessions in a row the real has weakened without the dollar getting stronger. Thursday's ECB reference rate printed 5.9759 — a 30-day high — and the arithmetic leaves no room for a dollar story: the real leg did more than the entire move, the euro leg worked against it. The driver is domestic: an election seven weeks out, a rate-cut path that just got shorter, and a big bank stepping off the trade. For Daniel's exchange, this is the best window of the month.

EUR/BRL is nothing more than USD/BRL multiplied by EUR/USD, and all three legs come from the same ECB fixing round — so Thursday's move splits apart exactly:

Read the signs. The euro leg was negative — a marginally firmer dollar, which alone would have pulled EUR/BRL down. It got overwhelmed five times over by the real. Strip the euro's slip out and the fix prints around 5.982. There is no version of Thursday in which this is a dollar event.

Idiosyncratic move: a currency going its own way instead of following the global tide. Picture a harbour at low tide — every boat drops. If one boat drops while the tide is coming in, the water isn't the problem; that boat has a hole. This morning's Dollar Index is the incoming tide (99.87, −0.09%), and the real is still sinking.

Forecast check. This page called Thursday's fix at 5.94–6.02, point 5.98, 62% confidence. It printed 5.9759: range hit, point 0.07% high. The method — overnight spot minus the roughly 0.24% premium spot carries over the later fix — has held two days running. It works because the trend is one-directional; on a turn day it overshoots.

JPMorgan Steps Off Brazil — and the Rate Followed Two Days Later

[BR] On Aug 11 JPMorgan cut Brazilian equities to Neutral from Overweight and trimmed its year-end Ibovespa target to 185,000 points from 190,000. Three reasons: a slower-than-expected rate-cut path, rising uncertainty into the October elections, and a decelerating economy and credit cycle. On the day of that note the dollar traded at 5.11; two sessions later it closed at 5.192.

Overweight → Neutral: a bank's recommendation on how much of a market a portfolio should hold versus its benchmark. Overweight means "hold more Brazil than the index says", Neutral means "hold exactly the index share". Not a sell — the bank taking its thumb off the scale. Large funds rebalance against these calls, so flow follows the language.

The same note carries what matters more for the currency than for stocks: JPMorgan now expects just one more 25 bp cut in September, then a pause into Q2 2027. The central bank's economist poll agrees — the year-end Selic median is trimmed to 13.75% from 14.00%.

My view: read literally, the downgrade is mildly positive for the exchange rate — fewer cuts means the rate advantage survives longer. The market traded the headline instead of the rate path, which says the driver is election risk, not carry. And positioning built on a vote seven weeks out carries an expensive holding cost at 14% funding.

Selic 14.00% — and the Interest Bill Underneath

[BR] Copom cut the Selic 25 bp to 14.00% on the evening of Aug 5 — unanimous, 7–0, no dissent and no vote for a bigger step. Against the Fed's 3.50–3.75% that's a gap of roughly 10.4 points; against the ECB's 2.25% deposit rate, 11.75.

Carry: borrow where money is cheap, park it where interest is high, pocket the difference. Like buying bread in one town at €1 and selling it in the next at €1.12 — real profit, as long as the road doesn't wash out. Here the road is the exchange rate: an 11-point interest advantage is wiped out by an 11% fall in the real. That's how a currency with enormous carry still sells off.

The uncomfortable number sits one layer down: Brazil's nominal deficit is running near 10% of GDP over the twelve months through June — and around nine of those ten points is interest cost, not spending.

Nominal vs. primary deficit: the primary balance is revenue minus spending before interest — the day-to-day household budget. The nominal deficit adds interest on existing debt. Brazil's household budget nearly breaks even; the credit-card bill on old debt blows the hole. Which is why a Selic cut does more for the fiscal picture than any spending freeze.

My view: this is simultaneously the strongest argument for more cuts and against them. Cut too slowly and the interest bill compounds; cut too fast into an election and the currency pays. The unanimous quarter-point is the "neither" answer — and the reason the rate path is now the market's biggest disagreement.

Oil at $87: the Bottleneck Stopped Getting Worse

[Global] Brent traded near $87.06 a barrel on Friday, essentially unchanged — and exactly where the US Energy Information Administration recently put its 2026 average forecast, raised to $87 from $82 a month earlier. The shock is now fully absorbed into expectations. The physical picture hasn't improved: roughly eight vessels a day cross the Strait of Hormuz against a pre-war average near 120, per maritime intelligence firm Kpler, and the US–Iran memorandum of Jun 17 collapsed over route disputes.

Why this sits in an FX brief: it's the reason the ECB is the hawk. Expensive energy pushed it into June's first hike since 2023 and keeps September on the table — and a hiking ECB lifts the euro leg of Daniel's rate directly. For Brazil the effect is two-sided: higher crude helps a net exporter's trade balance but feeds fuel inflation and constrains Copom. Right now the euro side is the one paying.

What the tension tells me: credit and currency are pricing different things. In a solvency story CDS widens — it's tightening. Two Central Banks, Two Directions

  • [EU] ECB held all three key rates on Jul 23 — deposit 2.25%, main refi 2.40%, marginal lending 2.65% — after June's surprise hike, its first since 2023. Statement: "the full inflationary impact of the energy shock has yet to play out." Lagarde: "forward guidance is not currently in the cards." Next call Sep 10, base case 2.50%.
  • [USA] Fed held at 3.50–3.75% on Jul 29 — fifth straight hold, three members dissenting for a hike.
  • [USA] Then the data cooled: July consumer prices 3.4% year over year (June: 3.5%), core 2.5% (released Aug 12); on Aug 13 producer prices undershot and jobless claims printed 209,000 against a 202,000 forecast.

The asymmetry is the trade. Europe is pushed toward tightening by an energy shock it imports; America is released from it by cooling domestic prices. If both hold, the euro gains on the dollar — and with the real weakening against the dollar on its own account, Daniel's rate gets pushed up from both ends. That, not any single Brazilian headline, is why the monthly and quarterly tiers lean the way they do.

It's Friday, August fourteenth. For the third session running, the Brazilian real has weakened without the dollar getting any stronger. That's the story of the day, and it changes what the number means.

The European Central Bank's reference rate for the euro against the real stands at five point nine seven five nine, set on Thursday. The day before it was five point nine five two three. That's a gain of zero point four percent and the highest fix in the last thirty days. The cross-check through the two underlying legs lands on exactly the same number, so there's no source conflict to report.

What's interesting is where the move came from. Euro-to-real is simply dollar-to-real multiplied by euro-to-dollar. The dollar leg rose zero point four nine percent. The euro leg fell zero point one zero percent, which on its own would have pushed the rate down. So the real did more than the entire move, and the euro worked against it. Without that euro slip, the fix would have printed around five point nine eight two.

This morning the Dollar Index sits at ninety-nine point eight seven, down zero point zero nine percent. That index measures the dollar against a basket of six major currencies. When it falls, emerging-market currencies like the real usually benefit. The real hasn't caught that tailwind for three sessions. When the global driver points one way and the local currency goes the other, the cause is local.

The local cause has a name and a date. On Tuesday, JPMorgan cut Brazilian equities to Neutral from Overweight and lowered its year-end target for the Ibovespa to one hundred eighty-five thousand points from one hundred ninety thousand. The reasons: the October election, a slower path of interest-rate cuts, and a decelerating credit cycle. On the day of that note the dollar traded at five point one one. Two sessions later it closed at five point one nine two, the strongest dollar close since the second of July.

There's a detail that contradicts the panic. Brazil's five-year credit default swap, essentially the annual insurance premium against a government default, stands at one hundred twenty-four basis points. In early July it was one hundred twenty-six. So insurance has become cheaper while the currency was losing ground. What's being sold is the currency, not Brazilian risk in general. That looks like hedging an election, not fleeing a country. And election hedges expire.

On the interest-rate side, Copom cut the Selic to fourteen percent on the fifth of August, unanimously. Against the Federal Reserve that leaves an advantage of roughly ten and a half points, and against the European Central Bank nearly twelve. The uncomfortable number sits underneath: Brazil's nominal deficit is running near ten percent of economic output over the twelve months through June, and around nine of those ten points is pure interest cost, not spending.

In oil, Brent is trading near eighty-seven dollars a barrel, unchanged on the day. That is exactly where the US Energy Information Administration recently put its forecast for the twenty twenty-six average, raised from eighty-two a month earlier. Physically nothing has improved: around eight ships a day are crossing the Strait of Hormuz against a pre-war norm near one hundred twenty. That's also the reason the European Central Bank is the hawk in this story, and a hawkish ECB lifts the euro side of Daniel's rate.

For exchanging money, this is the best window of the month. A hundred euros buy five hundred ninety-seven reais and fifty-nine centavos at Thursday's fix. At the monthly low on the twenty-third of July it was five hundred seventy-seven seventy-five. On a five-thousand-euro exchange that's a difference of roughly nine hundred ninety-two reais.

For today I expect the fix between five point nine five and six point zero two, most likely around five point nine eight. Next week the calendar has two markers: Monday brings the central bank's weekly economist poll, and the tenth of September brings the ECB decision, where a hike to two point five zero percent is the base case. Tomorrow's brief will mainly answer whether Friday's fix confirms the third up-day or finally converts the softer dollar into a firmer real.
