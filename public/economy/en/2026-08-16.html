FX in Detail: A Brazilian Shock, Not a Dollar Shock

Friday in two numbers: the commercial dollar closed in São Paulo at R$5.2213, +0.61% — the fifth straight winning session, the highest level since March 30. At the same time, the Dollar Index fell to 99.667, a one-week low after weak US retail sales. The dollar got cheaper worldwide — and the real weakened anyway. That's the single most important observation of the weekend.

Why this hits you harder than a typical dollar move. EUR/BRL isn't its own market — it's a product: USD/BRL times EUR/USD. When the dollar strengthens globally, it rises against both the real and the euro — in the product, the two effects partly cancel out, so your exchange rate moves less than the headline. When the cause is Brazilian, only the first half happens: USD/BRL rises, the euro leg sits still, and the move passes through to you one for one. Friday was the second case — in its purest form.

DXY −0.30%→ EUR/USD 1.1567, flat+ USD/BRL 5.11 → 5.2213→ EUR/BRL 6.0395

The cross-check breaks — and it stands. The market cross (5.2213 × 1.1567) comes to 6.0395; against the reference rate of 5.9873, that's +0.87% — above the 0.5% threshold at which this brief flags rather than contextualizes.

Reference rate (fix): not a traded price, but a snapshot. The ECB polls the market once a day at 14:15 Central European time — like a photo of the scoreboard at three o'clock sharp, not the final score. In Brazil that's 09:15 in the morning, shortly after trading opens. Whatever the afternoon still does only shows up in the next day's fix.

This isn't a data error: in the same fixing round, USD/BRL stood at 5.1762, at the close 5.2213 — also +0.87%. The entire gap is the time of day. In concrete terms: on a €5,000 exchange, that's R$29,936 at the reference rate versus R$30,198 at the market cross — a R$261 difference, and the gap runs in your favor. Not a guaranteed markup at the counter, but the distance between two measurement points; it disappears on any day the real doesn't move consistently in one direction.

The Sell-off Has a Ledger — It's in the Focus Report

"Fiscal risk" was, until Friday, a phrase in market commentary. In the Brazilian central bank's Focus report from Aug 7, it's a table: the median of surveyed institutions puts net public debt at 69.90% of GDP this year, then 73.40% (2027), 76.40% (2028), 79.00% (2029). More than nine percentage points in four years — not as a warning scenario, but as the market's midpoint. Two rows down is the reason why: the primary balance has stood unchanged at −0.50% of GDP for 25 weeks, the nominal balance at −8.74%.

Primary balance: the government budget without interest — revenue minus spending, before debt service. Like a household ledger where the loan installment hasn't even shown up yet. If this line alone is negative, the debt pile grows even if not a single interest payment were due. The gap to the −8.74% nominal figure is the interest: roughly 8.2 percentage points of GDP, every year, for debt service alone.

The election is the trigger, not the cause. On Aug 12, Société Générale gives Lula a 65% win probability and Flávio Bolsonaro 30% — and spells out the link: a Lula win could widen fiscal risks as social spending and public investment grow without offsetting revenue, worsening the primary balance and raising the debt ratio. So the market isn't trading "the election" — it's trading exactly this line in the table.

My dissent from the panic reading. Because the path is already in the consensus, it's partly priced in — the surprise would be an acceleration past it or a credible reversal, not confirmation. And the same table has a friendlier side: trade balance $76.90 billion, foreign direct investment $78.48 billion (both rising) against a current-account deficit of $60.00 billion. Brazil finances its external deficit more than fully out of foreign direct investment — the external side isn't the problem, the domestic side is.

One Cuts, Two Don't — the Squeeze on Carry

Brazil's central bank is cutting: the Selic has stood at 14.00% since Aug 5, the fourth straight cut, unanimous. The minutes from Aug 11 deliberately give no September guidance; the committee speaks of a "marked increase in uncertainty, de-anchoring of expectations, and elevated risks" and calls for "composure and caution." The Focus median nonetheless reads one more step to 13.75% into it — JPMorgan expects exactly that single cut, then a pause through the second quarter of 2027. The ECB has held at 2.25% since Jul 23 following the June hike, its first tightening since 2023; the Fed has held at 3.50–3.75% since Jul 29.

BCB cuts→ ECB hikes→ Advantage shrinks from both sides→ EUR/BRL up
Carry: the interest-rate spread you pocket when you invest cheaply borrowed money where rates are high. Brazil pays 14.00%, the Fed 3.50–3.75%, the ECB 2.25% on deposits — 10.4 and 11.75 percentage points, accruing daily like interest on a savings account. If the real falls faster than the spread comes in, the party's over.

The level itself remains enormous — but FX markets trade the derivative, and that points down in Brazil, up in Frankfurt. For you, the ECB side counts double: a hike lifts the euro leg and lowers the real's relative appeal at the same time.

The Euro Leg Nobody in Brazil Is Pricing

Every figure in the table above assumes a stable EUR/USD — and that's exactly where the dispersion is wider than in the real forecasts themselves.

Goldman Sachs cut its euro-dollar targets on Jul 11: 1.14 over three months, 1.12 over six and twelve — down from 1.18 (6M) / 1.20 (12M) before. Converting the Focus median of 5.20 at 1.12 instead of Friday's rate of 1.1567 drops EUR/BRL from 6.01 to 5.82: more than 3% lower, without the real gaining a single centavo. At 1.20 — the level older bank targets cluster around — it would be 6.24.

That settles the math. Between the dated real forecasts for end-2026, there's a 1.9% gap (Focus versus Itaú). Between 1.12 and 1.20 in the euro leg, there's a 7.1% gap. For your exchange rate over twelve months, the Europe question is roughly four times as heavy as the Brazil question — and it doesn't appear in a single Brazilian market commentary, because nobody there is pricing in euros.

Source quality Goldman's targets are dated (Jul 11) and come from a named bank. The "cluster at 1.20–1.25," by contrast, comes from an undated aggregator and appears here as range context, not as a forecast anchor. A competing, dated bank call would carry more weight; none could be sourced today.

It's Sunday, August sixteenth. Markets are closed, so there's no reference rate today — but this brief still matters, because the market has shown a six before the decimal point since Friday, and for the first time, the reason can be traced to an actual table instead of just commentary.

Start with the rate that matters to you. São Paulo's Friday close works out to a euro-to-real rate of six point oh three nine five. The weekend quote confirms it: on Sunday, the euro trades at six point oh five reais, unchanged over twenty-four hours. The European Central Bank's reference rate, meanwhile, says five point nine eight seven three. Both are correct — they're just two measurements taken at different times of the same day.

The gap between them is zero point eight seven percent, above the half-percent threshold where this brief flags rather than explains away. The explanation is the clock: the central bank sets its fix at a quarter past two Central European time, which is a quarter past nine in the morning in Brazil. The dollar then climbed from five point one seven six two to five point two two one three — also exactly zero point eight seven percent. On a five-thousand-euro exchange, that's two hundred sixty-one reais, and the gap runs in your favor.

Here's the part that actually matters. The Dollar Index fell on Friday to ninety-nine point six six seven, a one-week low, after weak US retail sales for July. The dollar got cheaper worldwide — and the real fell anyway. That combination only happens when the cause is domestic.

Why this hits harder than an ordinary dollar move: euro-to-real is really dollar-to-real times euro-to-dollar. When a dollar story drives the move, both legs pull in opposite directions and partly cancel out. When a Brazilian story drives it, only the first leg moves, and the whole thing passes through to your exchange rate one for one. Friday was the second case, in its purest form.

The cause sits in the Brazilian central bank's Focus report from August seventh. The median of surveyed banks sees net public debt climbing from just under seventy percent of GDP this year to seventy-nine percent by twenty twenty-nine — more than nine percentage points in four years, and that's not a worst case, it's the middle of the market. The primary budget balance, before interest payments, has been stuck at minus zero point five percent of GDP for twenty-five straight weeks.

October's election is the trigger, not the cause. Société Générale gives Lula a sixty-five percent chance of winning and Flávio Bolsonaro thirty percent — and says a Lula win could widen fiscal risk through higher social spending without new funding to match. Consistent with that, foreigners pulled a net thirteen and a half billion reais from Brazil's stock exchange in August, and JPMorgan cut Brazil to Neutral on August eleventh.

The most important point, though, comes from Europe, and it doesn't show up in a single Brazilian market commentary. No bank forecasts euro-to-real directly — they all forecast dollar-to-real. Among the dated real forecasts for the end of twenty twenty-six, the spread is only about two percent. Among the euro-dollar assumptions behind them, the spread is over seven percent: Goldman Sachs cut its twelve-month target in July to one point twelve, while older bank targets still cluster around one point twenty. For your exchange rate over the next year, the European question matters roughly four times as much as the Brazilian one.

Over twelve months, five point eight oh to six point six oh, tilted higher. All of those percentages are my own confidence, not market prices.
