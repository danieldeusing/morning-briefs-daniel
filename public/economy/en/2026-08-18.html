FX in Detail: The Month Belongs to Brazil, Not Europe

Monday's fix of 6.0402 is the highest of the last 30 ECB fixings. That's +4.55% from the July low on 07/23 (5.7775), and +3.25% versus 07/17 (5.8499). Since 08/11 alone, that's +2.53% over four fixings.

What matters is which leg the move comes from. EUR/BRL isn't its own market — it's a product: euro-dollar times dollar-real. Over the month, the euro leg gained 1.38% (1.1435 → 1.1593), the real leg 1.85% (5.1158 → 5.2102). That's roughly 40% Europe, just under 60% Brazil. Over the past week, that ratio tips dramatically: of the 2.53% since 08/11, four-fifths comes from Brazil.

EUR/USD +0.46%+ USD/BRL +2.06%→ EUR/BRL +2.53%

That's the costliest distinction in this whole brief, for you. A dollar event moves both legs in opposite directions and partly cancels itself out. A Brazilian event moves only one leg and passes through one-for-one, all the way to your bank statement. This week is case two — which is why Wednesday's FOMC minutes matter less to you than they sound.

Reference rate (fix): not a traded price, but a snapshot. The European Central Bank polls the market once a day around 14:15 Central European Summer Time — like a photo of the scoreboard at three o'clock sharp, not the final score. At 03:00 Brazilian time, no fix exists yet for Tuesday; the most recent one is Monday's.

Yesterday's flag has resolved itself. On Monday, this space noted a 1.04% gap between the market cross (6.0496) and the then-most-recent ECB fix from Friday (5.9873) — with the caveat that it was a timestamp gap, not a contradiction. Monday's fix came in at 6.0402 and closed the gap to 0.16%. The market was right; the fix was three days behind.

In concrete terms: a €5,000 exchange comes to R$30,201 today. At the July low, it would have been R$28,888 — R$1,313 less. At the start of the year (6.3743), though, it would have been R$31,872 — R$1,671 more than today. Best rate of the month, yes. Best rate of the year, no — it's 5.5% off that.

The Outflow Is Political — the Credit Market Says So

The number that explains August: foreigners pulled a net R$4.7 billion (roughly US$911 million) out of Brazil's B3 exchange on 08/11 — the largest single-day outflow since April 2021. For August as a whole, that adds up to roughly R$11.9 billion, and to over R$32 billion net since April. The Ibovespa gave up 5.9% to 167,491 points over seven sessions, dropping below the 170,000 mark it had held for months; 08/14 marked the eighth straight losing day — market data shows this as one of the rarest losing streaks in twenty years (the exact frequency varies slightly by data provider).

And now the point almost nobody puts side by side. Brazil's 5-year CDS was quoted at 126.56 basis points on 08/17 per Investing.com's live feed. At the end of July, before the entire sell-off, it was 126.27. Difference: 0.3 basis points.

CDS (Credit Default Swap): insurance against a borrower's default — here, the Brazilian state. Like fire insurance on a house: if a neighborhood is suddenly considered dangerous, the premium rises immediately, whether or not anything's actually burning. 126 basis points means 1.26% of the insured amount per year. That this premium hasn't budged despite billions in outflows is the most telling signal of the day.

My read — and it contradicts the tone of the headlines. If investors genuinely believed Brazil were heading into a fiscal crisis, the CDS would be the first thing to move: it's liquid, it trades around the clock, and you don't need to sell stocks to buy it. It hasn't moved. What has moved are equity positions ahead of an election date — JPMorgan cut Brazil from Overweight to Neutral on 08/11 and lowered its Ibovespa target from 190,000 to 185,000 points, explicitly citing that Brazilian assets typically underperform in the six months before an election. That's calendar risk management, not a credit reassessment.

Election premium: the markup investors demand for keeping their money in a country through an uncertain date. Like a landlord who accepts less rent while announced construction sits out front — not because the apartment got worse, but because the timing is uncertain. Once the date passes, the discount usually disappears within days, in one direction or the other.

What this means for your exchange. Positioning moves run fast and reverse fast; credit moves run slow and stick. The current rate belongs to the first category — a window, not a new normal to base six months of planning on. Fitting with that: how small the damage stays on an annual scale. Despite all of August's outflows, the foreign flow for 2026 as a whole is still positive at R$24.4 billion.

The political backdrop: Quaest's 08/14 poll puts the runoff at Lula 43% against Flávio Bolsonaro 40% — a technical tie within a ±2-point margin of error, and tighter than on 08/05 (44 to 39). The narrowing is the news, not the lead.

Itaú's Target Has Been Reached — the Reasoning Has Been Disproved

On 06/26, Itaú raised its year-end target for USD/BRL from 5.15 to 5.30. Today the rate stands at 5.204 — the target is a good 98% reached. Looks like a good call. But the reasoning behind it had three parts: a less favorable external environment, Brazilian election risk, and worse terms of trade from lower oil.

Terms of trade: the price ratio between what a country sells and what it buys. Like a kid trading collectible cards with an allowance: if the value of their cards rises and the price of what they want in return falls, they get richer without working any harder. Brazil sells oil, ore, and soy — when those prices rise, dollars flow into the country and the real should actually firm up.

Oil hasn't fallen — it's risen. Brent was quoted at $91.50 a barrel on 08/18, up 0.69% on the day, 2.55% for the month, and 39.08% year over year. The exact opposite of the assumption. And Goldman's April case for a strong real — a terms-of-trade boost plus high carry — is thus fundamentally better supported today than it was in April.

Why this is more than nitpicking. When a target is reached even though two of the three assumptions behind it never materialized, the third has to carry it alone: election risk. That's exactly the diagnosis the unmoved CDS delivers next door — from two entirely independent angles. For the forecast, that means: the rate is standing on one leg. A leg with a date in October.

The counter-argument: oil up 39% is also an inflation driver. Focus sees 2026 IPCA at 5.02% and 2027 at 4.24% (raised from 4.22%) — that limits how far the central bank can cut. Both oil effects therefore support the real; for your rate, they point down.

The Pincer: Brazil Is Cutting, Europe Has Hiked

On 08/05, Copom unanimously cut the Selic by 25 basis points to 14.00% — the fourth straight cut, in a cycle that began at 15.00% in March 2026. The minutes explicitly gave no signal for September; the size of the cycle would be adjusted as the scenario evolves. JPMorgan reads exactly one more 25-basis-point cut in September, followed by a pause through the second quarter of 2027. Focus sees the Selic at 13.75% by the end of 2026 and 12.00% by the end of 2027.

On the other side sits something that turns intuition on its head: the ECB is hiking. Its deposit rate has stood at 2.25% since 06/17/2026 (main refinancing 2.40%, marginal lending 2.65%) — a 25-basis-point hike and the first change since 06/11/2025.

Rate advantage (carry): the gap between what money earns in Brazil and what it earns in Europe — currently 14.00 versus 2.25%, or roughly 11.75 points. Like two savings accounts side by side: as long as the Brazilian one pays substantially more, investors park money there and demand for the real rises. The catch: if the rate breaks, a year's worth of interest advantage can vanish in a week. That's why a wide spread is a support and a risk at the same time.

Why this is your most important twelve-month driver. Both legs of the pincer move in the same direction for you: Brazil is cutting toward 12%, Europe has started hiking. Even if only a quarter of that gap disappears, the real's savings account becomes relatively less attractive — and EUR/BRL gets structural lift, with no election and no crisis needed. That's why the twelve-month range above has an upward tilt, even though the short-term move is pure positioning.

The honest counter-argument: 11.75 points is a massive buffer. Even after two points of compression, Brazil remains by far the higher-yielding place to park money. Anyone concluding from this that the real is headed for a rapid collapse is overreaching — the pincer works over quarters, not weeks.

Start with the numbers. The European Central Bank's reference rate for Monday came in at six point oh four oh two reais per euro, the highest of the last thirty trading days. That's up four point five five percent from the July twenty-third low, and three point two five percent from a month ago. Now, where is this move actually coming from? Your rate isn't its own market — it's euro-to-dollar times dollar-to-real. Over the month, roughly forty percent of the rise came from Europe and just under sixty from Brazil. But over the last week, that ratio flips hard: of the two point five three percent gained since August eleventh, four-fifths came from Brazil alone. That matters, because a dollar event moves both legs in opposite directions and partly cancels out, while a Brazilian event hits your rate one for one.

What happened in Brazil has a clear date. On August eleventh, foreign investors pulled four point seven billion reais out of the stock exchange in a single day — the largest one-day outflow since April twenty twenty-one. For August as a whole, that's roughly eleven point nine billion, and over thirty-two billion net since April. The Ibovespa lost five point nine percent over seven sessions and fell below one hundred seventy thousand points. JPMorgan downgraded Brazil to Neutral on August eleventh, citing the historical pattern that Brazilian assets underperform in the run-up to elections.

Here's the point almost nobody puts side by side. The price of insurance against a Brazilian government default — the five-year credit default swap — stood at one hundred twenty-six point five six basis points on August seventeenth. At the end of July, before any of the sell-off, it was one hundred twenty-six point two seven. Three-tenths of a basis point of difference. Picture fire insurance on a house: if a neighborhood is suddenly seen as dangerous, the premium jumps immediately, whether or not anything's burning. This premium hasn't moved. So investors are clearing out equity positions ahead of an election, not marking down Brazil's creditworthiness. For you, that means: the current rate is real and usable, but it's a window, not a new normal.

A second, unrelated story lines up with the same diagnosis. Itaú raised its year-end dollar-real target to five point thirty back in June and has nearly nailed it. But one of its three reasons was falling oil. Brent is trading today at ninety-one dollars and fifty cents, up thirty-nine percent over the past year — the opposite of what Itaú assumed. When a target lands even though two of three assumptions behind it were wrong, the third one is carrying it alone. And the third one is election risk.

On the consensus side, the sharpest thing is how little conviction is behind it. The Brazilian central bank's Focus survey, published this past Monday, is the first one taken after the sell-off — and its median forecast stayed at five point twenty, essentially unchanged, while the actual rate sits at five point two oh four. In other words, the economists are saying nothing more happens between now and New Year's. That's not really a forecast — it's a capitulation to the spot price. More telling is the spread between forecasters: Goldman's five flat from April against BTG's five point forty from July is an eight percent gap — in your currency, that's five point eighty versus six point twenty-six, or about twenty-three hundred reais on a five-thousand-euro exchange.

Longer term, the pincer is what counts. Brazil cut unanimously to fourteen percent on August fifth, its fourth straight cut, with the Focus survey projecting twelve percent by the end of twenty twenty-seven. Europe, meanwhile, has been hiking and sits at two point two five percent. Both sides are narrowing Brazil's rate advantage, which today stands at roughly eleven point seven five points. That pushes your rate structurally higher over time — but over quarters, not weeks; the cushion is still enormous.
