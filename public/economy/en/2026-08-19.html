FX in Detail: Tuesday Was a Euro Decline

Tuesday's ECB fix stands at 6.0281 — the first decline after four straight increases, −0.20% versus Monday's 6.0402. Sounds like nothing. What's interesting is where that nothing came from.

EUR/BRL isn't its own market — it's a product of two prices: euro-to-dollar times dollar-to-real. On Tuesday, the euro leg fell 0.147% (1.1593 → 1.1576), the real leg 0.052% (5.2101 → 5.2074). Three-quarters of the move, in other words, came from Europe.

EUR/USD −0.15%+ USD/BRL −0.05%→ EUR/BRL −0.20%

That's the reverse of the prior week. Of the +2.33% gained between 08/11 and 08/18, 86% came from Brazil and only 13% from Europe; over the full month (07/20 → 08/18, +3.36%), the ratio runs roughly 39% Europe, 60% Brazil. One trading day doesn't reverse that — but it's the first sign that the euro leg is developing a life of its own again.

Reference rate (fix): not a traded price, but a snapshot. The European Central Bank polls the market once a day around 14:15 Central European Summer Time — like a photo of the scoreboard at three o'clock sharp, not the final score. At 03:00 Brazilian time, no fix exists yet for Wednesday; the most recent one is Tuesday's.

Live trading sits above that. Brazil's spot market closed Tuesday at 5.2229 — after the European fix, and 0.30% above it. Combined with Wednesday morning's independently pulled euro-dollar rate (1.15847), that produces a cross of 6.0506: above Monday's record fix. Mixed timestamps, so indicative only — but the direction is clear, and the gap to the fix stays below the flag threshold at 0.37%.

In concrete terms: a €5,000 exchange comes to R$30,140 at Tuesday's fix. At the July low (5.7775 on 07/23), it would have been R$28,888 — R$1,253 less. You're just R$61 away from Monday's record fix. The window this brief has been describing since Monday is still open.

The Real Just Had Its Worst Week in Months

USD/BRL +2.68% on the week, +3.00% for the month — yet the real is still 4.70% firmer year over year (StoneX, 08/16). The Ibovespa closed 08/18 at 166,334.86 points, −0.27%: an eleventh straight losing day, on volume of R$15.3 billion, below the 50-day average.

The driver has a name: foreigners. On 08/14 they pulled out R$2.52 billion — a ninth straight day of outflows; for August, that adds up to R$18.1 billion. And here's the scale missing from the headlines: for the full year, the balance still stands at +R$18.2 billion. August, in other words, wiped out exactly what seven months had built up — no more. (A second source counts only R$13.56 billion through 08/12; reconcilable given the different cutoff dates, but a different house.)

The rates curve says the same thing, only more precisely. On 08/18, short DI contracts fell by up to 1.0 basis points, while long ones rose by up to 8.0.

Curve steepening: short and long rates pull apart. Like a family that can easily cover next month's rent but no longer knows whether it can still afford the house in ten years. Translated: the market still believes in the central bank's ongoing rate cuts (short end down), but doubts the fiscal and inflation outlook further out more strongly (long end up). Exactly the pattern that forms before elections.

What this move isn't, at least not yet: a credit question. Brazil's five-year credit-default spread was last verifiably at 126.27–126.90 basis points in calendar week 28, i.e. early July — a current print wasn't obtainable today, the usual sources now only serve JavaScript. Yesterday's brief still pulled 126.56 basis points for 08/17; that's within July's band. As long as that premium doesn't move, it's a positioning unwind, not a repricing of solvency — and moves like this reverse as fast as they run.

Sentiment fits: in a Bank of America survey, 53% of Latin America fund managers expect negative earnings revisions in August, and just under half see the Ibovespa below 170,000 by year-end. Counterweight: Brent at $91.10 (+0.25%) supports Brazil's terms of trade.

The Pincer — and a Set of Minutes This Afternoon

On 08/05, Copom unanimously cut the Selic by 25 basis points to 14.00% — the fourth cut of the cycle that began in March 2026, effective 08/06. The 08/11 minutes are read as "marginally more dovish" and leave room for another move; the next meeting is 09/15–09/16, base case 13.75%. Focus sees exactly that for year-end, then 12.00% by end-2027.

On the other side sits something that runs counter to intuition: the ECB is hiking. The deposit rate has stood at 2.25% since 06/17/2026. The reason is in its own projection — 3.0% inflation for 2026, driven by energy: the eurozone's flash estimate for July came in at 2.9%, with energy up 10.0% year over year, explicitly because of renewed hostilities between the US and Iran.

Rate advantage (carry): the gap between what money earns in Brazil and what it earns in Europe — currently 14.00 versus 2.25%, or roughly 11.75 points. Like two savings accounts side by side: as long as the Brazilian one pays substantially more, investors park money there and demand for the real rises. The catch: if the rate breaks, a year's worth of rate advantage can vanish in a week.

This afternoon brings the third side. The minutes from the Fed's 07/28–07/29 meeting land at 15:00 BRT. The target band held at 3.50–3.75% — but with three dissents in favor of a hike, the first three-way dissent in the same direction in a decade (secondary source); those hike bets then collapsed after the weak July jobs report. For you, this is a pure dollar event and therefore damped: a hawkish tone lifts USD/BRL (good) while simultaneously weighing on EUR/USD (bad) — half of it cancels out. A Brazilian event, by contrast, passes through one for one. That's why the minutes sound louder than they actually are for you.

The risk factor with no calendar entry is the trade dispute: Brazil, via Camex, has invoked its reciprocity law against the US tariffs and requested consultations; on 08/10, Washington signaled it was "à disposição" (available) for talks. Both escalation and a deal hit the real directly and undamped, just in opposite directions. StoneX names exactly three drivers for this week — the election, the FOMC minutes, the trade dispute — and sees all three as dollar-positive.

On Tuesday, the euro leg did almost all the work, falling zero point one five percent while the real leg slipped just zero point zero five. Three-quarters of the move came out of Europe. The week before, it was the exact opposite: of the two point three three percent gained since August eleventh, eighty-six percent came from Brazil. One day doesn't flip a trend, but it's the first sign that the euro leg is acting on its own again.
