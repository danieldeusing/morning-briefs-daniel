FX in Detail: Why a Falling Dollar Doesn't Do Much for You

Wednesday's ECB fix stands at 6.0394, +0.19% versus Tuesday. The interesting part, again, is the breakdown.

Two legs: EUR/BRL isn't its own market. Nobody trades euros directly against reais in any meaningful volume; the rate emerges as the product of euro-to-dollar times dollar-to-real. Like a currency exchange at the airport with a layover: euros into dollars first, then dollars into reais. That's why every move in your rate has exactly two possible sources — Europe or Brazil.

On Wednesday, the euro leg rose 0.25% (1.1576 → 1.1605), while the real leg fell 0.06% (5.2074 → 5.2041). The entire rise — and then some — came from Europe, against a slightly firmer real.

EUR/USD +0.25%− USD/BRL 0.06%→ EUR/BRL +0.19%

This morning that turns into the real lesson. The dollar is easing on a broad front: euro-dollar stands at 1.16737 (+0.59% versus Wednesday's fix), dollar-real at 5.17780 (−0.51%). Both moves work against each other for you — the stronger euro lifts your rate, the stronger real pulls it down. Net, +0.08% is left, and the cross sits at 6.0444.

That's not a coincidence, it's arithmetic. If the dollar eases by x percent purely on dollar-driven moves and the real matches it one for one, your rate is unchanged by construction. In practice, that means the Dollar Index is the weakest of the three drivers for you. What actually gets through are shocks that hit only one leg — a Brazilian fiscal headline, an ECB decision, an election poll. Last week was full of those, which is why your rate sits 4.5% above the July low.

In concrete terms: exchanging €5,000 comes to R$30,197 at Wednesday's fix. At the July low (5.7775 on 07/23), it would have been R$28,888 — R$1,310 less. The window this brief has been describing since Monday is still open — but it hasn't widened further since then.

The Election Premium Is in the Rate, Not in Credit

The real was the weakest of 24 emerging-market currencies over the past week: −2.8%, closing the week at 5.2228 per dollar. Three triggers are converging — the general election on 10/04, an eleven-measure spending package from the Lula government announced back in May worth R$143.7 billion (roughly $27.5 billion) that's still weighing on the fiscal outlook, and, per market reports, a JPMorgan downgrade of Brazilian assets from overweight to neutral.

Risk premium: the extra return investors demand to sit through an uncertain outcome. Like a landlord who asks a tenant without a formal job contract for three months' deposit instead of two — not because he thinks the tenant is dishonest, but because he wants to be paid for the uncertainty. Ahead of elections, that deposit rises; afterward it often falls again within days, if nothing bad happens.

What matters is where this premium sits. Brazil's five-year credit-default spread closed at 125.90 basis points on 08/19, down 0.66% from the day before. In mid-July, the same figure stood at roughly 126.3 to 126.9. That's identical within measurement tolerance — the credit market simply didn't react to the worst EM week of the year.

CDS (credit-default spread): insurance against a country's default. 125.90 basis points means: 1.259% of the insured amount per year. Like homeowner's insurance whose premium rises as soon as there's a burglary on the street — if it doesn't move, the market considers the house just as safe as before.

The bond market confirms this: Brazil's 10-year government bond yielded 14.788% on 08/19, 0.077 points lower than the day before — bonds were bought, not sold. What's being unwound here, in other words, is a crowded bet on the rate advantage, not a repricing of solvency. Moves like this reverse as fast as they run — they can close the window at 6.04 just as quickly as they opened it.

What the central bank is doing: since 08/05 it has been rolling over the FX swaps maturing 09/01; one auction round allotted 34,000 contracts worth $1.7 billion — rollover activity, not new intervention. I couldn't verify the total outstanding stock today.

The Pincer Is Closing — From Both Sides

On 08/05, Copom cut the Selic by 25 basis points to 14.00% — unanimously, all seven members including Governor Gabriel Galípolo, with no dissent and no vote for a bigger step. There was explicitly no pre-commitment for September: the scope of the cycle would depend on the data. The next meeting is 09/15–09/16; Focus sees year-end at 13.75%.

Rate advantage (carry): the gap between what money earns in Brazil and what it earns in Europe — currently 14.00 versus 2.25%, or 11.75 points. Like two savings accounts side by side: as long as the Brazilian one pays substantially more, investors park money there and demand for the real rises. The catch: if the rate breaks, a whole year's worth of rate advantage can vanish in a week.

On the other side sits the ECB, with a deposit rate of 2.25%, last confirmed on 07/23. The gap to the Fed is 125 to 150 basis points, and to Brazil's central bank, 1,175. Both ends of this pincer are moving toward each other — Brazil is cutting, Europe isn't — and that's exactly the structural upward pressure on your rate.

The third side is the most restless. The Fed has held at 3.50–3.75% since 07/29, but with three dissents in favor of a hike (Hammack, Kashkari, Logan). The July CPI report on 08/12 — headline rate 3.4%, down from 3.5% in June, core 2.5% — has dampened those bets: hike probability for September 38%, with roughly 64% pricing in a hold. For you, this is a pure dollar event and therefore, as shown above, damped.

Chips, Power, Oil — Three Channels Into the Same Inflation

Four headlines are hitting the same spot: global goods prices, the very thing the Fed calibrates its hawkishness against.

Samsung is raising its chip-fabrication prices by up to 15% — customers in China and the US pay 10 to 15% more, in Taiwan 5 to 10%, effective since July on new orders. The reason is capacity utilization: AI demand is filling the 4-nanometer lines. That's a cost increase right at the start of almost every electronics supply chain.

Nvidia's H200s are flowing into China again: roughly 10,000 units each to ByteDance and Tencent over the past few weeks — about 13% of what each approved customer is allowed to buy under the US framework from January (75,000). Extra well-funded demand in an already tight market.

And then there's power. Europe's data centers used roughly 145 TWh in 2025, and that's expected to reach 238 TWh by 2030 — up 64% in five years. The bottleneck isn't land, it's the grid connection: operators look for available grid capacity first and pick the location second, which is why new projects are landing in Berlin, Milan, Madrid, or Warsaw. A direct inflation channel into the ECB's projections.

On top of that, Brent at $91.86 (08/19, +0.92%), because the Strait of Hormuz is effectively closed — on Sunday, three ships passed through, versus around 130 per day before the conflict began in February. For Brazil, expensive oil supports the trade balance and the real; for the world, it's inflation and it supports hawkish central banks. For you, both effects pull against each other again.
