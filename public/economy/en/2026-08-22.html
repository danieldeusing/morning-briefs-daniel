An Election Poll Turns the Real — the ECB Fix Doesn't Notice

The Essentials in 30 Seconds

On Friday afternoon Brazilian time, the real gained about one percent because a new Datafolha poll called the runoff a dead heat. The ECB reference rate had already been locked for five hours by then. Result: the official fix reads 6.0518, while your actual tradable rate at the end of the week sat around 6.01. That's not a data error — it's a timing artifact — but it's the first time in weeks the gap has been large enough to distort an exchange decision. Today is Saturday, nothing is trading; the question is what Monday's fix makes of it.

FX in detail: an afternoon the fix never saw

Friday's fix stands at 6.0518, −0.24% versus Thursday. That sounds like a quiet day. It wasn't.

Reference rate (fix): Every business day around 14:15 Central European time, the ECB polls central banks for current rates and publishes one official number per currency pair. Think of it as a photo of a weather board taken at exactly 3 p.m.: accurate for that instant, but it says nothing about whether it was still raining at five. In Brazil, it's only 09:15 at the moment that photo is taken — the trading day has barely started.

Friday's real action didn't start until after that. Dollar-real fell 1.00% to R$ 5.1417, the Ibovespa rose 1.85% to 171,031 points. Extrapolating that spot close with a euro-dollar rate of 1.1699 gives an EUR/BRL of 6.0153. An independent path — TradingEconomics' directly quoted EUR/BRL closing line — gives 6.0066. The two sit 0.14% apart; the fix sits 0.75% above both.

Flag 0.75% is above this brief's 0.5% threshold — on any other day, reason to distrust a source. Not today: both independent readings point the same direction and agree with each other to within 0.14%. The fix is correct for 09:15 BRT and stale for 17:00 BRT. My FX tool's automatic cross-check, incidentally, flagged 0.0% deviation — worthless, since all three legs there come from the same ECB series and necessarily agree with each other.

The breakdown over longer horizons — measured fix-to-fix here, so both ends share the same timestamp:

Week: EUR/USD +1.14%· USD/BRL −0.06%→ EUR/BRL +1.08%
Month: EUR/USD +2.55%· USD/BRL +1.84%→ EUR/BRL +4.43%

On the week, the entire rise came from Europe; the real barely moved. On the month, it's more balanced: roughly 58% euro, 42% real. The euro sits at 1.1699, its highest fix of the month — and that's exactly where the risk sits that drives the whole forecast further down.

In concrete terms: €5,000 came to R$ 30,333 at Thursday's fix, R$ 30,259 at Friday's fix, but only R$ 30,076 actually tradable Friday evening. R$ 257 of difference between Thursday's peak and the real week-end close. At the month's low on 07/23, it would have been R$ 28,888 — the month remains a very good one despite Friday.

Jackson Hole: why Warsh's speech cuts differently for you

From Thursday to Saturday, roughly 120 central bankers from over 70 countries meet at the Jackson Lake Lodge in Wyoming. This year's theme: "Financial Innovation: Implications for Payments and Policy." But the date everyone's watching is Friday morning: Kevin Warsh's first policy speech as Fed chair, three weeks before the FOMC meeting with new projections.

Fuzziness Two sources name Friday, 08/28, as the date (the traditional slot for the keynote); one weekly calendar places it on Thursday, 08/27. No official time has been published. Plan for 08/27–28, with Friday as the likely focus.

Warsh's pre-speech signaling has been unusually blunt: he says he wants to step back from "shortsighted debates" and ask "big questions," has pre-announced a monetary-policy "regime change," and says inflation has stayed above target too long. The facts on the ground: the Fed has held at 3.50–3.75% for five straight meetings, but in July, three members voted for a hike of 25 basis points.

Hawkish / dovish: "Hawkish" means the central bank leans toward higher rates because it fears inflation. "Dovish" means the opposite. Like a parent who's either strict or lenient about dessert — you can tell instantly from the kid's reaction which one is on duty.

And now the asymmetry that works in your favor. A hawkish Warsh strengthens the dollar against everything. The euro might give back a percent — but the real gives back a percent and a half, because emerging-market currencies swing harder on a dollar shock. Since your rate is the product of both legs, the net effect is a plus:

Warsh hawkish→ EUR/USD −1%· USD/BRL +1.5%→ EUR/BRL ≈ +0.5%

A dovish outcome, conversely, costs you roughly the same. This isn't a forecast, it's a sensitivity: the Fed shock isn't particularly dangerous for you, and in one direction it's actually helpful. The dangerous case is the third possibility — a speech with no news value while the real keeps running its domestic-politics rally. Then only one leg moves, and it isn't yours.

The election premium switched direction on Friday

Datafolha surveyed 2,058 people from August 18 to 20 (margin of error ±2 points, TSE registration BR-04496/2026) and published Friday. In the first round, Lula leads with 39% over Flávio Bolsonaro at 33%. In the simulated runoff, it's 47 to 43 — the lead shrinks from six to four points, a statistical dead heat.

Two scenarios, two numbers Datafolha ran the first round twice: without Pablo Marçal — who is running under an injunction but was ruled ineligible by the TRE-SP through 2032 — Flávio stands at 33% (a six-point gap); with him, at 32% (seven points, Marçal takes exactly that one point off him). I'm using the 33%: it's the figure Brazilian outlets consistently report as the headline result and the one Datafolha carries forward in its own time series (prior poll: Lula 41%, Flávio 31%).

Why a tighter poll strengthens the real isn't self-explanatory — the chain runs through a market assumption worth knowing. Société Générale spelled it out explicitly on 08/13: the bank puts a Lula win with a split Congress at 65% probability and expects the real at 5.25–5.35 in that scenario — weaker, in other words. Any poll that makes that exact scenario less likely pulls risk premium out of the real. That the Ibovespa is up 1.85% at the same time fits this reading — currency and equities tend to move together on a political repricing, and often against each other on a pure rate or dollar impulse.

Caution This is an interpretation, not a measured cause. The Brazilian source cites two reasons — "election news flow" and "US-dollar weakness." The second is disproved by the Dollar Index itself: −0.08% doesn't carry a 1.04% move. So it comes down to politics — but don't count on the causality still holding next week.

Positioning: R$ 20.4bn is out — and the real climbs anyway

This month's contradiction: foreign investors have pulled R$ 20.4bn (roughly US$ 3.9bn) out of Brazil's stock market so far in August, as of around 08/20. Investors selling stock usually convert the reais back to dollars — which weighs on the currency.

That's exactly what shows up once you strip out Friday: on the month, the real lost ground against the dollar (ECB cross +1.84%, TradingEconomics reports a monthly +1.50%). So Friday's rally isn't a reversal in flows, it's a news day cutting against an ongoing outflow. What that means for you: the structural headwind is still there — Friday just overrode it for one day.

The stock market shows little fear. The Ibovespa closed at 171,031 points (+1.85% on the day, +2.4% on the week) — even though on Thursday, banks were still smarting from Casas Bahia's insolvency proceedings (Bradesco as the most exposed creditor at around R$ 4.8bn, its stock down 1.5%).
