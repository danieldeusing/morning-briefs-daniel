FX in detail: the dollar mostly cancels itself out in your rate

There's nothing new to quote today, so it's worth looking at something that gets lost on trading days: how your rate is actually built. EUR/BRL isn't traded directly anywhere. It's the product of two legs — euro against dollar, dollar against real.

Cross rate (two legs): For a pair with no liquid market of its own, you route the calculation through the dollar as a stopover. Like a connecting flight: the total price comes from two legs, and if one gets pricier while the other gets cheaper, you might not notice anything by the end. Formally: EUR/BRL = EUR/USD × USD/BRL.

That produces a property that surprises a lot of people: a dollar that rises evenly against everything pushes EUR/USD down and lifts USD/BRL up. The two effects run against each other and largely cancel out. Your rate isn't a dollar bet, then. It's a bet on "euro versus real" — the dollar only shows up where it hits the two currencies unevenly.

The past month is the textbook case of exactly that. Fix to fix, meaning the same time of day at both ends:

Month: EUR/USD +2.55%· USD/BRL +1.84%→ EUR/BRL +4.43%

The dollar fell against the euro and rose against the real. Both legs moved the same direction — hence the strong month. That wasn't a single dollar event, but two independent ones: a firm euro and a weak real. Friday ran exactly the opposite way, but on only one leg: the dollar index eased 0.08%, while dollar-real dropped over 1%. Nothing happened globally; it all sat in Brazil.

Rule of thumb When DXY and USD/BRL diverge, your rate moves. When they run in parallel, little happens to you. That's why the DXY card above isn't decoration — it's the test for whether a "strong dollar" headline even has consequences for you.

For Monday, that means: the fix forms around 09:15 BRT and starts from where the market actually closed Friday. If both legs stay unchanged, it mechanically prints 6.0153 (spot close 5.1417) to 6.0176 (Rio Times reading 5.1437), that is 0.56–0.60% below Friday. Catch-up, not bad news.

In money: €5,000 brought R$30,333 at Thursday's fix; by Friday evening it was really only R$30,077 — R$257 less in one afternoon. Remember that R$257; it becomes the yardstick for the whole forecast.

Horizon-matched: Multiplying forecasts from different weeks is like mixing two weather reports — yesterday's rain forecast with today's temperature forecast. Each one is consistent on its own; together they describe a weather nobody predicted. "Matched" means both legs come from notes with the same date and the same horizon.

And that's exactly where my second mistake was. I multiplied the real targets of dollar bulls (Itaú, BTG, Goldman — all of whom explicitly justify their upgrade with a stronger dollar) by the euro targets of dollar bears (1.20–1.22 assumes a weaker dollar). That counts the same dollar strength twice in my favor and produces an EUR/BRL that not a single house believes in.

Take instead the two freshest complete ladders — Goldman (Aug 18) for the real, Bank of America (Aug 17) for the euro, published 24 hours apart — and it looks like this:

This week: three inflation prints and an inaugural speech

For the first time in days, the calendar is fully dated — including the IPCA-15 release, which was still unconfirmed yesterday.

Monday, 08:25 BRT: the Focus report. It matters more than usual this week, since it's the first one released after Friday's repricing.

Focus report: Every week, Brazil's central bank surveys over a hundred banks and funds on their forecasts for inflation, interest rates, and the exchange rate, and publishes the averages on Monday. Like a weekly class poll asking "How do you think the test went?" — not the actual grade, but a very good sense of what everyone expects. The year-end dollar has stood unchanged there for nine weeks at R$5.20, at 5.29 for end-2027, with the Selic at 13.75%.

The timing is notable: 08:25 BRT falls about 50 minutes before the ECB fix. If the Focus report moves the real, Monday's fix will still catch it — unlike Friday's afternoon rally.

Wednesday, 09:00 BRT: IPCA-15 for August.

IPCA-15: Brazil's official inflation gauge, but with a collection window that closes about two weeks earlier — so it's a preview of the number that later arrives as the full IPCA. Like a progress report before the final grade: not final, but it tells you fairly precisely where things are headed. Copom watches it closely; BTG expects 5.3% for 2026 as a whole.

An upside surprise slows Brazil's rate cuts — that supports the real and pushes your rate down; a downside surprise works the other way. It wasn't the poll that drove this — it was the investigations

Pinning Friday's real rally on a single Datafolha poll falls short. The poll was the trigger; the underlying cause has been running for a month.

Brazil's Supreme Court has approved three investigations against Fábio Luís Lula da Silva — the president's son, known in Brazil as "Lulinha" — two on Jul 30 and a third on Aug 3, 2026. On Aug 17, chat messages with lobbyist Roberta Luchsinger leaked, which Gazeta do Povo says show "strong indications of influence peddling." At stake are, among other things, R$85 million in federal contracts for a private company, R$1.5 million from a person tied to social security, and lobbying for medical-cannabis authorizations at the health ministry.

On sourcing: the TSE's primary site refuses access; the data comes from a search-index excerpt of that same page, confirmed by a second source and by the constitutional rule (first and last Sunday of October, respectively), both checked mathematically as falling on Sundays.

My take: from here on, every new poll is an FX event, and the asymmetry runs against you. The market has priced in "a change of government is getting more likely" and stripped out premium accordingly. If the narrative flips back, the premium tends to return faster than it was removed. That would be good for your rate — you just can't plan around it.

It's Sunday, August twenty-third, morning in Ribeirão Claro. This is the second day without trading — no reference rate, no new poll, no political news. Even so, something real has shifted for your exchange decision, just not in the rate itself, but in the forecasts behind it.

Let's start with a correction. Yesterday, this brief said analysts were nearly unanimous on the real, at five twenty by year-end. That was a selection error. I'd left out two Brazilian banks because their numbers were floating around without a solid date, and both happen to sit above that cluster. The second new anchor comes from the United States, and it matters more. That's the freshest dated euro forecast out there, and its three-month figure sits below today's rate of one seventeen. This brief had been using a range of one seventeen to one twenty-two. The lower end has now dropped.

And here's the real takeaway. Multiply the two freshest complete ladders together — Goldman Sachs for the real, dated August eighteenth, and Bank of America for the euro, dated August seventeenth, published twenty-four hours apart — and your rate comes out at five ninety-eight in three months, five ninety-seven in six months, and six even in twelve months. Why that's not a coincidence has to do with how your rate is built. Euro-real isn't traded directly anywhere. It's a product of two legs: euro against dollar, times dollar against real. A dollar that strengthens evenly against everything pushes the first leg down and lifts the second one up. The two effects run against each other and largely cancel out. Your rate isn't a dollar bet — it's a bet on euro versus real. And both banks cited here are dollar-friendly, which is exactly why combining them produces almost no movement.

That was also my own methodological mistake yesterday. I multiplied the real targets from dollar bulls by the euro targets from dollar bears. That counts the same dollar strength twice in your favor, and produces a rate that not a single bank actually believes in. So I'm lowering my twelve-month point from six fifteen to six-oh-five. Not because of news — because of the math.

In money terms, that's very concrete. Your tradable rate stood around six-oh-one-five Friday evening. The twelve-month expectation sits at six-oh-five. A full year of waiting is worth about a hundred seventy-four reais on five thousand euros. For comparison: Friday afternoon alone moved two hundred fifty-seven reais, and this one month's range moved fourteen forty-five. The expected trend is smaller than the noise. That's not a buy signal, it's a stance: waiting for a better rate has no expected payoff by this math, but grabbing the good days within your normal exchange rhythm still does.
