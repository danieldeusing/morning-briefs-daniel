The 30-second version

FX in detail: two days, same dollar, twelvefold effect

Anyone wanting to understand why EUR/BRL sometimes jumps and sometimes goes quiet gets the textbook example handed to them this week — two consecutive trading days, almost the same dollar move, an effect that differs by a factor of twelve.

Cross rate (two legs): For a pair with no liquid market of its own, you route the calculation through the dollar as a stopover. Like a trip with a connection: the total price comes from two legs, and if one gets pricier while the other gets cheaper, you might not notice anything by the end. Formally: EUR/BRL = EUR/USD × USD/BRL.

Monday was the "uneven" case. The dollar strengthened against the euro (1.1699 → 1.1664, −0.30%) while simultaneously weakening against the real (5.1729 → 5.1499, −0.44%). Both legs pulled your pair down. Result: −0.74%.

Today is the "even" case. The dollar index stands at +0.08%, the euro eases 0.08%, the real 0.08% — measured against Monday's fix, that's −0.08% on the first leg and +0.14% on the second. The two nearly cancel out: +0.06%.

Rule of thumb Your rate isn't a dollar bet — it's a bet on "euro versus real." The dollar only shows up where it hits the two currencies unevenly. A headline saying "dollar strong" tells you nothing by itself — only the question "stronger against whom, more?" does.

And in money: €5,000 bought R$30,259 at Friday's fix, R$30,034 at Monday's fix — R$225 less over a weekend with not a single trading day in between. Tradable today, it's R$30,052. That puts you practically back on the tradable Friday close of around 6.0153: the gap between the fix and the market, which had torn open to 0.75% on Friday, is fully closed.

This isn't news, it's a timing correction — and it was flagged here Sunday: expected 5.96–6.07 with a point estimate of 6.01, printed at 6.0068. What matters is the lesson, not the hit: when the fix only reflects afternoon moves the following day, a single fix jump is often not a signal but a calendar effect. Mix up the two, and you exchange at the wrong moment.

This week: a clock with no time zone — and what follows from it

The weekly agenda that the consensus figures below come from prints a time for every event — but no time zone. That's solvable: US initial jobless claims always appear at 08:30 New York time, and the agenda lists them at 12:30. In August, that's exactly GMT. So every other time on the agenda is also GMT — and can be converted to Brazil time (minus three hours) and to the ECB fix.

Two things follow from that which otherwise get overlooked:

1. Wednesday's IPCA-15 lands before the fix. Publication at 12:00 GMT = 09:00 BRT = 14:00 CEST, the fix locks in at 14:15 CEST. A surprise therefore still hits your reference rate the same day — unlike most Brazilian data.

IPCA-15: Brazil's official inflation gauge, but with a collection window that closes about two weeks earlier — a preview of the number that later arrives as the full IPCA. Like a progress report before the final grade: not final, but it tells you fairly precisely where things are headed. An upside surprise slows Brazil's rate cuts, supports the real, and pushes your rate down.
Consensus conflict Two agendas cite different expectations: 4.5% year over year after 4.52% (Rio Times) versus −0.31% month over month / 4.34% year over year (a Brazilian economic agenda). That's a 16 basis point difference in expectations — enough that the same printed number counts as a beat or a miss depending on which consensus you cite. I don't resolve it, I name both.

2. Warsh's speech lands after the fix. Jackson Hole runs Aug 27–29 in Wyoming, hosted by the Kansas City Fed, themed "Financial Innovation: Implications for Payments and Policy." Friday is traditionally the day of the marquee speech, usually around 10:00 New York time — that's 16:00 CEST, roughly 1¾ hours after the fix. Caveat: the official agenda wasn't retrievable; "Friday, ~10:00 ET" is the customary time, not a confirmed one.

Why this speech matters: it's Kevin Warsh's first policy speech as Fed chair at this symposium — he's held the role since May 2026 and is seen as a hawk. The federal funds rate has stood at 3.50–3.75% since Jul 29; for Sep 16, the FedWatch reading as of Aug 20 prices in no change at 68.4%. For scale: Powell's Jackson Hole speech in 2025 moved the probability of a September cut from 71 to 91.5 percent — in a single afternoon.

PCE price index: the price index the Fed uses to gauge whether inflation is really falling. Unlike the better-known consumer price index, it adjusts when people switch to alternatives because of high prices — like a shopping list that notes you bought chicken instead of pricey beef. It lands Wednesday for July, two days before Warsh's speech.

Focus from Aug 24: frozen up front, moving in back

Focus report: Brazil's central bank surveys over a hundred banks and funds every week for their forecasts on inflation, interest rates, growth, and the exchange rate, and publishes the averages on Monday. Like a weekly class poll asking "How do you think the test went?" — not the actual grade, but a very good sense of what everyone expects.

Yesterday's report looks boring at first glance, and isn't on closer inspection. Dollar-real for end-2026 sits at R$5.20 for the tenth week running. Ten weeks without movement, while the spot rate over the same period ranged between 5.06 and 5.21 — the consensus has simply decided not to forecast the election year.

Everything behind it has moved. The dollar for end-2027 was raised from 5.29 to R$5.30, the 2027 inflation expectation from 4.24 to 4.25%, and 2026 growth was cut from 1.98 to 1.95%. For 2026, inflation stays at 5.02% — unchanged for the second week, and well above the 3% target.

The most interesting figure is the Selic expectation. The policy rate stands at 14.00% today after four consecutive 25-basis-point cuts. Focus sees it at 13.75% by year-end — exactly one more step for the rest of the year. The consensus is implicitly saying: the cutting cycle is essentially over. For 2027, it's 12.00%.

My read Lower growth alongside stuck inflation is the combination that ties a central bank's hands: cutting helps the economy but prolongs the inflation problem. That's exactly why Brazil's yield advantage stays elevated longer than expected — and that's the news working against you. A real paying 14 percent interest while the ECB sits at 2.25% is hard for foreign capital to pass up. That Focus still sees the dollar at 5.20, above today's 5.1569, is the election-risk premium in numbers.
Carry trade: borrow money where it's cheap (euros at 2.25%) and invest it where it's expensive (Brazil at 14%). As long as the real doesn't crash, the rate gap is your profit. If it crashes, the party's over — which is why the real hangs so sensitively on election polls.

Rates, oil, and a swap roll — plus the gaps

Brazilian bonds ease back, but only a little. The ten-year government bond yielded 14.63% on Aug 24, 14 basis points below the prior day. That's a good 60 basis points above the Selic — the market still demands a premium to finance Brazil past the election. A falling long-term rate alongside a stable Selic is normally a confidence signal; at 14.63%, it's more of a very slow thaw.

Oil remains the quiet counterweight. Brent traded at $92.12 a barrel on Aug 25. Expensive oil supports Brazil as a net exporter — working against you. It also happens to undercut the oldest argument in the field: Itaú explicitly justified its June depreciation forecast with falling oil prices and deteriorating terms of trade. That premise no longer holds. Anyone citing Itaú's 5.30 should note that one of its three justifications has since flipped to the opposite.

The central bank is rolling, not intervening. On Aug 20, the BCB auctioned 25,000 contracts in each of two auctions — $2.5 billion combined — maturing Nov 3 and Dec 1, 2026. That continues the rollover, begun Aug 5, of contracts expiring Sep 1.

FX swap (swap cambial): Brazil's central bank sells a contract that behaves like a dollar for the buyer, without a single real dollar changing hands. Like a voucher for ice cream instead of ice cream: anyone wanting a hedge gets their hedge, and reserves stay untouched. Important: "rolling" just means replacing expiring contracts with new ones — this is not a new intervention and not a signal that the central bank is defending the rate.
Data gap What couldn't be documented today, and so appears here instead of vanishing: the central bank's stock of FX swaps (only rollover activity is documented), CFTC positioning in real futures, the DI futures curve, NTN-B real yields, and EWZ flows. It's Tuesday, August twenty-fifth, morning in Ribeirão Claro. Monday delivered exactly what this brief called for on Sunday, and today delivers its mirror image. Put the two days together and you get about the cleanest lesson on your exchange rate you're likely to see.

Monday first. The European reference rate for euro-real printed six point zero zero six eight. The forecast here had called for a range of five ninety-six to six-oh-seven, most likely six-oh-one. That wasn't a lucky call, though — it was arithmetic. The fix simply had to catch up with gains the real had already booked Friday afternoon, after the fixing window closed. On five thousand euros, that catch-up cost two hundred twenty-five reais.

How it happened is the interesting part. On Monday, the dollar strengthened against the euro and weakened against the real at the same time — both legs dragging your rate down together, zero point seven four percent in all. Today runs the opposite way: the dollar index is up zero point zero eight percent, the euro is down zero point zero eight, the real is down zero point zero eight. Three nearly identical numbers — and because they land in exactly the setup where everything cancels out in your rate, euro-real today is moving by a mere zero point zero six percent. Two days, essentially the same dollar move, a twelvefold difference in the outcome.

That's the rule to take away from this brief. Your rate isn't a dollar bet. It's a bet on euro versus real, and the dollar only shows up in it where it hits the two currencies unevenly. A headline about a strong dollar tells you nothing on its own. Only the question of who it's gotten stronger against, and by how much more, actually tells you something.

As for where things stand: your tradable rate today is around six-oh-one, calculated from two quotes from the same provider on the same day. That puts you practically back on Friday's tradable close. The gap between the reference rate and the market, which had torn open to three-quarters of a percent on Friday, is now fully closed.

Friday's fix won't see the reaction at all; the tradable market will see it immediately, and Monday's fix will catch up afterward. Brazil's preliminary inflation reading runs the opposite way: it lands Wednesday at nine in the morning Brazil time, a quarter hour before the fix, so it can still hit your reference rate the same day.

On timing: my twelve-month expectation sits at six-oh-five, and your tradable rate today is already around six-oh-one. A full year of waiting is worth roughly a hundred ninety-eight reais on five thousand euros, by that math. This one month's range alone moved fourteen twenty — seven times as much. The expected trend is much smaller than the noise. That's not a buy signal, it's a stance: waiting for a better rate has no expected payoff by this math, but grabbing the good days within your normal exchange rhythm still does. And every percentage in this brief is my own confidence level, not a market price.
