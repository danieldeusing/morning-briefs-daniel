Deflation in Brazil, and the Real Gets Stronger

The 30-second version

Wednesday delivered two inflation numbers that, by the textbook, should both have worked for you: Brazil printed its first monthly deflation in a year — normally the signal for faster rate cuts and a weaker real — and the US printed a too-hot number, which strengthens the dollar globally. Your rate fell anyway, by 0.16%. The reason is a mechanic that never makes a headline: when inflation falls and the policy rate holds still at the same moment, the rate left over after inflation rises — here from 9.07% to 9.36% in a single day. The deflation didn't make the real less attractive. It made it more attractive.

FX in detail: the deflation that made the real stronger

On Wednesday at 09:00 BRT, Brazil printed its first monthly deflation in a year: −0.40% for the month, versus a forecast of −0.32%. The twelve-month rate fell from 4.52% to 4.24%, dropping below the central bank's 4.5% tolerance ceiling for the first time since April.

By the textbook, that should have weakened the real. Less inflation means more room for rate cuts, less resistance to cutting means less yield for foreign money, and less yield means less demand for the real. Forward rates fell right on cue, especially in the short and medium contracts. The real got stronger anyway: the dollar lost 0.22% against it.

Real interest rate: the rate left over once you subtract inflation. If your savings account pays 5% and prices rise 4%, your real gain is 1%, not 5. Like a salary that rises 5% while your rent rises 4% — you're only richer by the difference.

And that's exactly where the contradiction resolves. The policy rate has stood at 14.00% since Aug 5 and didn't move on Wednesday. Inflation did. So the real rate rose the moment the number printed — by the math, from 9.07% to 9.36%, a 29-basis-point jump in a single day, without any central bank doing anything.

Carry trade: borrow where money is cheap (euros at 2.25%) and invest where it's expensive (Brazil at 14%). As long as the real doesn't collapse, the rate gap is your profit. But what matters for the investor's math isn't the rate on the label — it's the rate after inflation, and that got better, not worse, on Wednesday.
Rule of thumb Falling inflation is only bad for a currency once the central bank follows through. Here, three weeks separate the number from the rate decision — and for those three weeks, the real pays more in real terms than before. That's why the Copom date (Sep 15–16) matters more than the inflation date.

And in money terms: €5,000 bought R$30,076 at Tuesday's fix, R$30,027 at Wednesday's — R$49 less. Over the week that's R$232, and over the month, R$1,085 more than on Jul 27.

But the month belongs to the euro, not the real. Of the +3.75% since Jul 27, 2.46 percentage points come from the euro-dollar leg and only 1.26 from the dollar-real leg. The Dollar Index confirms it from the other side: −2.35% for the month. Anyone telling this month's story as "the real is collapsing" is telling the wrong story two-thirds of the time.

The Fed flips direction — and nobody has the number for it

The US PCE price index for July came in hot on Wednesday: 3.7% year over year, unchanged from June but above the forecast 3.6%. Month over month, +0.2% versus the expected +0.1%. The core rate stands at 3.3% year over year, which several outlets described as in line with consensus. Consumer spending stalled at the same time.

PCE price index: the price gauge the Fed uses to judge whether inflation is really falling. Unlike the better-known consumer price index, it adjusts when people switch to alternatives because of high prices — like a shopping list that notices you bought chicken instead of pricey beef.

The result is a regime change in the narrative. Reuters frames it as the number keeping alive the expectation of a Fed hike by year-end, calling Warsh's speech the ultimate test of that. On Tuesday, this brief still carried the FedWatch reading from Aug 20 showing 68.4% for "no change" on Sep 16 — as a debate over whether the Fed would cut. That question is no longer on the table.

Data gap, named honestly There's no fresh rate probability today. The usual sources only render their numbers in the browser and return nothing to a fetch. The last verified reading stays 68.4% hold on Sep 16, as of Aug 20 — and it's substantively outdated by the Aug 26 PCE. I'm deliberately not writing a new figure here: the direction of the discussion is documented, its magnitude isn't.

Why this matters to you despite being an American story: a Fed that doesn't cut, or even hikes, keeps the dollar elevated. A strong dollar hits emerging-market currencies first, because their investors then have no reason to take on risk for yield. That lifts EUR/BRL. The catch: a strong dollar also weighs on the euro, and that second leg pulls the other way. Net, what's left for you is only whatever hits the real harder than the euro — historically the case, but not guaranteed.

The date to watch: Jackson Hole runs Aug 27–29 under the theme "Financial Innovation: Implications for Payments and Policy." Warsh's keynote is scheduled for Friday, 10:00 ET — 16:00 CEST, 11:00 BRT, roughly 1¾ hours after Friday's fix.

IPCA-15: benign headline, stubborn core

IPCA-15: Brazil's official inflation gauge, but with a collection window that closes about two weeks earlier — a preview of the number that later arrives as the IPCA. Like a progress report before the report card: not final, but a pretty accurate read on where things are headed.

The deflation didn't come from everywhere — it came from three groups: housing −1.41%, transport −1.00%, food and beverages −0.57%. Clothing and communication eased slightly. That's a pattern worth reading with caution — in Brazil, housing mostly means electricity, and electricity prices hinge on the tariff flag being reset tomorrow.

The core tells the other half of the story. The average of the core indices rose 0.23% for the month, slightly below the 0.26% forecast, and stands at 4.3% year over year — essentially where the headline rate sits. The diffusion index came in at 52%.

Core rate and diffusion index: the core rate strips out the wildest items — fuel, electricity, fresh food — to see what's left of inflation once you ignore the weather and oil prices. The diffusion index simply counts what share of all products in the basket got more expensive. 52% means: despite deflation in the headline number, a good half of all prices still rose. So the deflation is narrow, not broad.

That's why the banks disagree. Genial Investimentos sees enough evidence for a September cut; Marcela Kawauti of Lifetime Gestora calls the result a confirmation that the cutting cycle continues. Inter expects 25 basis points at every remaining meeting of the year and lands at 13.25% by year-end. Goldman Sachs, by contrast, sees "little room" for further cuts, arguing the August deflation is seasonal and non-recurring while services pressure persists. Mirae Asset calls the composition mostly benign — while noting that underlying services accelerated more than projected.

My read I lean closer to Goldman than to the consensus, for a reason baked into the number itself: if 52% of prices are rising and the core rate sits at 4.3% year over year, the central bank hasn't seen disinflation — it's seen a good month. Focus was still penciling in 13.75% for year-end last week, meaning exactly one more step. Monday's upcoming report is the first to digest this number — and if it moves little, the market will have sided with Goldman. For you, that would mean fewer rate cuts, a stronger real for longer, and a pricier exchange for longer.

Positioning, bonds, and a chip quarter

The central bank is rolling, not intervening. On Aug 20, the BCB rolled 50,000 contracts — $2.5 billion combined — into maturities in November and December 2026. That continues the rollover, begun Aug 5, of contracts expiring Sep 1. This is rollover activity, not the outstanding stock — the stock figure wasn't obtainable anywhere across the source chain.

Swap cambial (FX swap): Brazil's central bank sells a contract that behaves like a dollar for the buyer, without a single real dollar changing hands. Like a voucher for ice cream instead of ice cream. "Rolling" just means replacing expiring contracts with new ones — it's not a new intervention and not a signal that the central bank is defending the rate.

The stock market is standing still, and so is oil. The Ibovespa closed at 174,586 points (+0.01%), Brent at $88.88 a barrel (−0.03%). Expensive oil supports Brazil as a net exporter — that is, against you. Morgan Stanley brackets the Ibovespa through mid-2027 between 240,000 and 130,000 — a factor of two across the election, an honest admission of how little is plannable here.

And a chip quarter that reaches you indirectly. Nvidia reported $96.2 billion in revenue after the close yesterday (+106% year over year). The stock rose 4.7% after hours, S&P futures 0.4%, the Asian index 0.7%.

Risk-on: when investors worldwide feel bold, money flows into riskier, higher-yielding markets — emerging markets like Brazil first. When they're nervous, it flows back into the dollar. Like a school class that floods onto the sports field in good weather and runs back to the auditorium in the rain. That's why an American chip quarter can move your exchange rate without anything changing in Brazil at all.

Brazil first. The preliminary reading for August inflation came in at minus zero point four zero percent for the month, versus a forecast of minus zero point three two — the first monthly deflation in a year. By the textbook, the real should have weakened from here. Less inflation means more room to cut rates, and less room to cut means less reason for foreign money to come back for yield. The opposite happened: the dollar lost zero point two two percent against the real. The reason sits in a mechanic no headline captures.
