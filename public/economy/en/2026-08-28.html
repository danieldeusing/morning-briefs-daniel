FX in detail: 83% of your month is an ECB bet

Yesterday's fix came in at 5.9879 — the first reading below 6.00 since Aug 14. On the day, that's −0.29% or R$88 on €5,000. On the week, −1.06% or R$320. That's the entire bad news, and it's small.

The real number is the month, and the breakdown is surprising. EUR/BRL has risen 2.96% since Jul 28. But your pair has no independent existence — it's the product of two rates. And they contributed very unevenly: the euro gained 2.45% against the dollar, the dollar gained only 0.50% against the real.

EUR/USD +2.45%× USD/BRL +0.50%= EUR/BRL +2.96%

Multiply the two together and you get exactly the monthly figure. This isn't an approximation, it's arithmetic on real ECB fixings. In money terms: of the R$860 this month brought you on €5,000, about R$715 comes from the euro and about R$145 from the real.

Why a rate has two legs: no dealer trades euros directly for reais — the pair is too small for that. In practice, every euro first becomes dollars, and those dollars then become reais. Like a flight with no direct connection: you route through Frankfurt, and if your connection there runs late, you arrive late even if the sun is shining in São Paulo. For you, that means: two central banks set your rate, not one.

And that's how August got a date. The euro didn't rise in August by chance. It rose because the market priced in the ECB's Sep 10 meeting: in mid-July, the probability of a hike stood at around 80% according to one of the two surveys, yesterday at 84 to 87%. The deposit rate is set to rise from 2.25% to 2.50% — the ECB is the only major central bank whose most recent move was a hike (effective Jun 17, 2026).

"Priced in": the market doesn't wait for the meeting to react — it reacts beforehand, as soon as it thinks the outcome is likely. Like a child who already lights up when the parents take the cake out of the oven, not only at the first bite. Consequence: if the ECB hikes on Sep 10 as expected, almost nothing happens to the euro anymore. Only a surprise can still move it.

And that's exactly where the risk lies for you. Reuters, via RTE, cites three sources saying the ECB will hike to dampen the energy price pass-through from the Iran war — eurozone inflation in July stood at 2.9%, the energy component at +10.0% — but with "no appetite for more": long-term expectations are said to be firmly anchored at 2%, with "no case for signaling further tightening."

The scenario that costs you the most A hike that's already 85% priced in, plus a press conference that says "and that's it for now." That's a dovish hike — the rate move happens, but the expectation of further moves disappears. The 2.45 percentage points the euro brought you this month hang directly on that expectation. They can vanish on Sep 10 at 14:45 CEST, without anything happening in Brazil at all. The market, per the same report, expects "one to two more hikes" — that's the number at stake, not the Sep 10 one.

The counter-date lands on Sep 1: the eurozone's flash August inflation estimate. If it comes in above 2.9%, the Council can hardly wave it off. If it comes in below, "hike with reservations" quickly turns into "hike and go quiet."

Brazil pulls in two directions

Yesterday Brazil printed two numbers, both big, and they work in opposite directions for your rate. That's the real tension of the day, and it doesn't get resolved in the headlines, because one number lands in the labor-market column and the other in the trade column.

First: the unemployment rate fell to 5.3% in the quarter through July, down from 5.4% through June and 5.6% a year ago — according to IBGE, the lowest reading for this quarter since the series began in 2012. The number of unemployed people fell to 5.8 million, down 8%, or 503,000 people, from the quarter through April. The composite underutilization rate fell from 13.8% to 13.0%.

Underutilization rate: the unemployment rate only counts people actively searching and finding nothing. Underutilization also counts everyone working too few hours or who's given up the search out of frustration. Like the difference between "how many kids are absent today?" and "how many are here but not participating?" Both numbers falling together means: the labor market is genuinely tight, not just tight on paper.

Second: the current account missed badly. The July deficit came in at $8.11 billion, the Reuters poll had expected $6.6 billion. Foreign direct investment came in at $7.46 billion — below the roughly $7.9 billion expected and 11% below the $8.402 billion of July 2025. Over twelve months, the deficit adds up to $62.9 billion, against $61.7 billion in the year-earlier period.

Current account and its coverage: a country spends more abroad than it earns — someone has to finance the gap. The preferred source is foreign direct investment: a factory, a company acquisition, money that stays for years. The alternative is portfolio money, which can leave again in a single afternoon. Like the difference between a long-term tenant and a weekend guest. In July, foreign direct investment covered only 92% of the deficit — the rest has to come from the weekend guest.

The Copom sits right between these two numbers, and the banks are genuinely split. TradingEconomics reads the tight labor market as a reason to pause the cycle of 25-basis-point cuts per meeting. On that side stand XP and C6 Bank (cycle over, Selic at 14.00% by year-end) and Goldman Sachs ("little room," calling the August deflation seasonal). Against that, Inter (13.75% in September, then 25 basis points every meeting down to 13.25%), JP Morgan, and Genial expect a cut; Itaú sees year-end at 13.75%.

My read I lean toward a pause, on the strength of the evidence in both data sets. The Aug 26 deflation was one number from one month, one where the diffusion index still showed 52% of all prices rising. The labor market is a series that has pointed the same direction for months and has now hit a record going back to 2012. A central bank forced to choose between one good month and a firm trend chooses the trend. For you, that would mean: the real rate of roughly 9.4% holds, the real stays well paid, your rate stays capped. Monday's Focus report will show whether the market is doing the same math.

The Treasury gives up on duration

Almost unnoticed, Brazil's Tesouro Nacional revised its annual financing plan yesterday — today's longest-lasting piece of news. The target share of Selic-linked paper in the federal debt stock by year-end rises to 49–53%, up from 46–50% at the start of the year. In exchange, inflation-linked paper falls to 21–25% (from 23–27%) and fixed-rate paper to 20–24% (from 21–25%). The year-end debt stock stays at R$9.7 to 10.3 trillion, with average remaining maturity at 3.7–4.1 years.

Selic-linked versus fixed-rate: the government borrows money and can promise one of two things in return. Either a fixed rate for ten years — in which case the buyer carries the risk that rates rise in the meantime. Or a rate that tracks the policy rate — in which case the government carries the risk. Like a phone plan with a fixed price versus one where the price is recalculated every month. When customers suddenly want only the tracking plan, that says something about their expectations.

The official justification is worded harmlessly: the change reflects "higher demand for these instruments, which investors seek out more in moments of uncertainty and volatility." Exactly. The election is five weeks away, and nobody wants to hold a Brazilian ten-year with a fixed coupon when the aftermath could bring a budget that Morgan Stanley's extreme case models with an 18% ten-year yield.

Why this affects you Two effects, both friendly for you — that is, rate-lifting. First, it's a risk-premium signal: the market demands a price for locking in a rate across the election that the government isn't willing to pay, so it steps aside. Second, more floating-rate debt makes the interest burden more rate-sensitive — with the Selic at 14% and a debt-to-GDP ratio heading, per a UBS write-up, toward 84% of GDP by year-end, every rate cut that doesn't happen immediately gets more expensive. This is exactly where the Copom pause bites: what supports the real makes the budget more expensive. The debt ratio is a secondary-source figure — read it as such.

And that's the thread back to Goldman. The same bank describes its election base case as "fiscal muddling-through, with forward rates staying high and the real protected by the real carry." The plan revision is the accounting behind exactly that sentence: the government muddles through by swallowing the rate risk itself — and the real stays protected for as long as the real carry stays high.

The central bank is rolling, not intervening. On Aug 20, the BCB rolled 50,000 contracts — $2.5 billion combined — into maturities in November and December 2026, continuing the rollover of contracts expiring Sep 1 that began Aug 5. This is rollover activity, not the outstanding stock — the stock figure again wasn't obtainable anywhere across the source chain.

Swap cambial (FX swap): Brazil's central bank sells a contract that behaves like a dollar for the buyer, without a single real dollar changing hands. Like a voucher for ice cream instead of ice cream. "Rolling" just means replacing expiring contracts with new ones — it's not a new intervention and not a signal that the central bank is defending the rate.

Both at once isn't a contradiction — they're different time horizons.

It's Friday, August twenty-eighth, morning in Ribeirão Claro. Yesterday your exchange rate dropped back below six for the first time since August fourteenth — and by this morning it was already back above it. The daily move is noise. The real story is what's inside this entire month.

Your pair doesn't exist on its own — it's built from two rates: euro against dollar, and dollar against real. Since July twenty-eighth, your rate is up two point nine six percent. Of that, two point four five percentage points came from the euro side, and only zero point five zero from the real side. In other words, about eighty-three percent of this month's gain is a bet on the European Central Bank, not on Brazil. In money terms: of the roughly eight hundred sixty reais this month brought you on five thousand euros, about seven hundred fifteen came from the euro and only one hundred forty-five from the real.

And that bet now has a date. The euro didn't rise in August by accident — it rose because markets priced in the European Central Bank's meeting on September tenth. In mid-July, the odds of a rate hike stood at around eighty percent; yesterday, they stood at eighty-four to eighty-seven. The deposit rate is expected to rise from two point two five to two point five percent — Europe is the only major central bank whose last move was a hike.

But that's also exactly where the risk sits. Reuters, citing three sources on the European Central Bank's Council, reports the bank will hike to offset the energy-price hit from the Iran war — but, in its own words, with no appetite for more. If a hike that's already eighty-five percent priced in comes with a press conference that signals a stop, that would strip the euro of exactly the expectation that made you money this month. September tenth, for you, isn't a buying opportunity — it's a risk date.

Brazil printed two big numbers yesterday that pull in opposite directions for your rate. First, the unemployment rate fell to five point three percent, the lowest reading for this quarter since records began in twenty twelve — a genuinely tight labor market, and the best argument for Brazil's central bank to pause its rate-cutting cycle. Second, the current account missed badly: a July deficit of eight point one one billion dollars against an expected six point six, with foreign direct investment covering only ninety-two percent of the gap — the rest has to come from flightier portfolio money, which raises the stakes of October's election.

Brazil's rate-setters sit squarely between those two numbers, and the banks are honestly split. XP and C6 Bank say the cutting cycle is over at fourteen percent; Goldman Sachs sees little room to keep cutting. Inter, JP Morgan, and Genial expect a cut to thirteen point seven five in September instead. I lean toward a pause: the labor-market trend has pointed the same direction for months and just hit a record, while the recent inflation dip was one month where, by the diffusion index, a slim majority of prices still rose. A pause would keep the real well paid and keep your rate capped.

A third story almost slipped under the radar, and it may outlast the rest. Brazil's Treasury revised its funding plan, raising the target share of debt whose interest tracks the policy rate to forty-nine to fifty-three percent, while trimming fixed-rate and inflation-linked bonds. Translation: with the election five weeks away, investors don't want to lock in a rate anymore, so the government is absorbing that risk itself — a signal about how nervous the market already is.

This afternoon, Kevin Warsh delivers his first policy speech as Fed chair at Jackson Hole — ten a.m. New York time, eleven a.m. in Brasília, four p.m. in Central Europe, about one hour and forty-five minutes after today's European reference rate is set. So today's fix won't capture whatever he says; the tradable market will react immediately, but Monday's fix is the first to reflect it. Roughly a third of bond traders are now pricing in a Fed rate hike for September sixteenth, not a cut — though there's no precise probability available today, so I'm not inventing one.

On timing: my twelve-month expectation stays at six point one zero, against a tradable rate today of about six point zero one six. A full year of waiting is worth roughly four hundred twenty-two reais on five thousand euros by the math. This single month's range alone was twelve hundred eighty-six reais — more than three times as much. The expected trend still stays much smaller than the noise. I'm deliberately not moving today's twelve-month number, because today's two new facts — Brazil's wider external deficit, and Goldman's real-friendly election odds paired with a more likely Copom pause — largely cancel each other out. All percentages in this brief reflect my own confidence, not the market's pricing. Monday's brief will settle two things: what Warsh actually said this afternoon, and how the Focus report reads yesterday's Brazilian numbers.
