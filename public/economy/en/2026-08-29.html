FX in detail: this time it was Brazil

The fix jumped back above 6.00, to 6.0126 — +0.41% on the day. On €5,000, that's R$124. That's the whole story for the day; the month is what's interesting.

Since Aug 3 — the first trading day of the calendar month — your pair is up +2.86%. Your rate has no existence of its own: it's the product of euro-against-dollar and dollar-against-real. And in this window, the two legs delivered very unevenly.

EUR/USD +0.94%× USD/BRL +1.91%= EUR/BRL +2.86%

Roughly two-thirds of your monthly gain is real weakness, roughly one-third euro strength. In money terms: of the R$836 this month brought you on €5,000, R$557 comes from Brazil and R$279 from Europe.

A contradiction with yesterday — and why both are right Yesterday's brief said 83% of the month was euro. Today it says 33%. The only difference is the start date. The rolling 30-day window begins on Jul 28, and in the four trading days through month-end, the euro jumped from 1.1367 to 1.1485 — a good one percent that's no longer included in the calendar-month window. Run the rolling month through today instead, and it's still around 72% euro. The lesson isn't the number, it's the sensitivity: a breakdown without a stated window is worthless, and four trading days can flip it from a "euro story" to a "real story." From today on, I'll name the window in every breakdown.
Why a rate has two legs: no dealer trades large amounts of euros directly for reais — the pair is too small for that. In practice, every euro first becomes dollars, and the dollars then become reais. Like a flight with no direct connection: you route through Frankfurt, and if the connection there runs late, you arrive late even if the sun is shining in São Paulo. For you, that means: two central banks set your rate, not one — and which one is calling the tune keeps changing.

In practice, that switch matters. A rate carried by real weakness hangs on Brazil's election premium, the Copom, and the fiscal narrative — that is, on events with a date, running through Oct 4. A rate carried by the euro hangs on a single ECB press conference. Both are vulnerable, but they break at different times. Anyone who waited in August on the euro argument got the right result for the wrong reason.

Warsh turns the tone — after the fixing

Kevin Warsh's first policy speech as Fed chair at Jackson Hole turned the dollar on Friday. The core message: the Fed still has "work to do" on inflation, and rate hikes could become necessary. The bond market answered immediately.

Warsh: "work to do"→ 2-yr +6 bp to 4.298%→ DXY +0.52%→ USD/BRL +0.43%

The pattern in yields is the real news. The two-year yield rose 6 basis points to 4.298%, the thirty-year fell 2 to 5.168%. Short up, long down — that's the classic signature of expected tightening.

Why short and long rates move in opposite directions: the two-year yield is essentially a bet on what the central bank does over the next two years — it tracks policy-rate expectations almost immediately. The thirty-year is a bet on inflation and growth over a generation. When a central bank credibly says "I'm going to crack down harder," the first one rises (money costs more now) and the second one falls (less inflation later). Like a father announcing that starting tomorrow, no more candy is allowed: today everyone stocks up fast, but everyone also expects less tooth decay next year.

The concrete consequence: the market-implied probability of a hike of at least 25 basis points at the FOMC meeting on Sep 15–16 jumped to around 50%, up from around 35% before the speech. A second report from the same day puts it at 55.7%, and a jump of about 20 percentage points versus the day before. Both figures are snapshots from the same trading day, not conflicting measurements — the reliable takeaway is the 50–56% range and the direction, not the decimal point.

That rests on a foundation that's been visible for weeks: the Fed has held at 3.50–3.75% since December; Jul 29 was the year's fifth meeting without a move, decided 9-3 — and the three dissenters wanted to hike. Warsh isn't saying anything new about the Fed; he's saying out loud what's been sitting in the minutes for a while.

The timestamp that matters The ECB reference rate is set around 14:15 CEST, which is 09:15 in Ribeirão Claro. Friday's euro-dollar fix moved 0.02% (1.1645 → 1.1643), the dollar index 0.52%. Two measurements of the same dollar diverging that far can only mean one thing: they come from different hours. The fix you see above doesn't know Warsh yet. This conclusion is an inference from the pattern — I couldn't independently confirm the exact time of the speech.

The election premium got a price tag

Goldman Sachs raised its dollar-real forecast — didn't lower it. That's notable, because practically every other anchor has stood still for weeks. New: 5.20 at three months, 5.10 at six, 5.00 at twelve. Previously the sequence was 4.90 / 5.00 / 5.00 — so the twelve-month target stayed the same, only the path there shifted upward. Reasoning: Brazil's election demands a bigger risk premium.

Risk premium: the markup an investor demands just to touch an uncertain asset at all. Like a friend who lends you €100 — from a reliable payer, he just wants it back; from an unreliable one, he wants €110. Ahead of an election with unclear fiscal policy, Brazil is the second case: investors stay, but they want more return, and that pushes down the real's rate.

Goldman also assigned probabilities to the election outcomes. The branch most friendly to Brazilian assets — a right-wing win with orthodox fiscal policy — gets 40% and is modeled as -4% on dollar-real. At the same time, the bank notes that without clearer fiscal consolidation after the election, it sees "little room" for dollar-real to stay durably below 5.00.

The polling backs up the uncertainty. Datafolha surveyed 2,058 voters from Aug 18 to 20 (margin of error ±2 points): Lula 39%, Flávio Bolsonaro 32 to 33% — depending on how the pollster reports it — with the runoff at 47 to 43. In July it stood at 40 to 32. A four-point gap in the runoff, with a ±2-point margin of error, is mathematically still open. Another pollster reads the same week at 41 to 36, with a technical tie in the runoff — a different survey, a different methodology; I deliberately don't mix the two data sets.

My read: Goldman is currently the only named bank with a fresh real forecast dated to the post-election reassessment. That's a thin basis for such a large share of my 3-to-6-month view, and I'd rather say so than paper over it. Itaú's figure (around 5.35 for 2027) dates from April and no longer holds up in today's environment. A second fresh Brazilian voice is missing — and it's missing precisely where Brazilian banks are usually better informed than global ones.

Positioning — and what's missing today

The central bank is rolling, not intervening. The BCB began on Aug 5 rolling the FX swaps maturing Sep 1, to keep the maturity itself from creating volatility. This is rollover activity, not the outstanding stock — and the maturity date happens to fall right on GDP Tuesday.

FX swap (swap cambial): Brazil's central bank sells a contract that behaves like a dollar for the buyer, without a single real dollar changing hands. Like a voucher for ice cream instead of ice cream. "Rolling" just means replacing expiring contracts with new ones — it's not an intervention, and not a signal that the central bank is defending the rate.

Weekly wrap from a market overview dated Aug 24: dollar-real down 6.15% year over year, up 1.43% month over month; the dollar index down 1.09% for the month. Both signs have since flipped on a day-over-day basis — Friday's figures above are the fresher read.

The lesson isn't the number, it's how sensitive it is: a breakdown without a stated time window is worthless. From now on, every one names its window.

The second big story comes out of Wyoming. Kevin Warsh gave his first policy speech as chair of the Federal Reserve at Jackson Hole and said the Fed still has work to do on inflation — rate hikes could become necessary. Bond markets answered immediately. The two-year Treasury yield rose six basis points to four point two nine eight percent, the thirty-year fell two. Short rates up, long rates down — that's the signature of an expected tightening. The odds of a September rate hike jumped from around thirty-five to somewhere between fifty and fifty-six percent.

And here's the detail that matters for you. The European reference rate is set around a quarter past two Central European time — a quarter past nine where you are. Friday's euro-dollar fix moved just two hundredths of a percent, while the dollar index gained over half a percent. Two measurements of the same dollar diverging that much can only mean one thing: they were taken at different hours. The rate you're looking at predates the speech. Monday's fix is the first one that will have heard it. That conclusion follows from the pattern — I couldn't independently confirm the exact time of the speech.
