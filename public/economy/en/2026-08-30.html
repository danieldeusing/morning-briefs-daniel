A book that only points one way

CFTC positioning report (COT): the US futures regulator publishes every Friday how the big market participants were positioned the Tuesday before — broken out by asset managers, hedge funds, and banks. Analogy: imagine a class takes a vote, and three days later a sheet goes up showing who voted how. Too late to vote yourself, but early enough to see whether everyone stood on the same side.

For the real contract on the Chicago futures exchange, that sheet looks like this (as of Aug 25, published Aug 28, total open interest 131,553 contracts):

GroupLongShortNet
Asset managers50,7131,834+48,879
Hedge funds38,84924,136+14,713
Banks / dealers14,05459,822-45,768
Other reportables5,55528,215-22,660

The decisive number isn't the net, it's the ratio: 50,713 to 1,834 is roughly 28 to 1. Among asset managers, there's practically no dissenting view left. Net long here means long the real — the contract is quoted in dollars per real, so buying it is a bet on a stronger real. Real money and hedge funds together hold 63,592 net long contracts; the other side is almost entirely banks hedging client business — not convinced sellers.

Crowded trade: when almost everyone holds the same bet, there's no one left to buy more of it — the price stops rising even though everyone wants to be right. Analogy: a boat where everyone stands on the same side. As long as nothing happens, it floats. If someone shouts "watch out," everyone runs to the other side at once, and it capsizes — not because of the shout, but because of the simultaneity.
28:1 long real→ no buyers left→ bad fiscal news→ everyone sells at once→ USD/BRL up→ EUR/BRL up

My read, and it cuts against the first instinct: this isn't a reason to think the real is strong — it's a reason to see the tail risks as asymmetric. Positioning says nothing about whether the consensus is right; it says what happens if it's wrong. And that outcome sits on the friendly side for you: unwinding the long position pushes EUR/BRL higher. The price for that is a binary political event on Oct 4 whose outcome nobody knows. Caveat: the report is five days old and covers only the Chicago contract — not the far larger spot market in São Paulo. It shows sentiment, not the market's overall balance.

Warsh, read two days later

Yesterday this said that the tone had turned. Now it's clear what Kevin Warsh is basing that on — and that's the harder part. He cited twelve-month PCE inflation at 3.7% and added that 54% of the basket is still rising faster than 3%. Summer data had been better than expected, but "not enough to establish a meaningful shift"; the 2 percent target remains "firm and unmoved."

Breadth of inflation: not how high inflation is, but how many prices are rising at the same time. Analogy: if only coffee gets more expensive at the supermarket, that's a coffee problem. If half the shelves get more expensive, that's a money problem — and only rates fix that.

The bond market read the statement accordingly, and the numbers land harder than in Friday midday's first report. Two-year US yields closed +11 basis points to 4.32–4.34% — the highest level since July — thirty-year yields eased 2 basis points to 5.168%, ten-year yields stood at 4.67% ahead of the speech and rose to roughly 4.72–4.73% afterward (+4–6bp).

PCE 3.7%, breadth 54%→ 2-yr +11 bp→ 30-yr -2 bp→ curve flattens

The flattening curve is the most interesting part. Short rates up, long rates down means: the market believes the Fed is braking now — and choking off growth later. The long end isn't buying the inflation story that the short end is pricing in.

The hike probability for Sep 16 rose during the day from around 35% to about 50 to 57% — four providers, four snapshots of the same day, ending near 56 to 57%. I'm deliberately citing the range and not an average: an average would be false precision from four different times of day. The Fed has stood at 3.50–3.75% since Jul 29, held by 9 to 3 — the three dissenters wanted to hike even then.

The counter-evidence I'm not hiding On the same day, consumers' one-year inflation expectations (University of Michigan) fell from 4.3% to 4.0%, while the five-year expectation held at 3.3%. That's a dovish data point on a hawkish day — and a reason why a September hike remains a coin flip, not a done deal.

It's Sunday, August thirtieth. Second consecutive non-trading day, no new reference rate, no fresh numbers off the ticker. The euro still stands at six point zero one two six reais, same as Friday at a quarter past two in the afternoon. On five thousand euros, that's thirty thousand sixty-three reais.

When prices stand still, there's time for two questions that usually get lost on a weekday. First: who's actually positioned how? Second: are the published forecasts even still current? Both answers are uncomfortable today.

Start with positioning. The American futures regulator publishes every Friday how the big players were lined up the Tuesday before. For the Brazilian real, this report shows a picture you rarely see this clean. Asset managers hold fifty thousand seven hundred thirteen contracts betting on a stronger real, and only one thousand eight hundred thirty-four betting on a weaker one. That's a ratio of roughly twenty-eight to one. There's practically no dissenting view left in this group.

Picture a boat where every passenger stands on the same side. As long as nothing happens, it floats steady. If someone shouts "watch out," everyone rushes to the other side at once, and it capsizes. Not because of the shout — because of the simultaneity. That's exactly the situation in the real, five weeks before the presidential election on October fourth.

For you, that's actually good news, even if it doesn't sound like it. If this book flips, everyone sells the real at the same time. The dollar rises against the real, and your euro rises with it. You get more reais for your money. Other people are carrying the risk in this position. For you, it's the upside at the edge.

Now the second question, the forecasts. I looked up the publication date for every bank forecast on the real, and the result is sobering. Exactly one house is fresh: Goldman Sachs, with five point two zero at three months, five point one zero at six, and five even at twelve. Itaú is from June twenty-sixth, BTG from May tenth, Bank of America from March twenty-sixth. Two to five months old.

Bank of America shows how much that matters. Its forecast assumes a Brazilian policy rate of eleven point seventy-five percent. It's actually at fourteen. The number isn't miscalculated — it describes a different world.

On the euro side, it's even trickier. The only fresh forecast comes from ING, dated the twenty-fourth and twenty-sixth of August, putting the euro at one eighteen by year-end. It's based on a cooling American labor market and a European Central Bank tightening ahead of the Fed. Two days later, new Fed chair Warsh signaled exactly the opposite at Jackson Hole. The thesis isn't disproven — it's untested. It gets tested on Friday, when American jobs numbers come out.

Cross all these forecasts onto the euro-real pair, and you get a statement that matters for your timing. Apart from one four-month-old outlier forecast, no common combination brings your rate below five point nine within a year. The center sits between five point nine four and six point two five — at or above where you are today. There's no published case for waiting in hopes of a much better rate.

Tomorrow it gets concrete. At a quarter past two Central European time comes the first reference rate that knows Warsh's speech. And in the morning, Brazil's central bank publishes its market report — the first survey taken after the turn. Its dollar forecast has held steady at five point two reais for ten weeks. For the first time since mid-June, it can move. After that comes Tuesday's Brazilian second-quarter growth number, and Friday's American jobs report.
