Why nothing moved

Your rate is a product of two numbers: how many dollars a euro costs, and how many reais a dollar costs. On Friday, Warsh moved both — in opposite directions.

Warsh turns hawkish→ EUR/USD 1.1643 → 1.1590 (−0.46%)→ USD/BRL 5.1641 → 5.1935 (+0.57%)→ EUR/BRL +0.11%

The euro loses against the dollar — that pushes your rate down. The real loses against the dollar — that pushes it up. Both effects are almost equal in size, so almost nothing is left over: 6.0193 crossed against 6.0126 as the fix — eleven hundredths of a percent, roughly R$34 on €5,000.

Why a dollar shock barely moves your pair: EUR/BRL doesn't contain a dollar at all — it cancels out. Analogy: you measure your brother's height by both of you standing against the same doorframe. If someone moves the doorframe, both readings change — but the difference between you stays the same. The dollar is the doorframe. Your rate only really changes when the euro or the real moves under its own steam.

That gives you a rule for the whole week: don't watch the dollar. Watch whether a piece of news applies to only one of the two legs. Tuesday's euro flash inflation estimate is one. Brazil's GDP the same day is another. Friday's US jobs report, by contrast, hits both legs at once — and will probably move things less than the buildup around it suggests.

Warsh: hold becomes hike

[USA] Kevin Warsh's first Jackson Hole speech as Fed chair, on Friday, Aug 28, was the only event this summer that genuinely turned rate expectations. According to reports from the Washington Post and CNBC, he called himself "impressed" by the economy's strength, but voiced concern that the "underlying trends" in inflation hadn't improved — and again refused to give any forward guidance on the rate path ahead.

Forward guidance: a central bank's promise of roughly where rates are headed. Analogy: the driver announces ahead of time that he's about to brake — so everyone can hold on. Warsh announces nothing. That makes every single data release more important, because the market has no other map.

The effect was immediately measurable: the probability of a hike on Sep 16 jumped from around 35% to nearly 60%; a second reading of the same futures contracts puts it at 60.4%, versus roughly 56% intraday. The policy rate stands at 3.50–3.75%. The data backs Warsh up: July PCE inflation, released Aug 26, came in at 3.7% year over year (core 3.3%), with the monthly rate 0.1 points above consensus.

My read: the market is pricing a hike that remains a coin flip. 60% isn't certainty — but it's enough to broadly support the dollar, and that's why Brazil's dollar leg gained +0.67% on Friday and +1.00% for the week. For you, that only matters as long as the euro leg doesn't follow suit.

Larak Island — and the crack in Itaú's thesis

[Global] US forces on Sunday, Aug 30, struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz — the first military action in roughly a month, in a war that's been running for more than half a year. The launchers were reportedly meant to fire sea mines into the strait, according to US officials. Iran vowed retaliation. Brent rose 1.9% to $89.79, WTI 1.8% to $84.94; on Friday, Brent had stood at $88.29.

Terms of trade: the price ratio between what a country sells and what it buys. Analogy: if your neighbor pays you more for your apples while bread stays the same price, you're better off — without working any harder. Brazil sells oil, soy, and iron ore. When oil rises, Brazil's terms of trade improve, more dollars flow into the country, and the real tends to strengthen.

That's the real point for you, and it's uncomfortable. Itaú bases its weaker-real call (5.30 by year-end, 5.50 for 2027) on three pillars: high US rates, election risk — and worsening terms of trade from lower oil prices. That third pillar just tipped in the opposite direction. Conversely: BTG raised its forecast from 4.90 to 5.40 in July, citing a tougher Fed — a rationale Warsh confirmed on Friday.

Two caveats I won't hide. First, the opposite direction is just as plausible: an escalation at Hormuz is a classic risk-off event, and money flows into the dollar during those — bad for the real and therefore good for your rate. Second, Brazil's own oil question is currently tangled up in a legal fight: a federal court suspended the 12% export tax on crude oil on Aug 27, and hours later the government extended it through Nov 6. The Ibovespa still closed +0.30% at 175,665, its eighth straight winning day, led by Petrobras at +2.0%.

Brazil: weaker GDP as a lever on the rate

[BR] On Sep 1, statistics agency IBGE releases Q2 GDP. Consensus expects a slowdown to +0.4 to +0.5% quarter over quarter after a robust Q1. July industrial production follows on Sep 2.

Why this matters for the rate: a weak GDP gives the Copom room to keep cutting. The Selic has stood at 14.00% since Aug 5; the Focus median (survey Aug 24) sees 13.75% by year-end and 12.00% by end-2027, and Banco Inter even considers 13.25% by December possible. The futures market is already pricing that in: the January 2027 DI contract stood at 13.615% on Aug 28.

Carry: the rate advantage a country offers foreign money. Analogy: two savings accounts, one pays 2%, the other 14%. As long as the 14% country's currency doesn't collapse, the difference is your profit. That exact advantage has pulled money into Brazil and supported the real in recent months. Every Copom cut shrinks it — so does every Fed hike.

The headwind to the rate-cut story remains inflation: IPCA stood at 4.44% year over year in July, and Focus sees 2026 at 5.02% — well above the 3% target midpoint. And domestic-demand stocks read the situation skeptically: MRV −5.1%, Magazine Luiza −4.6% on Friday.

It's Monday, August thirty-first. A lot happened overnight, and yet your rate stands almost exactly where it stood on Friday. The last published reference rate from the European Central Bank is six point zero one two six reais per euro. On five thousand euros, that's thirty thousand sixty-three reais. Today's fix isn't out yet — it comes at a quarter past two in the afternoon, Central European time.

That nothing moved isn't a coincidence, it's arithmetic. Your rate is a product of two numbers: how many dollars a euro buys, and how many reais a dollar buys. New Fed chair Kevin Warsh's hawkish speech on Friday moved both — in opposite directions. The euro lost just under half a percent against the dollar. That pushes your rate down. The real lost a bit more than half a percent against the dollar. That pushes it up. What's left over is eleven hundredths of a percent — on five thousand euros, about thirty-four reais.

Picture measuring your brother's height by both of you standing against the same doorframe. If someone moves the doorframe, both readings change — but the difference between you stays the same. The dollar is that doorframe. A pure dollar shock barely moves your pair. What really moves it is news that applies to only one leg at a time.

That's why Tuesday matters more than Friday does. Tuesday brings Brazil's second-quarter growth number — the consensus expects a slowdown to just four or five tenths of a percent — and, the same day, Europe's flash inflation estimate for August, with July's reading at two point nine percent. Two numbers, each hitting exactly one leg. For once this week, they could add up instead of canceling out. Friday's American jobs report, by contrast, hits both legs at once, and will probably move things less than the buildup around it suggests.

On Warsh himself: his first speech in Jackson Hole as central bank chief turned hold into hike. The odds of a rate increase on September sixteenth jumped from around thirty-five to nearly sixty percent. He said he was impressed by the economy's strength, but concerned that the underlying inflation trends hadn't improved — and once again refused to give the market any guidance on where rates go from here. That makes every single data release more important, because nobody has a map anymore.

The second piece of news comes from the Persian Gulf. On Sunday, American forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz. It was the first military action in about a month. Iran has vowed retaliation. Oil jumped: Brent rose one point nine percent, to eighty-nine dollars and seventy-nine cents.

For you, that's an uncomfortable piece of news, for a reason that's easy to miss. Brazil is a net oil exporter. When oil rises, its terms of trade improve, more dollars flow into the country, and the real tends to strengthen. A stronger real means fewer reais per euro for you. And it just so happens that the bank Itaú builds its pessimistic outlook for the real explicitly on falling oil prices — that pillar just tipped over.

On the forecasts, there's a correction to yesterday. BTG Pactual no longer stands at four ninety — back in July it raised its year-end call to five forty, citing a tougher Federal Reserve. That means no current bank forecast puts the dollar below five reais by year-end anymore. In four months, the published consensus has swung from a stronger real to a weaker one.

So what does that mean for your timing? My twelve-month expectation sits at six point one two, against today's anchor of six point zero one. A full year of waiting is worth, by the math, about five hundred thirty-four reais on five thousand euros. This single month's swing was more than double that. The expected trend, in other words, stays smaller than the noise. That's not a recommendation to buy or to wait — it's an attitude: catch good days within your normal rhythm, rather than holding out for a big jump.
