The 6.00 Test — and Why the Fix Sits Too Low

Two things are certain this morning, and they only appear to contradict each other. First: the first fully post-Warsh fix didn't help you — it cost you 0.26%. Second: the market is already trading above six anyway.

The first half can be broken down exactly, since both legs come from the same ECB query:

Fri, Aug 28 → Mon, Aug 31→ EUR/USD 1.1643 → 1.1596 (−0.40%)→ USD/BRL 5.1641 → 5.1718 (+0.15%)→ EUR/BRL −0.26%

The hawkish dollar pushed the euro down 0.40%, but the real only 0.15%. This time the dollar leg wasn't symmetric — and the difference lands entirely on your side, with the sign flipped. That's the key correction to yesterday: a tougher Fed isn't automatically good for your rate. It's good if the real suffers more than the euro. On Monday, it was the other way around.

Cross-check (and the trap in it): EUR/BRL can be measured two ways — once directly as the published rate, once indirectly as USD/BRL × EUR/USD. If the two diverge by more than half a percent, something's off. Analogy: you weigh yourself on two scales. Only if they're truly two separate scales does agreement mean anything — if both secretly use the same sensor, they'll always show the same reading, even if both are wrong. That's exactly what happens today with the automated check tool: it pulled both comparison legs from the same ECB family and dutifully reported 0.0%. Worthless. With a genuine second scale — the independently measured dollar leg — it's 0.25%.

And that 0.25% points up: the implied rate stands at 6.0123, the fix at 5.9972. On €5,000, that's a R$76 difference. Anyone exchanging through a Brazilian bank this afternoon is more likely working with a six up front than a five. To be honest about it: the two legs carry different timestamps — the dollar side is from today, the euro leg from yesterday. If EUR/USD moves during the day, the implied value shifts along with it.

Three Central Banks, Nine Days

[Global] Between the 10th and the 16th of September, all three central banks that determine your rate meet — and for the first time in weeks, two of them are pulling in the same direction, one that's favorable for you.

DateBankCurrentExpectation

Tue/Wed, Sep 15–16CopomSelic 14.00%Cut to 13.75%, door open
Wed, Sep 16Fed3.50–3.75%Hike, 50–66%, contested

Why the first two rows belong together: an ECB hike makes the euro more expensive — your numerator rises. A Copom cut makes the real cheaper — your denominator falls. Both effects point in the same direction, and unlike with the dollar, they don't cancel each other out. Combined, Brazil's rate advantage over the US shrinks, within a single week, from 10.4 to 9.9 percentage points (my calculation: Selic minus the policy-rate midpoint, before and after).

Carry: the rate advantage a country offers foreign money. Analogy: two savings accounts, one pays 3.6%, one pays 14%. As long as the 14%-country's currency doesn't collapse, the difference is your profit. That exact advantage has supported the real for months. Every Copom cut shrinks it — so does every Fed hike. That's why the combination "Fed up, Copom down" is the single most favorable variant for your exchange.

Where I won't commit to a single number: the Fed. Four dated readings from Aug 31 paint different pictures — Saxo says "above 50%," a CNBC analysis of the same futures contracts says 60.4% (up from ~56% on Friday), a Forbes headline says 66%. On Aug 17 the value still stood at 30%. The prediction market Kalshi has since moved to price it at around 58% — despite the different trading-venue format, that's now close to the CME range.

Why the same "probability" produces different values: it isn't measured, it's backed out of futures-contract prices — at a specific moment, with a specific assumption about the step size. Analogy: three people estimate the height of the same tree, each from a different spot in the yard. All are roughly right, none exactly match. Quoting a single decimal place from that fakes a precision that doesn't exist.

The trigger for the jump from 30 to 50–66 percent was Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech on Aug 28: the Fed chair reaffirmed the 2% target and called PCE inflation at 3.7% concerning — the better summer readings, in his view, showed no genuine trend reversal. The most prominent counterargument comes from Goldman Sachs (Jan Hatzius, Aug 17): "We still think market pricing for the funds rate is too hawkish." Important for context: that note is eleven days older than Warsh's speech. I'm citing it as a counterposition, not as a current response to it.

Hormuz — and the Crack in Itaú's Thesis

[Global] Over the weekend (Aug 29/30), US forces struck Iranian rocket launchers near the Strait of Hormuz that were reportedly meant to lay sea mines in the strait; Iran responded with attacks on US bases in the region. Between 6 and 8 million barrels of crude oil pass through the strait daily. Brent trades today at $91.48 (+1.09%).

Terms of trade: the price ratio between what a country sells and what it buys. Analogy: if your neighbor pays you more for your apples while bread stays the same price, you're better off — without working any harder. Brazil sells oil, soy, and iron ore. When oil rises, more dollars flow into the country and the real tends to strengthen.

Oil is working in two directions at once here, and that's the interesting part. Real-friendly via Brazil's trade balance — bad for your rate. Euro-friendly via euro inflation, because expensive energy keeps the ECB on its hiking path — good for your rate. Lagarde herself flagged exactly this upside risk on Jul 23. My read: in the short term, the inflation channel wins, because a central-bank meeting in nine days is an appointment, while terms-of-trade effects take quarters. Over a six-month horizon, that relationship flips.

That's costly for a forecast. On Jun 26, Itaú raised its dollar-real call from 5.15 to 5.30 by year-end, basing that explicitly on higher US rates, election risk, and worsening terms of trade from falling oil prices. The first pillar has since gotten stronger, the third has flipped. So I'm taking Itaú's number for what it is: a June forecast with a premise that no longer holds in August.

It's Tuesday, September first. The European Central Bank's latest reference rate is five point nine nine seven two reais per euro — an eighth of a cent shy of a flat six. On five thousand euros, that's twenty-nine thousand nine hundred eighty-six reais.

More interesting than that level is the calendar. Between the tenth and the sixteenth of September, all three central banks that drive your rate hold meetings. The European Central Bank goes first, on the tenth, and the futures market prices a rate hike at ninety-eight percent — that's not a bet anymore, it's an appointment. Brazil's Copom meets on the fifteenth and sixteenth, and its August minutes leave the door open for a cut. The Federal Reserve also meets on the sixteenth, and that one's a genuine toss-up.

The first two matter most, and here's why. A European hike makes the euro pricier — that's the top of your rate going up. A Brazilian cut makes the real cheaper — that's the bottom going down. Both push your number the same way, and unlike a dollar move, they don't cancel out. Put together, Brazil's interest-rate edge over the United States shrinks, in a single week, from ten point four points to nine point nine.

Overnight, something else happened that's worth knowing. Yesterday's official rate was the first to fully price in Fed chair Kevin Warsh's hawkish turn — and it actually fell, not rose, by a quarter of one percent. The reason: the stronger dollar knocked the euro down four tenths of a percent, but only clipped the real by one and a half tenths. This time the dollar didn't move symmetrically, and that gap landed in your favor with the sign reversed. A tougher Fed, in other words, isn't automatically good news for you.

The market itself is already trading above six this morning regardless. An independent dollar reading has the greenback at five point one eight four eight; cross that against yesterday's euro rate and you get six point zero one two three — a quarter of a percent above the official fix, worth about seventy-six reais on five thousand euros. Anyone changing money at a Brazilian bank this afternoon will probably see a six up front, not a five.

One word of caution here. An automated check I ran today came back reporting zero deviation between two supposedly independent numbers — and that reading is worthless, because both numbers came from the same European Central Bank data feed. Picture weighing yourself on two bathroom scales that secretly share the same sensor: they'll always agree, even when they're both wrong. Bring in a real second scale — an independently sourced dollar quote — and the gap reappears at a quarter of one percent.

Two numbers are due out today, and I don't have either yet. Around eleven in the morning Central European time comes Europe's flash inflation estimate for August, expected to jump to three point three percent from two point nine in July. That would be a big move, and the oil price is the most likely explanation. If it lands as expected, September's European rate hike is all but locked in. Later in the day, Brazil releases second-quarter growth, expected between three and five tenths of a percent after a strong one point one in the first quarter. I'm not printing numbers that don't exist yet.

Oil itself is cutting two ways at once. After American strikes on Iranian rocket launchers near the Strait of Hormuz, Brent crude sits at ninety-one dollars and forty-eight cents. For Brazil, an oil exporter, that's good news — more dollars flow in, the real strengthens, and your rate drops. For Europe, expensive energy means more inflation pressure and a more determined central bank — and that pushes your rate up. In my read, the inflation channel wins in the short run, because a central-bank meeting nine days out is a fixed date, while trade-balance effects take quarters to show up.

A word of warning on forecasts. Not a single bank I cite publishes a forecast for the euro against the real — they all forecast the dollar against the real instead. But half of what you pay depends on the euro leg, and that's where the spread is even wider. Run the same dollar forecast through a weak euro and a strong one, and the gap is nine percent — bigger than the difference between the most extreme dollar forecasts themselves. The uncertainty around the euro is bigger than the uncertainty around the real, and that almost never gets said out loud.

So what does this mean for your timing? My twelve-month expectation is six point one five; today's anchor is six even. By the math, a full year of waiting is worth about seven hundred sixty-four reais on five thousand euros. This single month of August swung by more than that on its own. The expected trend, in short, is smaller than the noise. Six even is a solid rate, not a spectacular one — about three hundred fifty reais below August's high. Tomorrow brings the market's reaction to today's numbers, plus the Bank of Canada's rate decision.
