The 0.8% Gap — and Who Pays for It

This brief checks the same thing every morning: the published EUR/BRL rate should roughly match USD/BRL times EUR/USD. If the two diverge by more than half a percent, it gets flagged. Today it's flagged — for the first time in weeks.

Cross-check: EUR/BRL can be measured two ways. Once directly, as the published rate. Once indirectly, via the dollar detour. Analogy: you weigh yourself on the kitchen scale and on the bathroom scale. If both agree, it's probably right. If they disagree, one is broken — or you changed clothes in between. Today, the market changed clothes in between.

The three readings:

ReadingTimeEUR/BRL
ECB fixTue, Sep 1, 09:15 BRT6.0255
Market closeTue, Sep 1, session end5.9766
Cross rate (independent)Wed, Sep 2, early5.9752

Two independent readings say the same thing, one says something else. That's not a data glitch — it's a time of day. And it can be broken down exactly, because the dollar side at fix time and at the close is documented from different sources:

IBGE GDP 09:00 BRT: beat→ ECB fix 09:15 BRT: USD/BRL 5.1989→ Real appreciates, Ibovespa +1.30%→ B3 close: 5.1557 (−0.83% vs. fix)

The ECB fix is set a quarter hour after the IBGE release — effectively before the market's reaction to it. After that, the Brazilian session ran for another six hours, and it ran the whole way in the real's favor: stocks up 1.30%, the rate curve down, the currency firmer. The fix saw none of it.

What this means in practice. If you read "EUR/BRL 6.03" somewhere today, that's not your rate — yours is closer to 5.975, a difference of R$244 on €5,000. The direction is new: yesterday the market sat above the fix, so the fix was hiding a good rate from you. Today it's flattering you with a fake one. And for today's fix, that implies: absent fresh news, it has to catch down at 09:15 BRT, not the market catch up.

3.3% — Almost All Energy, and My Objection

[EU] Eurostat published the flash estimate yesterday: 3.3% annual inflation in the euro area for August, up from 2.9% in July. Consensus landed exactly there. That makes the ECB hike on Sep 10 practically a done deal.

The breakdown is still worth a look, because it puts the headline in perspective:

ComponentAugJul
Energy14.3%10.3%
Services3.0%3.3%
Industrial goods1.2%0.9%
Food/tobacco1.2%1.2%

Only one line actually jumped — and services actually fell. That's an economically meaningful distinction.

Why central banks normally look past energy prices: a rate hike works through demand — it makes credit expensive so people buy less. Analogy: if the oil tap in the Middle East gets turned off, gasoline gets pricier no matter how high your savings-account rate is. A rate can't conjure up supply. That's why central banks normally look through energy shocks and focus on services instead — that's the part driven by domestic wages and demand. And that exact part is cooling off right now.

This is where I disagree with the consensus — carefully, but explicitly. The ECB hiked in June for the first time in three years, explicitly because of the Iran war, and is set to hike again on Sep 10 per 57 of the 69 economists surveyed. So it isn't looking through the oil shock, it's reacting to it. That's defensible — second-round effects, expectation anchors — but it makes the hike less a signal of a genuine rate cycle and more an insurance move. The risk for your euro leg: the ECB hikes on Sep 10 and simultaneously signals that's it for the year, because core inflation is falling. Then the hike is priced in, the follow-through isn't — and the euro gives back ground after the meeting instead of running further.

Correction to yesterday This brief cited a market probability of ~98% for the September hike on Sep 1. That couldn't be re-substantiated with a date today. The only dated market-pricing figure I have is ~80% for September (plus ~40% for another by December) — as of Aug 13, i.e. before the flash estimate. The 3.3% print has probably pushed that figure up since, but I won't print a number I can't back up. Today's solid anchor is the economist consensus of 57 to 69.

The full August figure comes out on Thu, Sep 17 — a week after the decision. So the ECB is deciding, as almost always, on the extrapolation.

A GDP Print That Points Both Ways

[BR] The IBGE reported +0.5% quarter over quarter yesterday — above the Broadcast median estimate of 0.4% (range 0.2–0.7%) — and +2.0% year over year. The market read it as good news: Ibovespa +1.30% to 179,722 points, the real firmer.

The composition tells a different story:

  • Agriculture +2.8% — by far the largest contribution. A harvest is a one-quarter event, not a trend.
  • Services +0.2% — practically flat in the economy's largest sector.
  • Private consumption −0.4% — shrank. After five quarters with a Selic at 14% or above, that's the expected but still rough figure.

For you, the logic is inverted, and here it points clearly upward. A beat that comes from the ground while the consumer gives up is exactly the pattern a central bank uses to justify a cut: demand is no longer generating inflation pressure. The bond market read it that way immediately — DI Jan/27 at 13.585% (−3 basis points), i.e. below the current Selic of 14.00%, and Brazil's ten-year yield fell to 14.63% (−0.09 points).

DI contract (Depósito Interfinanceiro): Brazil's futures market for bank interest rates. The January 2027 contract tells you what average rate the market expects by then. Analogy: a betting market for the question "where does the policy rate stand in January?" If the contract sits below today's policy rate, the market is betting on cuts. That's exactly the case here.

Where I'm cautious: sources are split on whether the Copom cuts as soon as Sep 15/16 or waits until November/December. The Focus median of 13.75% by year-end only tells you a cut is coming, not which meeting. Yesterday this brief read it as "door open for September"; today I find just as many voices for a hold. I'm leaving both standing — the difference decides the timing, not the direction. Aside: Focus sees 2026 growth at 1.92%; after +1.1% and +0.5% across the first half, that looks too low.

Kharg Island — the Oil Leg Turns More Dangerous

[Global] Brent stood at roughly $91–92 on Sep 1 and trades at about $95 on Sep 2, with a daily range of 91.75 to 96.99. I won't print a precise day-over-day change — sources disagree, and false precision here would be worse than the honest range. Over the month, that's +8.96%; over the year, +32%.

What's changed qualitatively matters more than the number. US forces struck Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island, Iran responded with attacks on the Emirates and Jordan — and Trump has extended his threats to Kharg Island, Iran's main oil-export terminal. That's a different category: until now this was about the shipping route through the Strait of Hormuz (roughly a fifth of the world's oil before the conflict), now it's about the loading facility itself.

Terms of trade: the price ratio between what a country sells and what it buys. Analogy: if your neighbor suddenly pays you more for your apples while bread stays the same price, you're better off — without working any harder. Brazil sells oil, soy, and iron ore. When oil rises, more dollars flow into the country, and the real tends to strengthen.

Oil works on your rate in both directions at once, and that's why I don't treat it as a simple driver:

Lifts EUR/BRL — good for you: expensive energy is the sole reason euro inflation sits at 3.3%. Without the oil shock, the Sep 10 hike probably wouldn't happen.
Pushes EUR/BRL down — bad for you: Brazil's trade balance improves, dollars flow into the country, the real appreciates. That's exactly what won out yesterday against the rising dollar index.

My read, unchanged from yesterday: in the short term, the inflation channel wins, because a central-bank meeting in eight days is a fixed date, while trade-balance effects take quarters. Over a six-month horizon, that flips. What's new is the asymmetry: a strike on Kharg would be a supply shock with an immediate price reaction — and it would hit the inflation channel first, i.e. your euro leg, with Brazil's export earnings only catching up with a lag.
