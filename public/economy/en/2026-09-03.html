Three Percent on a One-Point Difference

[BR] Pollster Quaest published a runoff scenario yesterday: Lula 42%, Flávio Bolsonaro 41%, margin of error ±2 points. The market read a signal into that and traded it at full force.

Quaest: runoff tied→ Expected fiscal premium falls→ Foreign equity buying flow, EWZ +4.16%→ DI curve falls across every maturity→ Real +0.91%, EUR/BRL market to 5.90

In numbers: Ibovespa 185,205.09 points (+3.05%, the largest one-day gain since March, an eleventh straight positive close, intraday range 179,733 to 186,028), the Brazil ETF EWZ at $38.09 (+4.16%, $8.31B volume, one-year total return +34.91%), dollar-real at R$5.1065. Money Times cites the reasoning matter-of-factly: "a number of investors see a possible change of government as a potential signal for more orthodox economic policy."

Here I disagree with the tape — emphatically. A poll where two candidates sit one point apart and the margin of error is two points formally says: we cannot distinguish between them. That's not a signal about the election outcome, it's a signal about the limits of measurement.

Margin of error: A poll surveys a few thousand people instead of 156 million — the result is therefore always an estimate with some fuzziness. Analogy: you measure two kids with a yardstick that's accurate to within two centimeters, and one comes out one centimeter taller. You still don't know who's actually taller. The market traded yesterday as if it knew.

The second caveat comes from the capital flows themselves. The tape tells a story of foreign buying — the central bank's statistics tell a different story for the same window: Brazil's total FX flow stood at −$3.095B through Aug 28. That's a different time window than the rally, so not a contradiction in the strict sense — but it means this week's equity inflows are sitting on top of a negative overall balance. A move built on a poll within the margin of error and on a flow that was still pointing downward barely a week ago isn't one you should delay an exchange against.

In practical terms: this move works against you. The real is stronger, your euro buys less. The good news is it can reverse just as fast — the next poll is the same coin flip.

DI curve: Brazil's futures market for bank interest rates. Each contract tells you what average rate the market expects by a given month. Analogy: a betting market for the question "where will the policy rate stand by then?" The gap to today's policy rate lets you back out the implied probability of a cut. Currently: Jan/27 at 13.575% — clearly below the Selic of 14.00%.

Still, I'm not buying the 100%. Money Times names yesterday's price-move drivers explicitly: the election poll and a pullback in US Treasuries. Neither is a monetary-policy argument. A curve that prices in nearly a full cut on the back of a poll within the margin of error has mostly unwound its risk premium — and that can come right back on the next poll. My read: ~75–80% for the September move, not 100%.

One detail the curve itself gives away: Jan/29 sits at 13.950%, Jan/36 at 14.320% — both above Jan/27. So the market is pricing a short, shallow cutting cycle that then turns again. In other words: nobody here believes in a genuine rate-cycle reversal, just a breather.

It's Thursday, September third. Yesterday, a single point in an opinion poll triggered the biggest one-day loss in your exchange rate in three months. The European Central Bank's reference rate fell one point oh three percent, to five point nine six three five reais per euro.

The trigger was a poll from the Quaest institute on a potential runoff: Lula at forty-two percent, Flávio Bolsonaro at forty-one. The margin of error is two points. Formally, that means the two are statistically indistinguishable — it's not a signal about who wins, it's a signal about the limits of the measurement itself. The market traded it as a signal anyway. Brazilian stocks jumped three point oh five percent, the biggest one-day gain since March and an eleventh straight positive close. The dollar fell to five point one oh six five reais, its lowest close since August seventh.

Here's where I push back on the market, hard. Picture measuring two kids with a yardstick that's only accurate to within two centimeters, and one comes out a centimeter taller. You still don't actually know who's taller. That's the whole basis for yesterday's move. And Brazil's own central-bank data tells a different story for the same stretch: the country's total foreign-exchange flow through August twenty-eighth was negative three point oh nine five billion dollars. So this week's stock rally is sitting on top of a flow that, until very recently, was still running the other way.

And even the lowered official rate is still too generous. Three independent readings from the very same trading day range between five point eight nine nine seven and five point nine one two three — a gap of zero point eight seven to one point oh eight percent below the official fix, the second day running past this brief's half-percent alert line, and a wider gap than yesterday's. On five thousand euros, that difference comes to two hundred ninety-four reais.

The reason has become structural, not random. The reference rate gets set at a quarter past nine, Brazil time — about fifteen minutes after trading opens in São Paulo.
