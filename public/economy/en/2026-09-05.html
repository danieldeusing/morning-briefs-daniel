I Had the Wrong Explanation — the Right One Is Less Comfortable

The Sep 3 brief made two forecasts and hit both: Thursday's fix was called to land at 5.86–5.95, most likely 5.90 — it came in at 5.8935. Friday's fix was called to land at 5.84–6.00, most likely 5.93 — it came in at 5.9405. Two hits, both point estimates off by under 0.2%.

And yet the reasoning was wrong. I had claimed the fix "effectively measures the prior day's close." Let's check the math: the Sep 2 market close corresponded to a EUR/BRL of roughly 5.9123, and Sep 3's to roughly 5.9289. The Sep 3 fix came in at 5.8935 — below both. A model that assumes a one-session lag would not have produced that value.

Datafolha 46:44 the night before→ Ibovespa runs to +1.99% (188,891)→ Fix hits the real's high at 09:15 BRT→ Close: Ibovespa −0.01%, USD/BRL 5.1045→ Fix 0.60% "too real-strong"

The correct explanation is simpler and more uncomfortable. The ECB reference rate is not an average and not a closing price. It's a single measurement point, taken at 14:15 CEST ≙ 09:15 BRT — a good quarter hour after trading opens in São Paulo. On Thursday, that one point landed exactly at the intraday high of the election trade: Ibovespa was on its way to 188,891 points (+1.99%) and then closed at 185,188 (−0.01%). The fix photographed the day's most euphoric moment. The Wednesday before, the same mechanism had worked in the opposite direction.

Reference rate (fixing): An official rate the ECB sets once a day at a fixed time, so contracts, balance sheets, and statistics across Europe all use the same value. Analogy: a photo booth that snaps a picture every day at a quarter past nine — whether you happen to be laughing, sneezing, or looking away. The picture is real, but it's not "you on that day," it's "you in that one second." On a day when the market swings 2% intraday, every fix contains roughly one percent of pure timing luck.

What you should take from this is an attitude, not a formula. A single fix isn't a price, it's a sample. The series is reliable because it averages the timing luck out over many days — the individual daily value is not. Anyone who times their exchange to a one-day move in the fix is, to a good extent, betting on whatever happened to be on the screen at 09:15.

A Jobs Report Above Every Forecast

[USA] On Friday at 08:30 ET, the US Labor Department reported +162,000 new jobs for August. The Reuters consensus was +56,000, the Dow Jones consensus +53,000, and the range of individual estimates ran from −25,000 to +121,000. The actual figure therefore came in above the most optimistic forecast in the entire panel — that's rare, and says more about the state of the model than about the labor market.

The second blow is in the revisions. June was raised from +20,000 to +31,000, July from −23,000 to +21,000 — a combined +55,000. That means the July slump, which had flipped the Fed bet in early August, has retroactively disappeared. It never happened.

Revision: the first figure for any given month is a projection from incomplete reports; over the following two months, the latecomers get added in and the number is corrected. Analogy: the first exit-poll projection on election night versus the official final result three days later. Anyone who bet on the first number finds out two months later whether they were betting on the right event.

Beneath the surface, the report is less triumphant: leisure and hospitality (+59,000) and local government education (+42,000) carry two-thirds of the gain, while the information sector lost 23,000 jobs. Hourly wages rose 0.3% to $37.75, up 3.1% year over year. The unemployment rate held at 4.1%.

What matters for you is the chain, not the report itself. The hike bet for Sep 16 jumped from 49.4% (Thursday) to 53–58%, per CME FedWatch. The dollar index turned up to 99.1555 (+0.25%), USD/BRL rose 0.50%. That's the channel through which Washington reaches your rate: not directly, but through the price of the dollar against everything else.

Why a range and not a number FedWatch isn't a daily close, it's a live derivation from the Fed funds future — the value keeps updating throughout the entire session. At 09:05 ET, 35 minutes after the report, IBTimes read 52.6% (implied no-hike probability 47.4%); later in the day, the CNBC live blog read 58%. Both start from the same prior-day figure of 49.4%, so they don't contradict each other — they're two points in time on the same day. That's why I print the range. A single point value here would be a precision the instrument simply doesn't have. For you, this changes nothing about the direction: a hike bet flipping from under half to over half is the dollar-strengthening impulse — whether it lands at 53 or 58.

The Curve Falls Against the Payrolls Print

[BR] On Friday, the US two-year Treasury yield reportedly rose to its highest level since January 2025. Normally that pulls the Brazilian rate curve up with it. It fell anyway: DI Jan/36 to 14.200% (−8.5bp), Jan/29 to 13.840% (−4.3bp), Jan/27 practically unchanged at 13.575%. Brazil's 10-year yield moved to 14.35% (−0.06 points), so −13bp across two sessions — two sources: TradingEconomics 14.35%, Investing.com 14.352%, both as of Sep 4.

The spread between the long and short end is the number that actually matters. Jan/36 minus Jan/27 stood at 74.5 basis points on Sep 2 and at 62.5 on Sep 4. Twelve basis points of compression in two sessions, while the US moved the opposite way.

Evidentiary basis for these numbers The individual DI contract closes and their day-over-day changes are available to me from only one report (Money Times, Sep 4) — B3 and Reuters Brasil don't provide a retrievable closing file from here. But the curve's level is independently supported: the ANBIMA closing curve (ETTJ PRE) for Sep 4, 2026 shows 3 years at 14.0413% and 10 years at 14.4051%; Investing.com for the same day shows 2 years at 13.850%, 3 years at 14.043%, 10 years at 14.352%. That lines up with Jan/29 at 13.840% and a long end around 14.2–14.4%. What stays single-sourced are the deltas (−8.5bp / −4.3bp), and with them the exact compression figure of 74.5 → 62.5bp; the direction — long end down, short end flat — is carried by all three curves.
Long end / term premium: the extra yield investors demand for locking up their money for ten years instead of one. Analogy: a two-year phone contract costs a different price than a month-to-month plan — the difference is the price of uncertainty. When it falls in Brazil, that means the market sees the distant future as less risky. And here, the distant future means fiscal policy, not the policy rate.

Traders' comments support exactly that. André Shiokawa (Asset1): "the DI curve is mainly trading two vectors: the economic slowdown" — and Felipe Garcia (C6 Bank) names the second openly: "opposition candidate Flávio Bolsonaro is perceived as somewhat more fiscally minded." That's the election trade, expressed in rates instead of stocks, and it's strong enough to override a US impulse.

My caveat remains the same as on Sep 3: what's falling here is risk premium, not monetary policy. A premium that unwinds on a poll comes back on the next poll. The rate gap to the ECB stands at 11.75 points today; if the ECB hikes on Sep 10 and the Copom cuts on Sep 16, it becomes 11.25.

The Busiest Week of the Quarter — and Its Timing

Four events, and for three of them, the clock decides which fix actually captures them. The ECB reference rate is set at 12:15 UTC. Anything that comes after that only affects the following day's fix.

EventTime (UTC)Which fix catches it?
Mon, Sep 7 — B3 closedall dayMon fix, no BRL liquidity
Thu, Sep 10 — ECB decision12:15Thu fix: no (same minute) → Fri, Sep 11
Thu, Sep 10 — US PPI12:30Fri, Sep 11
Fri, Sep 11 — US CPI12:30Fri fix: no (15 min. too late) → Mon, Sep 14

This isn't hairsplitting, it's the week's key timing information. The ECB decision lands in the exact same minute as the fix — the press conference where Lagarde delivers the actual news doesn't start until afterward. So Thursday's fix contains none of it. And the US consumer price index on Friday misses the Friday fix by 15 minutes, just like the payrolls report last week. The two fixings where the week actually lands are Fri, Sep 11 and Mon, Sep 14 — the latter one day before Copom and the FOMC.

What's actually up for debate on Sep 10 isn't the move itself — 65 economists in the Reuters poll expect +25bp to 2.50%, which is fully priced. What's up for debate is what comes after. Danske Bank writes on Sep 4: a hike "in line with consensus and market pricing," but then a hold at 2.50% through 2026 and 2027; market pricing near 3.00% by 2027 is "excessive." Lagarde will give no forward guidance, in order to retain "full flexibility."

Forward guidance: when a central bank doesn't just say what it's doing today, but signals what it plans to do next. Analogy: the difference between "I'm paying today" and "I'm paying today and the next three times too." The second is more valuable — and riskier if the situation changes. Giving no guidance means: the central bank doesn't want to commit, and the market has to guess for itself.

Why this affects your euro leg: one reason the euro sits at 1.1622 against the dollar today is that the market is pricing in further ECB moves. If Danske is right, the euro is priced richer than justified over a three-to-twelve-month horizon — and every EUR/BRL forecast that builds on today's euro leg would be too high. That's the sharpest named dissent in the field, and the counterweight to my own upward lean above.

It's Saturday, September fifth. This brief is catching up on two trading days, since there was no edition on Friday. Over those two sessions, your exchange rate first fell one point one seven percent, then bounced back zero point eight percent. The European Central Bank's reference rate now stands at five point nine four zero five reais per euro, and it holds until Monday.

I need to start with a correction. In the September third brief, I wrote that the reference rate lags the market by one session. That's wrong. Both of that day's forecasts still came true — Thursday was expected at five point nine oh and came in at five point eight nine three five; Friday was expected at five point nine three and came in at five point nine four oh five. But the reasoning behind those calls was wrong, and that matters more than the hits themselves.

Here's the real explanation, and it's less comfortable. The reference rate isn't an average and it isn't a closing price — it's a single snapshot, taken at a quarter past nine, Brazil time. On Thursday, that snapshot landed exactly at the peak of the election rally: Brazilian stocks were running toward a gain of nearly two percent and then closed the day essentially flat, down zero point oh one percent. The fix photographed the most euphoric moment of the day. Picture a photo booth that snaps a picture every day at the same quarter-past-nine moment, whether you're laughing or sneezing or looking the other way. The picture is real, but it isn't you on that day — it's you in that one second. On a day when the market swings two percent from open to close, every fix carries about one percent of pure timing luck.

The lesson is an attitude, not a formula. The series of fixes is reliable because it averages that timing luck out over many days. A single day's reading is not. If you time your exchange around a one-day move in the fix, you're largely betting on whatever happened to be on the screen at a quarter past nine.

The second piece of good news: the gap between the official rate and the real market has closed. For two days it sat above this brief's half-percent alert line, with the official rate running above the market. Now it sits zero point two to zero point four percent below it. On five thousand euros, that's about a hundred six reais — and this time it works in your favor, not against you. Two days ago, the gap ran the other way, to the tune of two hundred ninety-four reais.

Out of the United States, Friday brought a jobs report that beat every single forecast on the board. Employers added a hundred sixty-two thousand positions in August, against a consensus of fifty-six thousand and a top individual estimate of just a hundred twenty-one thousand. On top of that, June and July were revised up by a combined fifty-five thousand jobs — erasing July's earlier slump entirely; on the books now, it never happened. The odds of a Federal Reserve rate hike on September sixteenth jumped from forty-nine point four percent the day before to somewhere between fifty-three and fifty-eight percent. I'm giving you a range on purpose: this tracker keeps recalculating all day long, and depending on when you checked, it read fifty-two point six percent or fifty-eight percent. What matters isn't the exact number, it's that the bet flipped from under half to over half. The dollar turned higher, and the real gave back about half a percent.

That's the same election trade, expressed in interest rates instead of stock prices.

Next week is the busiest of the quarter, and the timing is unkind. Monday, Brazil's stock exchange is closed for Independence Day — there will still be a fix, just no Brazilian market behind it to move it. Thursday, the European Central Bank decides, in the exact same minute the fix gets set — the reaction won't show up until Friday. And Friday's US consumer-price report misses Friday's fix by fifteen minutes, exactly like last week's jobs report. The two days the week actually lands on are Friday the eleventh and Monday the fourteenth.

As for what's actually being debated on Thursday, it isn't the rate move itself — sixty-five economists expect a quarter-point hike to two point five percent, and that's fully priced in. What's in question is what comes after. Danske Bank argues the central bank will then hold steady through both twenty twenty-six and twenty twenty-seven, and that the market's pricing of roughly three percent by twenty twenty-seven is excessive. If that's right, the euro is priced richer today than it should be, and every forecast for your currency pair that's built on today's euro rate would be too high.
