Five-Sixths Came From Brazil

Why breaking down the rate isn't hairsplitting

The two legs: There's no direct world market for euro against real. Your rate is built through the dollar: first euro → dollar, then dollar → real. Like a trip with a connection — delay can happen on either leg, and it matters which one. That's why it's worth checking daily whether EUR/BRL ≈ USD/BRL × EUR/USD holds. Friday: published 5.9405, computed 5.9405.

Over the week, EUR/BRL lost 1.20% (6.0126 → 5.9405). Broken down:

EUR/USD −0.18%+ USD/BRL −1.02%= EUR/BRL −1.20%

The euro lost almost nothing against the dollar. The real gained substantially — in a week when the US jobs report came in three times stronger than expected and the dollar index rose. A real that gains 1% against a strengthening dollar is doing that under its own power: Ibovespa +5.40% (the year's second-best week), Brent $96.28 after +21.2% for the month, the Selic at 14.00%.

Why that matters: the two legs run on different schedules. The euro leg has a Thursday appointment with a known date and a largely known outcome; the real leg has an election in four weeks with an open outcome. Anyone who files the week under "the euro was weak" is planning against the wrong risk. And it wasn't a calm week: intraweek, the rate ran from 6.0255 (Sep 1) to 5.8935 (Sep 3, −2.19%) and back to 5.9405 — a round trip of over two percent in three sessions, roughly R$600 on €5,000.

Monday Is a Dead Day for You

Independence Day — and what that means in practice

Monday, Sep 7, is Independence Day. The B3 doesn't trade (not stocks, not FIIs, ETFs, BDRs, derivatives, or private fixed-income), the banks are closed, and Tesouro Direto isn't taking orders — anyone who clicks on Monday gets filled Tuesday at Tuesday's prices. Pix keeps running, but it only moves reais that are already inside Brazil. A currency exchange needs a bank. So: not executable.

Will there even be a rate? Very likely, yes. Frankfurt trades as usual, and at the last multi-day Brazilian market holiday, the ECB kept fixing throughout: Carnival Monday, Feb 16, 2026, at 6.1935, Carnival Tuesday, Feb 17, at 6.1788, against 6.1916 the Friday before. That's +0.03% and −0.24% — both smaller than an average normal trading day.

Rule of thumb A holiday fix is a price with no onshore flow behind it, set offshore in a thin book. A placeholder, not a signal — don't base a decision on it.

Second effect: the central bank's Focus survey normally comes out on Mondays. This week Monday's a holiday, so the release is expected on Tuesday — that's my inference from the holiday calendar, not a retrieved BCB schedule announcement. The reliable reading remains the one from Aug 31.

The Rate Gap Is Shrinking From Both Sides

The mechanism behind the 30-day tier

Carry trade: Borrow money where rates are low, and invest it where they're high — the difference is the profit. Like a kid who buys candy at the cheap store and resells it for more on the school bus: the margin carries the trade, as long as the price doesn't collapse along the way. For Brazil, that margin is the gap between the Selic and the US policy rate. When it shrinks, holding reais pays off less — and the real gives ground.

That's exactly what's set up for Sep 16. The Fed's target range sits at 3.50–3.75%, last confirmed by a 9-to-3 vote on Jul 29 — Chair Kevin Warsh called it a "good family fight." The market now prices in around 66% odds of a hike to 3.75–4.00%, up from roughly 36% just days earlier. In the same 48-hour window, the Copom meets, and per Focus it's still headed toward 13.75%.

Fed +25bp→ Copom −25bp→ Gap 10.4 → 9.9pp→ Carry thinner→ BRL weaker→ EUR/BRL higher

Half a percentage point less of an edge sounds small, but it's the first two-sided compression of this cycle — until now, only the Copom was cutting. Nicolas Gomes (Manchester Investimentos) names exactly this as September's main driver. It shouldn't be overstated: 9.9 points remains historically enormous, and Goldman rightly points to that cushion.

Thursday Is About Tone, Not a Rate Decision

ECB, Sep 10 — why I push back on the naive reading here

Starting point: the deposit rate has stood at 2.25% since Jun 11 (main refinancing 2.40%, marginal lending 2.65%) — that was the first hike since 2023. On Thursday, 98.9% odds sit on 2.50%. The trigger was the August flash estimate: eurozone inflation at 3.3%, up from 2.9% in July, the highest since September 2024, driven by energy at 14.3%, up from 10.3%.

The naive chain runs: ECB hikes → euro stronger → EUR/BRL rises → good for you. I think that's wrong. First, the move is paid for: at 98.9% odds, the decision itself moves nothing more — it's too well known for that. What moves markets is the deviation from expectations — and that can only come from the 09:45 BRT press conference.

Second, the substance argues for restraint. Core inflation — prices excluding energy and food, meaning the part a central bank can actually reach with interest rates — has fallen to 2.4%. And the ECB attributes roughly 90% of the January-to-May energy-inflation surge to a supply shock, not demand. No interest rate helps against a supply shock: more expensive money doesn't open a pipeline. The textbook response is to look through it — and the ECB explicitly calls its response this time gradual, unlike the forced 2021/22 cycle.

My position For you, Thursday is more risk than opportunity. A fully priced-in move plus a tone with room to disappoint means: the euro leg can barely gain and can easily lose. Anyone who needs to exchange next week has better odds before Thursday than after.

Positioning — and What Wasn't Verifiable Today

Brazilian bonds, flows, and the honest gaps

Real yield (NTN-B): A Brazilian government bond that strips out inflation — you're guaranteed IPCA plus X percent, no matter how high inflation jumps. Like a savings account that grows along whenever bread gets more expensive. The X is the real yield: the actual value gain the market demands from the government.

In mid-August, Tesouro IPCA+ paid 7% to over 8% real, depending on maturity — a band reached in less than 10% of the historical series; above 7.5% on the 5-to-10-year premium has only happened before in 2015/16 and 2008. As of Aug 17, three weeks old, no daily figure. In the same period, roughly R$257B was paid out from maturing 2026 NTN-Bs, with no automatic reinvestment — a large pool of domestic money up for reallocation right in the middle of the election window.

On foreign flows, only a qualitative read, because the figures couldn't be dated cleanly: August brought record outflows on the tracked series, and the Ibovespa rally happened anyway — so it wasn't carried by returning foreign investors. Forbes calls it an anticipation rally on a fiscal pivot, with "high volatility ahead until there's more clarity."

It's Sunday, September sixth. Markets are closed, there's no rate today, and the last valid reading is Friday's fix from the European Central Bank: five point nine four zero five reais to the euro. That quiet gives me a chance today to take the week apart instead of just reporting it.

Over the week, your rate fell one point two percent. That's bad news for you, since you're selling euros and buying reais. The obvious story is that the euro was weak. That story is wrong. Split the rate into its two building blocks — euro against dollar, and dollar against real — and one point oh two percentage points came from the real, only zero point one eight from the euro. Five-sixths of the move is Brazilian strength, not European weakness.

And that's notable, because the real pulled off that strength against a dollar that was itself getting stronger. Friday's US jobs report came in at a hundred sixty-two thousand new positions, nearly three times what was expected. The real won anyway. Behind that: Brazilian stocks just had their second-best week of the year, oil is sitting near ninety-six dollars after jumping twenty-one percent in a month, and Brazil's benchmark interest rate is fourteen percent.

For the week ahead, a practical wrinkle comes first: Monday is Independence Day in Brazil. The stock exchange is closed, the banks are closed, and Tesouro Direto isn't taking orders. An exchange simply can't be executed on Monday. There will still be a rate — at the last multi-day Brazilian holiday, Carnival back in February, the European Central Bank kept fixing the whole time, and the moves were three hundredths of a percent and minus twenty-four hundredths. A holiday fix is a price with no flow behind it. Treat it as a placeholder, not a signal.

The real event is Thursday. The European Central Bank decides, and the market is pricing in nearly ninety-nine percent odds of a hike in the deposit rate to two and a half percent. The obvious chain would be: central bank hikes, euro gets stronger, your rate goes up. I think that's wrong. When a move is ninety-nine percent priced in, it doesn't move anything anymore — only the tone afterward can do that. And the tone has room to disappoint: core inflation has actually fallen to two point four percent, and the central bank attributes ninety percent of the recent energy-price jump to a supply shock. No interest rate fixes a supply shock. My read: Thursday is more risk than opportunity for you.

The month after that looks different. On September fifteenth and sixteenth, Brazil's central bank and the Federal Reserve both meet within forty-eight hours of each other. The Fed is priced at around sixty-six percent for a hike, not a cut, while Brazil is expected to keep cutting. Brazil's rate advantage is shrinking from both sides at once for the first time this cycle — from ten point four down to roughly nine point nine percentage points. A smaller advantage means less reason to hold reais. And then there's the election: four weeks to the first round, with one poll putting the runoff at forty-six to forty-four, within a two-point margin of error. A coin flip.

That's why my forecast runs in five separate tiers. The next fix, on Monday, should land between five point nine one and five point nine eight, most likely five point nine four. This week, five point eight four to six point zero zero, tilted lower. This month, five point eight five to six point two zero, tilted higher, most likely six point zero two. Three to six months out, five point seven five to six point three five, clustered around the election. And twelve months out, unchanged at five point six five to six point six zero, most likely six point one five. Every one of those probabilities is my own judgment, not a market price.

A word on the analysts: not a single bank forecasts your actual currency pair. They all work in dollars against reais. Convert their numbers using Friday's euro rate, and the range runs from five point eight at Goldman Sachs and XP to six point three at BTG Pactual and Nomad, with a Morgan Stanley tail case near seven. On five thousand euros, that's a spread of well over two thousand three hundred reais. That's not a consensus. That's an open question.

I'll also tell you what's missing today: Brazil's five-year credit-default-swap rate wasn't available current, neither was the central bank's foreign-exchange swap position, and from the US futures market there's only open interest, not the net position. Rising interest alone doesn't tell you a direction, so I'm not inventing one.
