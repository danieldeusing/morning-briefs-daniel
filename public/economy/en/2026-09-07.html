One Barrel of Oil, Two Legs

Why the same commodity pushes your rate up and down at once

Brent sits at $97.36 — up 1.12% on the day, up 10.98% on the month, up 47.46% from a year earlier. The reason isn't a demand boom, it's a tanker war: the US says it permanently disabled two Iranian oil tankers and destroyed a third, after the Revolutionary Guard fired ballistic missiles toward a US aircraft carrier. At the same time, the US Navy escorted 40 merchant ships carrying 18 million barrels through the Strait of Hormuz — a wartime high. Iranian exports have been at a standstill since July. The conflict has been running since February 2026; this is the latest escalation, not a year-in-review item.

This matters more to you than it sounds, because your rate has two legs and this one barrel of oil acts on both — in opposite directions:

Which channel is winning right now? The Brazilian one. In the week Brent kept climbing, EUR/BRL lost 1.20%. To be honest about it: the trigger wasn't oil but the Quaest poll — the real jumped to a three-week high because the market reads Flávio Bolsonaro as more fiscally restrictive. Oil was the tailwind, not the engine. Brazil is a net oil exporter, per Bank of America; expensive oil improves the trade balance regardless of who governs.

My position: for the next 30 days, the oil shock is net negative for you. The euro channel is already spent — the hike has been priced in for weeks and, per the Reuters poll, is the last of the cycle (more on that further down). The real channel, by contrast, keeps working, every day Brent stays elevated. One caveat remains: if the Strait of Hormuz were actually closed, the sign flips, because the global risk-off reflex would then drag down every emerging-market currency — the real included. That's a regime change, not a trend, and I'm not pricing it in.

The Rate Gap Is Shrinking From Both Sides

The mechanism behind the 30-day tier

Carry trade: Borrow money where rates are low, and invest it where they're high — the difference is the profit. Like a kid who buys candy at the cheap store and resells it for more on the school bus: the margin carries the trade, as long as the price doesn't collapse along the way. For Brazil, that margin is the gap between the Selic and the US policy rate. When it shrinks, holding reais pays off less — and the real gives ground.

Today that gap is enormous: the Selic at 14.00% against a Fed range of 3.50–3.75%, midpoint 3.625% — 10.38 percentage points. Against the ECB deposit rate of 2.25%, it's even 11.75, dropping to 11.50 from Thursday.

On Sep 16, both ends get pulled. The Fed is being traded at around 58% odds of a hike to 3.75–4.00% (CME FedWatch, as of Sep 4) — the day before, it was 49.4%. The trigger for the jump was the August jobs report, with 162,000 new positions against roughly 53,000 expected. In the same 48-hour window, the Copom meets, and per the Focus median it keeps cutting toward 13.75%.

Fed +25bp→ Copom −25bp→ 10.38 → 9.88pp→ Carry thinner→ BRL weaker→ EUR/BRL higher

Half a percentage point sounds small. What matters is the direction: it would be the first two-sided compression of this cycle — until now, only the Copom was cutting while the Fed held steady. Still, this shouldn't be overstated: a nearly ten-point edge remains historically enormous, and as long as it holds, patience in holding reais keeps paying off.

Thursday Is the End, Not the Beginning

ECB, Sep 10 — and why your euro leg then sits still for a year

Starting point: the deposit rate has stood at 2.25% since Jun 11. On Thursday at 09:15 BRT, 25 basis points are very likely to come on top, to 2.50%. The trigger was the August flash estimate: eurozone inflation at 3.3%, up from 2.9% in July, the highest since September 2024, driven by energy at +14.3%, up from +10.3%.

What's new isn't the decision, it's what comes after. Reuters surveyed 65 economists: they expect the hike almost unanimously — and about 91% expect 2.50% to hold through year-end, 78% through at least mid-2027. Danske Bank sums up the meeting in three words: "hiking, not guiding" — Lagarde will preserve full optionality and avoid committing to a fixed path, which limits the market reaction.

Core inflation: price growth excluding energy and food — that is, excluding precisely the two items a central bank can barely reach with interest rates. Like subtracting gas and produce when you look at your household budget, to see whether everything is really getting pricier or just those two volatile items. In the eurozone it fell to 2.4% in August, down from 2.5%. That's exactly why one step gets taken and then it's over: the central bank is reacting to a supply shock, not overheated demand — and no interest rate helps against a supply shock; more expensive money doesn't open up a pipeline.

What this means for you, in concrete numbers. The latest bank survey (Aug 23) sees the EUR/USD median at ~1.15 in Q3 2026, ~1.165 in Q4, and 1.18 in Q1 and Q2 2027. From today's level (1.16101), that's +1.6% over roughly nine months — that's the entire expected contribution from your euro leg. Two honest caveats: spot is already above the Q3 median, and the survey predates the Sep 4 jobs report that flipped the Fed's expectation to a hike. A hiking Fed against a parked ECB argues against 1.18 rather than for it.

My position Weigh them against each other: euro leg, about 1.6% expected contribution over twelve months. Real leg: the named calls spread from 5.00 to 5.40 USD/BRL — eight percent — and with the Morgan Stanley tail at 6.00, twenty. Five to twelve times as wide. From Friday on, Lagarde is background noise for your rate. Anyone still watching Frankfurt is watching the wrong leg.

Transparency: individual bank targets for EUR/USD from the same survey are circulating that contradicted each other across two independent research passes; the source page is paywalled. I therefore use only the median, which both passes returned identically.

Positioning — and the Honest Gaps

Brazilian bonds, flows, and what's simply closed today

Real yield (NTN-B): A Brazilian government bond that strips out inflation — you're guaranteed IPCA plus X percent, no matter how high inflation jumps. Like a savings account that grows along whenever bread gets more expensive. The X is the real yield: the actual value gain the market demands from the government.

In mid-August, Tesouro IPCA+ paid 7% to over 8% real, depending on maturity — a band reached in less than 10% of the historical series. As of Aug 17, so three weeks old, not a daily figure. In the same period, roughly R$257B was paid out from maturing 2026 NTN-Bs, with no automatic reinvestment — a large pool of domestic money up for reallocation right in the middle of the election window.

On foreign flows, only a qualitative read, because the figures couldn't be dated cleanly: August brought record outflows on the tracked series, and the Ibovespa rally happened anyway — so it wasn't carried by returning foreign investors. Nomad names that same capital flight as a risk for the real.

It's Monday, September seventh, and Brazil is marking two hundred four years of independence. The stock exchange is closed, the banks are closed, the central bank is closed. There's no official dollar rate today, no Ibovespa close, no Focus survey. You can't exchange money today. You can think about it, though — and a holiday is honestly a better day for that than any trading day.

The last valid rate is Friday's fix from the European Central Bank: five point nine four zero five reais to the euro. Today only the offshore market is open, and if you multiply its two pieces together — dollar against real near five point one three, euro against dollar near one point one six — you get about five point nine five three. That's roughly two tenths of a percent above Friday. But it's not an official fix, just a computed number from a thin market. Treat it as a placeholder, not a signal.

The real story today comes out of Frankfurt, and it isn't the rate decision itself — it's a survey. Reuters polled sixty-five economists, and they're almost unanimous that the European Central Bank raises rates by a quarter point on Thursday, to two and a half percent. What matters more is what they say happens next: ninety-one percent expect that two and a half percent to hold through year-end, seventy-eight percent expect it to hold through at least the middle of twenty twenty-seven. Thursday, in other words, isn't the start of a cycle. It's the end of one.

That has a pretty blunt consequence for you. Your rate has two legs: the euro leg and the real leg. If the European Central Bank sits still for a year after Thursday, the euro leg is essentially parked. The latest bank survey gives the euro only about one point six percent of expected gain against the dollar over the next nine months. The named forecasts for the real leg, meanwhile, spread across eight percent over the same stretch — and if you count Morgan Stanley's tail case, twenty percent. That's five to twelve times as wide. Bottom line: stop watching Lagarde. Your rate gets decided in Brasília.

The other big driver today is oil. Brent crude is near ninety-seven dollars, up eleven percent for the month and forty-seven percent for the year. The reason is a tanker war: the United States says it has disabled Iranian oil tankers, Iran has fired missiles toward American warships, and the US Navy is escorting merchant ships through the Strait of Hormuz. And this single barrel of oil pulls your two currency legs in opposite directions.
