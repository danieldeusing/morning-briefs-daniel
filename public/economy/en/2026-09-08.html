The Anchors Are Stale — Their Oil Argument Has Flipped

Why the spread across forecasts is a dating problem, not a difference of opinion

There is no named bank that forecasts EUR/BRL — not one. What exists are USD/BRL forecasts that you have to multiply by a euro leg yourself. At today's ECB cross rate (EUR/USD 1.1622, Sep 7), that looks like this:

Seven of nine anchors sit above today's spot. That sounds like consensus for a weaker real — but the median anchor is only 1.4% above today's rate, sixteen months out. That's not a forecast, it's a shrug. The Focus median has stood unchanged at 5.20 for ten weeks.

And now the real problem. Itaú raised its call from 5.15 to 5.30 on Jun 26 and explicitly cited "the deterioration in terms of trade, driven by the decline in oil prices" as the reason. That exact premise no longer exists: Brent trades at $97.62 on Sep 8, up 11.29% on the month and up 47.04% on the year. It gets even sharper with BTG. Back in May, the house argued the same commodity logic in the opposite direction — "Brazil is a net oil exporter; with the barrel up, the country earns more dollars" — and called for 4.90. Eight weeks later, on Jul 8, it raised the call to 5.40, this time citing a hawkish Fed.

Terms of trade: the price ratio between what a country sells and what it buys. Like a kid trading collectible cards: if your cards suddenly get more popular than everyone else's, you get more back for the same card. Brazil sells oil, soybeans, and iron ore — when their prices rise, more dollars flow into the country, and the real gets stronger.

My position: both of BTG's premises are alive at once today — but only one is still uncertain. Oil is measured and dated: up 47% year over year. The hawkish Fed is priced at 52 to 61%, so a coin flip. Reading the anchors, then, means reading nine timestamps, not nine opinions about Brazil. Take the Focus median for what it is — a ten-week-old shrug — and weigh the two live drivers yourself: oil (verifiable, strong) and the election (unknowable, in 26 days).

Where We Stand — Four Days Without an Executable Rate

A look back at the last trading days, and the blind spot

The real had a strong week and gave a little back at the close. The Brazilian central bank's official PTAX closing rates trace the path cleanly:

6.0309 (Aug 28)→ 5.9227 (Sep 3)→ 5.9540 (Sep 4)→ 5.9576 (ECB, Sep 7)

Peak to trough, that's −1.79% over four sessions, then +0.53% given back on Friday. On the dollar leg, the same pattern, just sharper: 5.2002 → 5.0959 (−2.01%), then back up to 5.1250.

PTAX vs. ECB fix: both are official daily snapshots of the same exchange rate, just measured by two different authorities at two different times. Like two referees timing the same race but stopping the clock at different points on the finish line. The ECB fix is pulled around 14:15 Frankfurt time — 09:15 for you. The BCB's PTAX close comes from Friday at 13:03 BRT, almost four hours later in the day.

The cross-check, and why it's worth more today than usual. On normal days, you'd compare the ECB fix against a cross of USD/BRL × EUR/USD — which doesn't tell you much when all three numbers come from the same institution, because then you're only checking your own arithmetic. Today it's better: the ECB says 5.9576 (Sep 7), the Brazilian central bank says 5.9540 (Sep 4). That's a 0.06% deviation — two completely separate institutions, three calendar days and a closed market in between; on the dollar leg, it's only 0.02%. This brief's threshold for flagging a discrepancy is 0.5%. We're nowhere near it: the rate is real, not an artifact of a thin holiday book. The blind spot remains, though — between Friday 13:03 BRT and this morning there was no Brazilian price, and oil's continued climb only gets processed in the book today.

Thursday Is Paid For, Friday Isn't

Why the ECB meeting is the smaller event of the week

The ECB has held a deposit rate of 2.25% since Jun 17, 2026 (main refinancing 2.40%, marginal lending 2.65%). Before that, the rate had stood at 2.00% since Jun 11, 2025 — so June's move was the first hike in three years, and Thursday will be the second. All 65 economists surveyed by Reuters between Aug 31 and Sep 3 expect +25 basis points to 2.50%; markets price that at 98.9%.

What's interesting isn't the move itself, but what comes after — and there two camps diverge: 91% of economists see 2.50% holding through year-end, 78% through at least mid-2027. Reuters calls this the ECB's shortest tightening phase since 2011. Rate futures, meanwhile, are pricing in a third hike. That contradiction is the actual tradable content of Thursday: Lagarde's tone and the new staff projections will decide which camp has to give ground.

The trigger for the hike is an energy price, not a demand boom. The August flash estimate put headline inflation at 3.3% (July 2.9%) — but core inflation fell to 2.4% (July 2.5%), below the consensus expectation of 2.5%.

Core inflation: price growth excluding energy and food — that is, excluding precisely the two items a central bank can barely reach with interest rates. Like subtracting gas and produce when you look at your household budget, to see whether everything is really getting pricier or just those two volatile items. It's falling right now. That's exactly why one step gets taken and then it's over.

Friday is the unpriced day. On Sep 11, the US Consumer Price Index for August and Brazil's IPCA for August both land. Two inflation prints, one date, both legs of your rate. And here's a calculation many people get wrong: your rate is a product, EUR/BRL = EUR/USD × USD/BRL. A hot US CPI strengthens the dollar — and the dollar sits on both sides of that product.

So the dollar partly cancels itself out. Only because the real typically reacts about twice as hard against the dollar as the euro does, does a net upward move remain — about half the size the headline "dollar strong, real weak" would suggest. The same holds in reverse: a cool US CPI helps you less than it looks like it should.

The Rate Gap Is Closing From Both Sides

Sep 16 brings the Fed and Copom into the same hours

Carry: borrow money where rates are low, and invest it where they're high — the difference is the profit. Like a kid who buys candy at the cheap store and resells it for more on the school bus: the margin carries the trade, as long as the price doesn't collapse along the way. For the real, that margin is the gap between the Selic and the policy rate in Europe or the US. When it shrinks, holding reais pays off less — and the real gives ground.

Brazil stands at a Selic of 14.00%, cut on Aug 5 — the fourth cut in a row, a full percentage point in total since March. Against the ECB deposit rate of 2.25%, that's an 11.75 percentage-point cushion. After Thursday, it's 11.50. And if the Copom follows the path the Focus median assumes — Selic at 13.75% by year-end — it's 11.25.

Against the dollar, the same move, just tighter: the Fed stands at 3.50–3.75%, 10.38 percentage points below the Selic. If it hikes on Sep 16 and the Copom cuts in the same cycle, that would be 9.88.

Fed +25bp→ Copom −25bp→ 10.38 → 9.88pp→ Carry thinner→ EUR/BRL higher

Both decisions land on Wednesday, Sep 16 — the Fed in the afternoon US time with a new dot plot, the Copom from around 18:00 BRT. That's the first two-sided rate compression of this cycle, and it happens within a few hours of each other.

To be honest about it: whether the Copom actually cuts on Sep 16 isn't confirmed — the Focus path calls for only one more 25-basis-point step through year-end, and Sep 16 is simply the next opportunity for it. The Fed, too, at 52 to 61%, is no sure thing. The point isn't that both are certain to happen, but that both get decided on the same day: your rate cushion gets completely repriced that Wednesday.

That premise doesn't exist anymore. Brent crude is trading near ninety-seven dollars and sixty-two cents a barrel today, up eleven percent for the month and forty-seven percent for the year. Brazil is a net oil exporter — rising oil prices bring the country more dollars, not fewer.

It gets even more awkward with BTG Pactual. Back in May, the bank used that exact same commodity logic to argue the opposite direction and called for four point nine zero. Eight weeks later it hiked its own forecast to five point four zero, this time blaming a hawkish Federal Reserve. The Fed hike is priced at roughly half, so call it a coin flip. My take: what looks like nine different opinions about Brazil is really nine different timestamps.

This week has two real dates. On Thursday, the European Central Bank is expected to hike almost unanimously — all sixty-five economists surveyed by Reuters see a quarter point, to two and a half percent, and the market prices that at ninety-nine percent. What's actually interesting is what comes after: ninety-one percent of those economists think that's it for the year, seventy-eight percent think it holds through at least the middle of twenty twenty-seven — yet interest-rate futures are still pricing in a third hike.

The day that isn't priced in yet is Friday, when the US Consumer Price Index for August and Brazil's own August inflation reading land on the same date — repricing both legs of your rate at once. Here's the part people get wrong: your rate is a product of two exchange rates, euro-to-dollar times dollar-to-real. A hot US inflation print strengthens the dollar, which pushes one leg down and the other up — so it partly cancels out. The net move ends up only about half as big as the headline would suggest.

Next week brings a double Wednesday: on September sixteenth, the Federal Reserve and Brazil's central bank both decide within hours of each other. Brazil's policy rate sits at fourteen percent right now, eleven and three-quarter points above the European deposit rate. After Thursday, that gap narrows to eleven and a half. And if Brazil's central bank cuts again as its own survey expects, it narrows further, to eleven and a quarter. Your interest-rate cushion could shrink by half a point within a single week.
