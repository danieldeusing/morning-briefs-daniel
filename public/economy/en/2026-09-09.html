Where We Stand — the Rally That's Costing You Money

Thirteen sessions, −2.38%, and a cross-check exactly at the threshold

The real has gained ground steadily since August 20, with a single genuine pullback. The Brazilian central bank's official PTAX rates trace the path without a gap:

6.0570 (Aug 20)→ 6.0315 (Aug 28)→ 5.9233 (Sep 3)→ 5.9546 (Sep 4)→ 5.9130 (Sep 8)

The low of the series is the most recent point — 5.9130, yesterday. From the high, that's −2.38%. The dollar leg shows the same picture, just with a different peak: 5.2005 on Aug 28 down to 5.0856 yesterday, −2.21%.

What this actually means. Exchange €1,000 today and you get about R$5,913 instead of the R$6,057 you'd have gotten on August 20: R$144 less. At €5,000, that's R$720 — the difference between two calendar days on which you could have made the trade.

The cross-check, and why it stands out today. The ECB fix and the Brazilian PTAX measure the same rate at different times of day. Yesterday they were 0.389% apart on the euro leg (5.9360 vs. 5.9130) and 0.501% apart on the dollar leg — exactly at the line where this brief flags a discrepancy. That's not a data error, it's the direction of the day: the ECB fix is pulled at 14:15 Frankfurt time, 09:15 for you; the PTAX averages four windows through around 13:00 BRT. The real kept appreciating in between. So the gap isn't measuring disagreement, it's measuring speed — and it tells you that exchanging money yesterday afternoon in Brazil was worse than exchanging it in the morning.

PTAX vs. ECB fix: two official daily snapshots of the same exchange rate, measured by two authorities at two different times. Like two referees timing the same race but stopping the clock at different points on the finish line. As long as they differ by less than half a percent, all is well; when the gap sits right at the threshold, as it did yesterday, it's worth checking which direction the day was running.

The Trigger Is a Poll in a Statistical Tie

41 to 41 with a ±2-point margin — and the market is trading it like a result

On Monday, Quaest published a poll for Globo on the runoff: Lula 41%, Flávio Bolsonaro 41%. Fieldwork ran Sep 3–6, 2,004 respondents, margin of error ±2 percentage points. In the first round, Lula still led 38 to 30 as of late August. TradingEconomics explicitly names the poll as the reason for the real's three-week high — market participants read Flávio Bolsonaro as more fiscally restrictive.

graph LR
  A["Quaest: 41 to 41 in the runoff"] --> B["Market prices higher odds of restrictive fiscal policy"]
  B --> C["Brazil risk premium falls"]
  C --> D["Inflows: Ibovespa +1.2%, EWZ +2.0%"]
  D --> E["USD/BRL falls to 5.0856"]
  E --> F["EUR/BRL falls to 5.9130 — your rate"]
Margin of error: a poll doesn't ask everyone, just a few thousand people — so the result is only approximately right. "±2 points" means the true value probably sits somewhere between 39 and 43. Like grabbing a handful of candy from a big jar and estimating how many are red from that: roughly right, never exact. At 41 to 41, both ranges overlap completely — statistically, that's not a tie, it's simply no reading at all.

My position: the market is overpaying here for a headline with zero informational content. A jump from 38–30 in the first round to 41–41 in the simulated runoff is a modeling exercise, not a measured election result, and it falls well within the range two surveys from the same pollster can drift apart purely by chance. And yet it has moved the rate 2.4% over thirteen sessions. That doesn't make the move wrong — it makes it fragile: a poll next week showing 43–39 in the other direction could send the rate right back the way it came.

And there's a named counter-witness. On September 8 — the very day of the rally — BTG Pactual cut its own macro forecast: 2026 GDP from 2.0% to 1.8%, gross debt-to-GDP for 2026 from 82.6% to 83.2%, and for 2027 to 87.6% — with the line that the current pace of debt accumulation is "insustentável," unsustainable. So the rate is celebrating a candidate, while the freshest sell-side model is drawing the fiscal curve steeper regardless of who wins.

Oil Tears Toward $100 — and Pulls in Both Directions

Brent +46.7% on the year, because tankers are burning in the Gulf

Brent sits at $99.01 a barrel, up 1.11% on the day, up 12.87% on the month, and up 46.70% on the year — the highest level in nearly seven weeks. The reason is military: Iran's Revolutionary Guard reported hits on two US ships and eight oil tankers in the Gulf on Wednesday; the US had previously struck three Iranian tankers. For Brazil, that cuts both ways:

Brent $99→ Brazil's export earnings rise→ Real firmer
Brent $99→ Global inflation→ ECB/Fed more hawkish→ Rate cushion thinner→ Real weaker
Terms of trade: the price ratio between what a country sells and what it buys. Like a kid trading collectible cards: if your cards suddenly get more popular, you get more back for the same card. Brazil sells oil, soybeans, and iron ore — when their prices rise, more dollars flow into the country and the real gets stronger.

Today, the upper path is clearly winning: Petrobras carried the Ibovespa up 2.1% yesterday, and the dollar index is only holding still because oil is unsettling the inflation outlook. The lower path is the slower one — it plays out over the central bank meetings of the coming months, not over the day.

Double Wednesday, Inflows, and the Open Gaps

What happens on Sep 16, where the money is flowing, and what's missing today

Carry: borrow money where rates are low and invest it where they're high — the difference is the profit. Like a kid who buys candy at the cheap store and resells it for more on the school bus: the margin carries the trade, as long as the price doesn't collapse along the way. For the real, that margin is the gap between the Selic and the policy rates in Europe and the US.

The Selic stands at 14.00%, cut in August from 14.25% — the fourth straight 25-basis-point step. Against the ECB deposit rate of 2.25%, that's an 11.75-percentage-point cushion; after Thursday, 11.50. Against the midpoint of the Fed's band (3.625%), it's 10.38.

Fed +25bp (57.4%)→ Copom −25bp (consensus)→ 10.38 → 9.88pp→ Carry thinner→ EUR/BRL higher

Both land on Wednesday, Sep 16: the Copom meets Sep 15–16 and decides from around 18:00 BRT; the FOMC decides the same day, with a new dot plot and press conference. Market-implied probabilities for the Fed stood at 57.4% hike, 42.6% hold, 0.0% cut as of Sep 8 — a coin flip with a slight tilt, not a sure thing.

Where the money is flowing. The Ibovespa closed Sep 8 at 187,367 points, +1.20% on the day, +8.82% on the month, +32.30% on the year; the Brazil ETF EWZ stood at $38.61, +1.98%, up 36.16% over the year including dividends. Both confirm the inflow path from the poll-driven chain: it isn't just the currency being bought, it's Brazil as a whole.

What's missing There are again no solid figures today for positioning or Brazilian bonds, and I'm not going to guess at them. The IPCA reading is still July's (+0.07% on the month, 4.44% over twelve months) — the August figure lands Friday.

It's Wednesday, September ninth, and Brazil's headlines are celebrating the real today — its strongest level in more than three weeks. For you, that same number carries the opposite sign: if you earn in euros and spend in reais, you're looking at the worst exchange rate you've seen since August twentieth.

Here's the number: yesterday's European Central Bank reference fix came in at five point nine three six reais to the euro, down thirty-six hundredths of a percent from Monday. Brazil's own official close for that same day was even lower, at five point nine one three — the low for thirteen trading sessions. That's down two point four percent from the high on August twentieth. In real money terms, a thousand euros buys you about one hundred forty-four reais less today than it did three weeks ago. At five thousand euros, that's seven hundred twenty.

The trigger behind this move is remarkably thin. It isn't an economic release or a central bank decision — it's an election poll. The pollster Quaest, working for Globo, measured a simulated runoff at forty-one to forty-one, Lula against Flávio Bolsonaro, dead even. Two thousand four respondents, a two-point margin of error. The market reads Flávio Bolsonaro as the more fiscally restrictive candidate and is buying Brazilian assets accordingly.

My take: the market is paying real money for a headline with essentially no information in it. With a two-point margin of error and a result of forty-one to forty-one, the two candidates' likely ranges overlap completely. Statistically, that's not a tie — it's simply not a finding at all. And yet it has moved the rate two point four percent over thirteen sessions. That doesn't make the move wrong, but it does make it fragile: a poll next week pointing the other way could send the rate right back where it came from.

A correction of our own here. Yesterday's brief claimed that the Brazilian central bank's database returned zero records for every survey date from September first onward. That was wrong. Today's pull from that same database returns data for September first through fourth. So there is a current Focus survey, dated September fourth, with one hundred thirteen institutions weighing in. It puts the year-end dollar unchanged at five point two zero, inflation at five percent, the policy rate at thirteen point seven five, and growth at one point nine three percent.

One line in there deserves special attention. The five forecasters the central bank ranks as most accurate see the dollar at five point two one by the end of twenty twenty-seven, versus five point three zero for the group as a whole. The people who've called it best so far expect a stronger real than the average forecast — which, for you, is the more pessimistic anchor.

The freshest counter-argument comes from BTG Pactual, and it's from just yesterday. The bank cut its twenty twenty-six growth forecast from two percent to one point eight percent, and raised its gross-debt forecast to eighty-three point two percent of GDP this year and eighty-seven point six percent next year. The current pace of debt accumulation, the bank says, isn't sustainable. So the market is celebrating a candidate while the freshest model on the desk is drawing the fiscal picture steeper.

Two other forces are in play. First, oil: Brent sits at ninety-nine dollars and a penny, up nearly forty-seven percent from a year ago, because a tanker war is raging in the Gulf. That's a tailwind for Brazil as a net oil exporter, but it's also pushing global inflation higher and making central banks tougher. Second, the calendar: on Thursday, the European Central Bank is all but certain to raise rates — all sixty-five economists surveyed expect it, and the market prices it at ninety-nine percent. An event priced in that thoroughly doesn't move anything just by happening — the only tradable part is Lagarde's tone.

The day that actually matters this week is Friday, when Brazil's and America's August inflation numbers land on the same date. And a week from now comes a double Wednesday: on September sixteenth, the Fed and Brazil's central bank both decide within hours of each other. Your rate cushion against the dollar, currently ten point four percentage points, could shrink to nine point nine on that single day.

My forecast: today, downward-sticky, five point eight eight to five point nine five, at seventy-two percent confidence, most likely five point nine two. This week, two-sided, five point eight four to six even. Over thirty days, tilted higher toward six point two five, most likely six even. Over twelve months, five point seven zero to six point five five, most likely six point one five. Those percentages are my own confidence, not the market's.
