The Rally Has Stopped — and the Cross-Check Is Clean Again

Seven sessions sideways, +0.75% off the low, and a 0.030% gap between two authorities

Yesterday's brief was about the real costing you money since August 20. That's still true — but the move behind it has come to a halt. The ECB reference fixings for the last seven trading days:

6.0255 (Sep 1)→ 5.8935 (Sep 3)→ 5.9576 (Sep 7)→ 5.9296 (Sep 9)

The low of the whole series falls on September 3, not today. Since then the rate has swung within a range of about 1.1% with no clear direction. From the low, that's +0.61% on the fix and +0.75% on this morning's live cross.

What that means in money. Against the August 20 high, €1,000 buys you about R$137 less today (at €5,000: R$685). But against the September 3 low, you're already getting R$36 more per €1,000. Whoever exchanged on September 3 caught the worst moment of the quarter so far.

The cross-check. Two authorities publish the same rate at different times of day: the ECB at 14:15 Frankfurt time, Brazil's central bank as a PTAX average across four windows through around 13:00 BRT. Yesterday they were 0.030% apart on the euro leg (5.9296 vs. 5.9278) and 0.177% apart on the dollar leg. For comparison: the day before it was 0.389% and 0.501%. This number is interesting precisely because it doesn't measure disagreement, it measures speed. If the real keeps moving strongly during the Brazilian session, the two clocks diverge; if it holds still, they align. So the 0.03% gap tells you the appreciation wave has petered out within the trading day — before you can even see it in the closing rate.

Reference fix: an official daily snapshot of an exchange rate, taken at a fixed time. Like a photo-finish shot at the finish line: it captures exactly one hundredth of a second, not the whole race. Two authorities photographing at different times inevitably get slightly different pictures — as long as the gap is small, that's not an error, just the time difference.

The bigger picture still isn't comfortable: your rate sits in the bottom quarter of its yearly range (52-week high 6.6181, low 5.7208, today 5.9375 — 24% up from the bottom).

Why a Dollar Shock Barely Moves Your Rate

The mechanics behind why today is a special day

There's no directly traded world market for euro against real. Your rate is built as a cross: EUR/BRL = EUR/USD × USD/BRL. Both legs hang off the dollar — and that has a consequence most commentary leaves out.

Cross rate: an exchange rate nobody trades directly — it's calculated from two others. Like wanting to know how many gummy bears a comic book is worth, but only knowing what both cost in marbles: you work it out through the marbles. The dollar is the marble here — and if the marble itself becomes more valuable, the comic-to-gummy-bear ratio barely changes.

If the dollar strengthens globally — say, after a hot US inflation print — EUR/USD falls and USD/BRL rises. In the product, the two effects largely cancel each other out:

graph LR
  A["Dollar globally +1%"] --> B["EUR/USD falls about 1%"]
  A --> C["USD/BRL rises about 1%"]
  B --> D["EUR/BRL = EUR/USD x USD/BRL"]
  C --> D
  D --> E["Net: only a small residual move up"]

The residual remains because the real is high-beta: it reacts to dollar strength by more than a one-to-one ratio. But it's still small. That's why EUR/BRL has stood still for a week even though oil has jumped eleven percent and the dollar index has lost 0.68%.

What actually moves your rate are events that touch only one leg:

Only the euro leg
ECB decisions and projections, European inflation data, European fiscal and bond crises (today: pressure on French government bonds).
Only the real leg
Copom, Brazilian inflation and fiscal figures, the Oct 4 election, commodity prices via the terms of trade.

My take: that's exactly why today is an above-average important day for you, even though the decision itself is priced in. The ECB is one of only four or five dates a quarter where the euro leg speaks on its own — and this one coincides with new staff projections that, for the first time, have to process an oil price above $100.

The ECB Hikes Because Oil Is Forcing Its Hand

3.3% inflation, of which energy +14.3% — and a decision that's 99% priced in

The ECB deposit rate has stood at 2.25% since June 17 (main refinancing 2.40%, marginal lending 2.65%). Today at 09:15 BRT, that's very likely to become 2.50%. The reason is in Eurostat's September 1 flash estimate: eurozone inflation jumped in August to 3.3% from 2.9% in July — the highest level since September 2023. And the breakdown is unambiguous:

Everything except energy is tame. So the ECB is hiking against a shock it can't influence with interest rates — it's hiking so oil inflation doesn't seep into expectations.

Priced in: when everyone expects the same thing, it's already reflected in the rate before it happens. Like a birthday present whose contents everyone already knows: unwrapping it triggers no reaction. Surprise — and with it, price movement — only happens if there's something different in the box than everyone thought.

What this means for you mechanically is clear regardless of tone — and it works slowly, but in only one direction:

ECB 2.25 → 2.50%→ Carry 11.75 → 11.50pp→ Holding reais becomes less attractive→ EUR/BRL tends higher

To put the size of this cushion in perspective: Brazil's policy rate, net of inflation, sits at around 9.8 percentage points in real terms (Selic 14.00% against 4.24% twelve-month inflation in the preview index). The eurozone sits at −0.8 in real terms (2.50% against 3.3%). The gap is absurdly wide — and that's exactly why it can shrink for years without the real toppling overnight.

Brent Above $100 — the Shock Behind Both Central Banks

+11.85% in seven sessions, +52% on the year, because Hormuz is nearly shut

Brent stands at $100.97 a barrel, briefly touching $102.57 overnight, and closed at $101.21 yesterday. TradingEconomics puts today's level at $100.88 — two independent sources, both clearly above the hundred mark. The path there ran over seven sessions: $90.49 on Aug 31 → $101.21 on Sep 9, or +11.85%; up 52% on the year.

The trigger is military: US strikes on Iranian tankers near Kharg Island, Houthi attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure including the Jazan refinery (400,000 barrels/day). Crude flows through the Strait of Hormuz sit below 2 million barrels a day — before the fighting it was 8 to 9. One analyst scenario sees $120 if disruption continues.

Terms of trade: the price ratio between what a country sells and what it buys. Like a kid trading collectible cards: if your cards suddenly get more popular, you get more back for the same card. Brazil sells oil, soybeans, and iron ore — when their prices rise, more dollars flow into the country and the real gets stronger.

For your rate, oil pulls in both directions, but on different timescales:

Brent $101→ Brazil's export earnings rise→ Real firmer→ EUR/BRL lower (bad for you)
Brent $101→ Energy HICP +14.3%→ ECB hikes→ Euro firmer + carry thinner→ EUR/BRL higher (good for you)

The upper path works immediately, day by day; the lower one plays out through central bank meetings. That's why oil has recently supported the real — and why the effect reverses over the coming quarters.

It's Thursday, September tenth, and today revolves around a quarter-past-nine appointment your time: the European Central Bank's rate decision. That might sound like background noise, but it's actually one of the few events on the calendar that moves only half of your exchange rate — the euro half — which is exactly what makes it worth paying attention to.

Here's the mechanics in a nutshell. Your rate is really two rates chained together: euro against dollar, and dollar against real. Almost everything that makes headlines pushes both of those legs the same way, so the effects mostly cancel out. That's why your rate has barely budged over the past week even though oil has jumped past one hundred dollars a barrel. What actually moves your number is something that hits only one leg — and the European Central Bank today is exactly that kind of event.

Now the numbers. Yesterday's official euro reference rate came in at five point nine two nine six reais, a tenth of a percent below the day before. But the rate kept moving afterward: it closed in New York at five point nine four five seven, and this morning it's trading around five point nine three seven five. And here's the genuinely good news buried in this issue: the three-week rally in the real that's been costing you money since August twentieth came to a stop on September third. Since that low, your rate has already recovered three-quarters of a percent.

In money terms: against the August twentieth high, a thousand euros still buys you about one hundred thirty-seven reais less today. But against the September third low, you're already getting thirty-six reais more per thousand euros. Whoever exchanged money on September third caught the worst moment of the quarter.

One more small but telling detail. The European Central Bank and Brazil's central bank publish the same rate at different times of day, and yesterday the two readings were only three hundredths of a percent apart, down from nearly four tenths the day before. That gap doesn't measure disagreement, it measures speed: the closer the two clocks align, the more the real's intraday moves have slowed down. In this case, it's telling you the rally has essentially stalled.

On to the bank itself. The deposit rate has sat at two point two five percent since mid-June, and the market is pricing in roughly a ninety-nine percent chance of a quarter-point hike today, to two point five zero. The reason is inflation: the eurozone's August reading jumped to three point three percent, the highest since 2023, driven almost entirely by energy prices, up over fourteen percent. Everything else in the basket is tame. So the central bank is hiking against a shock it can't actually control with interest rates — it's doing it so the oil spike doesn't spread into longer-term inflation expectations.

Because the hike itself is already priced in, it can't move your rate. What can move it is anything unexpected: new economic projections that, for the first time, have to account for oil above a hundred dollars, and the tone Christine Lagarde takes about whether this is the last hike or not. One bank, ING, flags a real risk here: a so-called dovish hike, where rates go up but the euro doesn't, because pressure on French government bonds has the central bank playing defense.

Behind both this decision and Brazil's own rate path sits the same story: oil. Brent crude is trading above a hundred dollars a barrel, up nearly twelve percent in just seven trading sessions and over fifty percent for the year, driven by escalating conflict between the United States and Iran that has choked oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz. For Brazil, that cuts two ways: in the short run, higher oil exports strengthen the real; over the coming months, it pushes central banks everywhere toward higher rates, which thins your interest-rate cushion and works in your favor.

Speaking of that cushion: after today's hike it narrows from eleven point seven five to eleven point five percentage points. It could narrow further, fast, in less than a week. On September sixteenth, the Federal Reserve and Brazil's Copom both meet on the same Wednesday, just three hours apart, and forecasters expect them to move in opposite directions, the Fed up, Copom down. If that happens, your cushion against the dollar alone could fall from ten point four to nine point nine points in a single day.

Forecasters are unusually split on where this all lands. One European bank, ING, sees your rate at five point six five a year from now. A survey of over a hundred Brazilian institutions implies six point two five. That's a ten-and-a-half-percent gap, and it's entirely about the real, not the euro. My own view sits in between, leaning toward the survey: ING's number requires the real to keep strengthening even as Brazil's own rate advantage shrinks, and those two things are hard to square at the same time.

My own numbers: today, two-sided with a slight upward lean, five point nine zero to five point nine eight, most likely five point nine four. This week, five point eight eight to six point zero two. Over the next thirty days, tilted higher toward six point two five, most likely six even. Over twelve months, five point six five to six point four five, most likely six point one zero. Those are my confidence levels, not the market's. Tomorrow brings the US inflation report for August — the first real test of how much of this oil shock has already fed through to American prices.
