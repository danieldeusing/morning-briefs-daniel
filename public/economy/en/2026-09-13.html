Three Weeks, 711 Reais — and Who Took Them

FX in detail — where we stand and why

Reference fix: Every business day around 14:15 CET (09:15 BRT), the ECB polls what a currency costs and publishes one official daily rate from that. Like a class photo: one captured moment, not a movie. No photo gets taken on weekends — Friday's value simply stands.

Nobody cares about the daily move today; there wasn't one. What matters is the arc. On Aug 20, the fix stood at 6.0666; on Sep 11, at 5.9244. That's −2.34%. On a €5,000 exchange: R$30,333 back then, R$29,622 now — a difference of R$711 for exactly the same work.[1]

What matters is which leg caused it — that determines whether it comes back. Your cross has no direct market; the euro and the real are converted through the dollar. Both legs moved against you, but very unevenly:

EUR/USD 1.1681 → 1.1592 (−0.76%)× USD/BRL 5.1936 → 5.1108 (−1.59%)= EUR/BRL −2.34%

Two-thirds of the loss is a Brazil story, only one-third euro weakness. The real strengthened against the dollar — carried by Brent above $100 (Brazil is a net oil exporter), by a rate advantage that's still double digits even after four cuts, and by an election race the market has been pricing as more fiscally favorable since early September.

Cross-check The B3 dollar close on that same Friday (R$5.125) times the ECB's EUR/USD (1.1592) gives 5.9409 — +0.28% above the fix, under the 0.5% flag. The difference is the sampling window: the ECB measures at 09:15 BRT, Brazil closes around 17:00 BRT. So the market where you actually exchange money sits closer to 5.94 than to 5.92.[1][2]

What that means in practice: anyone waiting for a return toward 6.05 isn't waiting for a weaker euro — they're waiting for Brazil's tailwind to fade. And that's exactly what the answer for this week hinges on.

Wednesday: Three Central-Bank Moves, One Day

Rate advantage (carry): the gap between Brazilian and European interest rates. Like a savings account next door paying multiples more: as long as the exchange rate doesn't run away, the difference is your profit. This gap is the reason global money holds reais at all — and the smaller it gets, the less money stays.

On Wednesday, Sep 16, three things that would otherwise be weeks apart collide:

  • The ECB hike takes effect. The Council voted unanimously on Sep 10 for +25 bp on all three rates — deposit rate 2.50%, main refi 2.65%, marginal lending 2.90%. The ECB's table lists the effective date as Sep 16; through Tuesday, 2.25% formally still applies.[3][4]
  • The Fed decides. The target range currently sits at 3.50–3.75%, unchanged for five meetings. A hike to 3.75–4.00% is expected — the odds jumped to ~80–86% after the Sep 11 US CPI print (Kalshi ~81%), up from around 56%/48% before that release.[5][6]
  • Copom decides. Meeting Sep 15–16, Selic currently 14.00%, consensus −25 bp to 13.75% — the fifth straight step of this size. Minutes on Sep 22.[7]

My take — and it departs from the conventional read: almost every commentary frames the pincer as "Fed up plus Copom down = 50 bp less carry." That's true, but it's the dollar carry (10.25 → 9.75 points). For your cross, what counts is the euro carry — and it shrinks in the same week by exactly the same 50 basis points, just from two different sources:

today: 14.00 − 2.25 = 11.75→ ECB +25 takes effect+ Copom −25= Wed: 13.75 − 2.50 = 11.25

The difference isn't cosmetic. The Fed's decision hits both legs of your cross in opposite directions and largely cancels out — if it hikes, EUR/USD falls (costs you) and USD/BRL rises (helps you). The ECB piece and the Copom piece don't have that buffer: both act one-sidedly in the same direction, namely upward for you.

What's already priced in The Copom step is priced in — Focus puts the year-end Selic at exactly 13.75%.[8] The ECB hike has been known since Wednesday; EUR/USD briefly dipped below 1.1600 and stabilized around ~1.1610, because the market had already fully priced it in beforehand.[4] Since the Sep 11 US CPI print, the Fed hike is now also roughly 80–86% priced in.[6] The residual risk is a surprise hold, not the hike itself. That's why my weekly range is tight, not wide.

It's Sunday, September thirteenth. Nothing is trading, and there's no new reference rate — every number you're about to hear carries Friday the eleventh's date. That's exactly why today calls for a step back from the daily noise and a look at the arc of the last four weeks.

The European Central Bank's reference rate stands at five point nine two four four. On August twentieth it was six point zero six six six — a drop of two point three four percent. Exchange five thousand euros today and you get roughly seven hundred eleven reais less than you would have three weeks ago, for exactly the same money. Within the four-week range of five point eight nine to six point zero seven, you're sitting in the bottom fifth — the wrong side, if you're the one converting.

The real question is which leg drove that move. Your exchange rate has no direct market — euros and reais both get converted through the dollar. Two-thirds of the drop comes from a stronger real against the dollar; only one-third is euro weakness. So this is a Brazil story: oil above a hundred dollars a barrel, a rate advantage that's still in double digits even after four cuts, and an election race the market has been treating as more fiscally friendly since early September. Anyone hoping for a bounce back toward six point zero five isn't waiting on a weaker euro — they're waiting for Brazil's tailwind to run out.

On Wednesday, September sixteenth, three central-bank moves land on the same day. First, the European Central Bank's rate hike from September tenth actually takes effect — the deposit rate steps up from two point two five to two point five percent, exactly that day. Second, the Federal Reserve decides, with its target range currently at three point five to three point seventy-five percent. Here's where my read differs from the conventional one. Most commentary frames this as Fed up, Copom down, fifty basis points less carry — true, but that's the dollar carry. That's not a cosmetic distinction. A Fed move hits both legs of your cross in opposite directions and largely cancels itself out.
