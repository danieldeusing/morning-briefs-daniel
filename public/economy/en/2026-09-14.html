Why the Dollar Cancels Out of Your Rate

FX in detail — where we stand and why

Cross rate: EUR/BRL has no meaningful market of its own. Nobody trades euros directly against reais at scale — both trade against the dollar, and your rate comes out of the multiplication. Like a detour through a connecting station: you want to go from A to C, but every train only runs via B.

So the formula holds: EUR/BRL = EUR/USD × USD/BRL. This morning that's 1.1592 × 5.1220 = 5.9374; an independent quote from investing.com sits at 5.9380.[1][2][3] The official rate remains Friday's fix of 5.9244. Today this formula is more than bookkeeping — it's the answer to Wednesday. Because if the dollar gains equally on both ends, here's what happens to your rate: nothing.

Dollar +1% globally→ EUR/USD −1%× USD/BRL +1%= EUR/BRL ±0%

The most important sentence in this brief: a pure dollar move cancels out of your cross. Only the difference gets through — and that's where the asymmetry lies: the real is a high-yield emerging-market currency and reacts more sharply to US rate shocks than the euro does. So a surprisingly hawkish Wednesday would still reach you — via the detour of "the real suffers more," not "the dollar gets stronger."

Cross-check ECB fix from Sep 11 (5.9244) against the cross rate from the same day's B3 dollar close (R$5.125) times the ECB EUR/USD (1.1592) = 5.9409. Difference +0.278%, under the 0.5% flag; the cause is the time window — the ECB measures at 09:15 BRT, Brazil closes at 17:00 BRT, and the dollar rose 0.44% into the close.[1][4] Checking against three ECB legs would be tautological; the 0.278% holds because the dollar close is independently sourced.

For your actual exchange, that means: the market where you really trade sits closer to 5.94 than to the official 5.92 — and in September only Sep 3 (5.8935) was worse. Anyone waiting is waiting for real weakness, not a strong euro.

Wednesday: Two Central Banks, Two Directions

Rate advantage (carry): the gap between Brazilian and European interest rates. Like a savings account next door paying multiples more: as long as the exchange rate doesn't run away, the difference is your profit. This gap is the reason global money holds reais at all.

On Wednesday, Sep 16, two central banks decide three and a half hours apart — in opposite directions:

  • Fed, 15:00 BRT (press conference 15:30 BRT), with updated projections. Target range 3.50–3.75%, unchanged since Jul 29 — even then, three FOMC members already voted for a hike. Futures markets now price 85% odds of +25 bp to 3.75–4.00%: the first Fed hike since July 2023.[5][6][7]
  • Copom, ~18:30 BRT. Selic 14.00%, last cut Aug 5 (cycle 14.50 → 14.25 → 14.00). Consensus: −25 bp to 13.75%. Minutes on Sep 22.[8][9]
The Crack in the Fed Consensus Futures markets: 85% hike. The Reuters economist survey says almost the opposite — roughly 70% expect a hold, down from 90% in August.[10] A gap this wide is rare. My take: the survey ran from Sep 4 to Sep 9, so it closed before the hot US core inflation print on Sep 11 that drove the odds up in the first place. The futures market is the fresher information — but the survey still shows how thin the support is behind a move that hinges on a single data point.

Now the math that matters for you. Almost every commentary adds the two moves up to "50 basis points less carry." True — but that's the dollar carry. Yours is different:

Euro today: 14.00 − 2.50 = 11.50→ only Copom −25= Wed: 13.75 − 2.50 = 11.25

The dollar advantage falls on Wednesday from 10.25 to 9.75 points — minus 50. Your euro advantage only falls from 11.50 to 11.25 — minus 25, because the ECB already made its move on Sep 10 (deposit rate 2.50%, a "no brainer" in Lagarde's words) and it's long since priced in.[11] The pressure on your side is half the size of the headline number.

SEP / "dot plot": four times a year, each FOMC member anonymously marks a dot for where they see the policy rate heading — like a class writing down their guesses and the teacher pinning them all up at once. Wednesday is one of those dates, and a single move there can turn into an announced cycle.

So the variance doesn't come from the hike itself, but from the dot plot next to it: if it shows two or three more moves, that's a different animal than a one-off insurance step — and it hits the real harder than the euro. The second source of variance is Copom's wording three and a half hours later, which has to react to a north that just turned hawkish.

It's Monday, September fourteenth. The day itself is light on news — there's no European Central Bank reference rate yet; that won't post until around eleven o'clock Brazil time, and the new Focus bulletin wasn't available as of brief time. But on Wednesday, two major central banks decide three and a half hours apart, and they're headed in opposite directions.

Start with the numbers. Your rate sits around five point nine three eight. That's an intraday spot quote, not an official fix — the last official reading is Friday's, five point nine two four four. The dollar in Brazil costs five point one two two, essentially unchanged. And Brent crude sits at one hundred seven dollars and twenty-one cents, up two and a half percent on the day and fifty-nine percent over the past year.

That fifty-nine percent is really the whole story, because it explains both rate decisions on Wednesday. The same oil price that's keeping American inflation hot is also filling Brazil's trade balance and propping up the real. One cause, two opposite effects.

So, Wednesday: the Federal Reserve decides at three p.m. Brazil time, with updated projections. Futures markets give a quarter-point hike an eighty-five percent probability — that would be the first Fed hike since July twenty twenty-three. Then, around six-thirty in the evening, Brazil's Copom decides, where the consensus calls for a cut from fourteen to thirteen and three-quarters percent.

There's a crack in that Fed consensus worth knowing about. While futures put the odds of a hike at eighty-five percent, a Reuters poll of economists has about seventy percent expecting the Fed to simply hold. That's an unusually wide gap. My take: the poll was conducted from the fourth to the ninth of September, so it closed before the hot US core inflation reading on the eleventh, which is what pushed the odds up in the first place. The futures market is the fresher read, but the poll shows just how thin the support is for a move riding on one data point.

Now, the part that matters for your exchange rate. There's no direct market for euros against reais — both currencies trade against the dollar, and your rate comes out of that multiplication. That means if the dollar strengthens equally on both ends, it cancels out of your cross. Euro against dollar falls, dollar against real rises, and the product barely moves. So the Fed's move barely reaches you. Only Copom acts one-sidedly.

In numbers: your rate advantage — Brazil over Europe — sits at eleven and a half points today and falls to eleven and a quarter after Wednesday, a drop of just twenty-five basis points. The dollar's advantage loses the full fifty points on the same day. The pressure on your side is half the headline number. What actually carries risk on Wednesday isn't the hike itself but the dot plot: if it signals two or three more moves, a single step turns into an announced cycle — and that would hit the real harder than the euro.

One thing worth not skimming past: today's rate is already stronger than every named forecast out there. Brazil's central-bank Focus survey has held at five point twenty for over ten weeks, BTG calls for five point four, Itaú for five point three. The market sits at five point one two. Only Goldman's twelve-month target of five even is lower. My read: the consensus is chasing the market, not the other way around, and oil is why.
