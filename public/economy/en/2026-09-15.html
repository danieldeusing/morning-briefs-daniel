Monday's Jump — and Who Actually Caused It

FX in detail — where we stand and why

Cross rate: EUR/BRL has no market of its own. Both currencies trade against the dollar, and your rate comes out of the multiplication — like a detour through a connecting station: you want to go from A to C, but every train runs via B. Formula: EUR/BRL = EUR/USD × USD/BRL.

Monday's brief had to go without a fix. Now it's in, and it's the month's biggest single-day jump: 5.9564, up from 5.9244 on Friday, +0.54%.[1] The reflex read would be "strong euro." Breaking it down says the opposite:

EUR/USD 1.1592 → 1.1551 (−0.35%)× USD/BRL 5.1108 → 5.1566 (+0.90%)= EUR/BRL +0.54%

The euro braked; the real carried it all. Both figures come from the same ECB fixing moment, so they're not measured at different times.[1] A real that loses 0.9% against the dollar on a day with no Brazilian news is doing what emerging-market currencies do ahead of binary events: it gets held more lightly, because nobody goes into a meeting with a full position when the outcome is 10% up for grabs.

Today it's partly reversing. USD/BRL stands at 5.1380 this morning (−0.10%), EUR/USD at 1.1535 (−0.12%).[3][4] Both legs are giving ground, so the cross rate falls twice over: 5.1380 × 1.1535 = 5.927. That's my calculation from two dated legs, not an official rate.

Cross-check ECB fix from Sep 14 (5.9564) against an independent quote from the same day (TradingEconomics, 5.94805): a gap of 0.140%, clearly under the 0.5% threshold.[1][2] What matters is where the cross-check comes from: recomputing the cross from two ECB legs only confirms your own division.

For your exchange, that means: Monday's fix was the second-best rate of the month — only Sep 1 (6.0255) was higher. Anyone who didn't exchange yesterday is sitting 0.5% worse off today, heading into a two-sided Wednesday. Not an argument to rush, but one to split the total: part today, the rest after the dot plot — tranches instead of a single moment, just as the Brazilian expat outlet recommends for this week.[13]

Tomorrow: What's Priced In — and What Isn't

Rate advantage (carry): the gap between Brazilian and European interest rates. Like a savings account next door paying multiples more: as long as the exchange rate doesn't run away, the difference is your profit. This gap is the reason global money holds reais at all.

Both central banks have been meeting since today; both decide tomorrow. Fed: decision at 14:00 ET (15:00 BRT), press conference 14:30 ET — one of the four meetings a year with projections and a dot plot.[8] Futures markets price around 90% for +25 basis points to 3.75–4.00%; the CME reading from Sep 12 stood at 85.5%.[9][10] Copom: decision on the evening of Sep 16, minutes on Sep 22; the Selic stands at 14.00% after four cuts since June, and B3 options put roughly 95% odds on a cut to 13.75%.[11][12]

The Rift I Won't Resolve One Brazilian source — Rio Times — reports that economists expect a hold at 3.50–3.75%.[11] That stands squarely against 85–90% hike pricing.[9][10] My read: not a market-versus-market dispute, but futures market versus economist surveys — surveys have days-long fieldwork windows and close before the most recent data. But: one camp being wrong doesn't automatically mean it's the survey camp. Treat the hike as likely, not certain.

Now the math that concerns you. Almost every commentary adds the two moves up to "50 basis points less carry." True — for the dollar carry. Yours is different:

Dollar: 14.00 − 3.625 = 10.375→ Wed: 13.75 − 3.875 = 9.875 (−50 bp)
Euro: 14.00 − 2.50 = 11.50→ Wed: 13.75 − 2.50 = 11.25 (−25 bp)

The reason for the halving: the ECB already made its move. On Sep 10 it unanimously raised the deposit rate by 25 basis points to 2.50% (main refinancing rate 2.65%); Lagarde called it "a no brainer."[14] The Fed's move also largely cancels itself out, because a stronger dollar pushes down EUR/USD and lifts USD/BRL.

Dot plot: four times a year, each FOMC member anonymously marks a dot for where they see the policy rate heading — like a class writing down their guesses and the teacher pinning them all up at once. Tomorrow is one of those dates.

So the variance doesn't come from the decision but from the sheet next to it. A single insurance-style move is a different thing from an announced cycle of two or three more — and a cycle hits the real harder than the euro, because high-yield emerging-market currencies react disproportionately to US rate shocks. Second source of variance: Copom's wording that same evening, which has to react to a north that just turned hawkish even as it cuts itself.

Terms of trade: the price ratio between what a country sells and what it buys — like a market stall where the apples get pricier while the flour stays the same price. When oil rises, Brazil earns more dollars per ton exported, and those dollars have to be converted into reais: real demand for reais, independent of any rate decision.

And this is exactly where the consensus breaks. Itaú built its depreciation call on Jun 26 on three points; one of them, verbatim, was "deterioration in terms of trade, driven by falling oil prices."[19] BTG raised its year-end target on Jul 8 from 4.90 to 5.40 — on the same oil assumption.[20] Brent trades today at $107.26.[15] For a net oil exporter, that argument now points the other way. My position: neither target is wrong, but a third of its reasoning is — and neither house has updated it since. The only anchor whose thesis survives the oil shock unscathed is BofA's ("high commodity prices plus carry")[21] — dated Mar 26, 2026, actually the oldest of the three calls, predating both Itaú's and BTG's later revisions. That's not the advantage of a fresher read: BofA's logic never hinged on falling oil prices in the first place, but on high commodity prices generally plus carry — which is why it stays intact regardless of the oil regime.

A second signal pointing the same way: the Focus dollar has held at R$5.20 for 13 weeks, while the market sits at 5.138.[16][17] A median that hasn't budged an inch in a quarter isn't a judgment anymore — it's a habit.

Positioning & Brazilian Rates: More Gap Than Signal

More honest than a guess: this section is nearly empty today. No BCB swap book, no CFTC positioning in BRL futures, no NTN-B real yield, no EWZ flows, no dated DI close — the research budget went into the two central banks, the oil shock, and the five forecast tiers. I'm not inventing DI tenor points.

The 5-year CDS — the price of default insurance on Brazilian government bonds, essentially the thermometer for country risk — stands at 111.79 bp from Sep 14, 2026 (+0.46% day over day).[24] Also worth logging: the automatic source ladder for the DXY failed; the 99.44 figure is hand-picked from two independent sites that agree within 0.06%.[5][6]

It's Tuesday, September fifteenth. Both central banks shaping this week are now in session — the American Federal Reserve and Brazil's Copom. Nothing gets published today. Tomorrow both decide, on the very same day.

First, the number Monday's brief didn't have yet. The European Central Bank's reference rate for yesterday is in: five point nine five six four. That's up zero point five four percent from Friday — the biggest single-day jump this month. This morning the market is giving back part of that: dividing today's dollar rate in Brazil by today's euro-dollar rate puts the cross at about five point nine two seven.

What matters more than the jump is who caused it. The reflex read would be "strong euro." Wrong. The euro actually fell against the dollar by zero point three five percent — it braked. The Brazilian real lost zero point nine percent, and that's the entire move. A real that gives up that much on a day with no Brazilian news is doing exactly what emerging-market currencies do ahead of a binary event: it gets held more lightly, because nobody walks into a central-bank decision with a full position when the outcome is ten percent up for grabs.

So, tomorrow. The Fed decides at three p.m. Brazil time, with fresh projections and the dot plot; Powell speaks half an hour later. Futures markets put the odds of a quarter-point hike at around ninety percent. Copom decides that evening, where options on Brazil's exchange give a cut from fourteen to thirteen and three-quarters percent about a ninety-five percent chance.

There's a contradiction here I can't resolve, and you should know about it. One Brazilian outlet reports that economists expect the Fed to simply hold. That stands flatly against the ninety percent priced into futures. My read: this isn't market against market, it's futures against economist surveys — and surveys close their fieldwork before the newest data comes in. But I couldn't verify that cleanly. Treat the hike as likely, not certain.

Now, the math that actually matters for your exchange. Almost every commentary adds the two moves together into fifty basis points less carry. That's true for the dollar. Yours is different, because the European Central Bank already made its move on September tenth — deposit rate to two and a half percent, unanimous, a "no brainer" in Lagarde's words. Your advantage falls from eleven and a half points to eleven and a quarter. Twenty-five basis points, not fifty. The pressure on your side is half of what the headline number suggests.

But the real story today is oil. Brent is at a hundred seven dollars and twenty-six cents, a four-month high. Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, which bypasses the Strait of Hormuz, remains shut after drone strikes, and a planned meeting between Iran and the Gulf states was abruptly called off. That single price is driving both of tomorrow's decisions at once: it's keeping American inflation hot, and it's filling Brazil's trade balance.

And this is exactly where the forecast consensus breaks down. The two best-known depreciation targets for the real — from Itaú in late June and BTG in early July — were built explicitly on falling oil prices. Brazil is a net oil exporter. Today that argument points the other way, and neither bank has updated its call since. The one target whose reasoning survives the oil shock intact is Bank of America's, because it rests on high commodity prices and carry. Fitting with that: Brazil's central-bank survey median has held at five reais twenty per dollar for thirteen weeks straight, while the market sits at five point one three eight. A median that hasn't moved a millimeter in a quarter isn't a judgment anymore — it's a habit.

On my ranges — and every probability here is my own confidence, not what the market is pricing — today: five point nine zero to five point nine seven. This week: five point eight six to six point zero two. The next thirty days, which include the first election round on October fourth: five point eight six to six point two two. Three to six months: five point eight eight to six point four two. And twelve months, a wide five point eight two to six point five five, most likely six point zero eight.

In practice: yesterday's fix was the second-best rate this month, and today you're about half a percent below it. That's not a reason to rush — it's a reason to split the total, part now, the rest after the dot plot. Tomorrow morning this brief looks at exactly that: not the two decisions already priced in, but the dots next to them, and the wording out of Brasília.
