The Dollar Cancels Itself Out

Why the biggest day on the central-bank calendar is the smaller one for your rate

Cross rate: EUR against BRL has no trading venue of its own. Both currencies trade against the dollar, and your rate comes out of the multiplication — like a train route with no direct connection: you want to go from A to C, but every train runs via B. Formula: EUR/BRL = EUR/USD × USD/BRL.

This formula implies something today's headlines don't give away: a pure dollar shock largely cancels itself out in your rate. If the dollar strengthens against everything, EUR/USD falls and USD/BRL rises — in the product, little is left over.[1]

Fed hikes hawkishly→ Dollar globally stronger→ EUR/USD −0.8%+ USD/BRL +1.2%= EUR/BRL +0.4%

The remaining 0.4 points are the difference between the two sensitivities: the real reacts more sharply to a dollar shock than the euro, because it carries an emerging-market risk premium. That's the entire channel through which the Fed reaches you today — everything else tonight is either real news or euro news.

The month shows this in real numbers. EUR/BRL fell from 6.0402 (Aug 17) to 5.9440 (Sep 15), i.e. −1.59%. Broken down: USD/BRL −1.13% (5.2102 → 5.1512), EUR/USD −0.47% (1.1593 → 1.1539).[2][3] Two-thirds of the move against you came from the firmer real. On Tuesday itself it was roughly even: −0.11% on the real leg, −0.10% on the euro leg.

And here's the uncomfortable observation: Tuesday was a day with a rising dollar — DXY +0.11%, its fifth straight up session — and the real strengthened along with it anyway.[5][6] The reason lies in the oil price: Brent at $107.46, Petrobras +3.09% as the main support for an Ibovespa up 0.54%.[4][25] Brazil is a net oil exporter; an oil shock that forces the Fed and ECB to hike improves Brazil's trade balance. That force is currently partly canceling out the usual emerging-market reaction — and you underestimate it if you only look at the rate advantage.

Tonight: What's Priced In — and What Isn't

Dot plot: four times a year, each Fed member anonymously plots a dot on a grid: where they see the policy rate by the end of this year, the end of next year, and longer term. Like a class survey where everyone writes down how warm they think December will be — the answer for today is already known, what's interesting is the spread for later. Today's meeting is one of those four.

Fed, 15:00 BRT. Decision plus projections and dot plot, press conference with Chair Kevin Warsh at 15:30 BRT.[8] Three independent pricing sources show the same picture: CME FedWatch 92%, prediction markets 87.3%, futures market 93% for +25 basis points to 3.75–4.00%.[5][9][10] It would be the first hike since 2023.[10]

With a decision priced in nine-tenths of the way, it's not the decision that moves the rate but everything around it. Warsh has announced a deliberately quieter Fed, going as far as floating the idea of no longer facing the press after every meeting.[11] A chair who says less shifts the weight onto the paper: read today's dot plot, not the press conference. The futures market already sees two more quarter-point moves by year-end; Danske expects +25 today plus another +25 each in December and March.[10][7]

Copom, in the evening (usual publication slot ~18:30 BRT — no separately confirmed time for September).[12] The Selic stands at 14.00%, with a cut of −25 basis points to 13.75% expected at roughly 96% probability.[13] Itaú (Diogo Guillen) expects a unanimous decision with deliberately ambiguous language and no path commitment; Warren and AW Capital expect the same cut and 13.75% by year-end.[13] Minutes on Sep 22.[12]

Rate advantage (carry): the gap between Brazilian and European interest rates. Like a savings account next door paying multiples more: as long as the exchange rate doesn't run away, the difference is your profit. This gap is the whole reason global money holds reais in the first place — and when it shrinks, the real loses its most important support.

For the dollar-real gap, today is a bad day: Fed +25 and Copom −25 squeeze it by 50 basis points within a few hours. Your euro-real gap loses only 25 — from 11.50 to 11.25 points — because the ECB already made its move on Sep 10, and its deposit rate of 2.50% takes effect today of all days.[14][15]

In practice Today's ECB fix is measured around 09:15 BRT and only published afterward — hours before both the Fed and Copom. What you see today as the "rate" contains nothing from today yet. The reaction will show up for the first time in Thursday's fix.

It's Wednesday, September sixteenth. In Brazil, today is called Superquarta — Super Wednesday. Two central banks decide within three and a half hours of each other, and they're headed in opposite directions. The Federal Reserve is likely to hike for the first time since twenty twenty-three. Brazil's Copom, in Brasília, is cutting.

Your starting point is yesterday's European Central Bank reference rate: five point nine four four zero reais per euro, about a fifth of a percent below the day before. Looking at the month, you're in the bottom third: it ran from six point zero seven on August twentieth down to five point eight nine on September third.

The most important takeaway today is a math rule, not a headline. Euro against real has no market of its own — your rate comes from two dollar rates multiplied together. That's why a pure dollar shock mostly cancels itself out in your number: when the dollar strengthens, one leg falls while the other rises. So whatever hangs on tonight's Fed decision is smaller than the headlines suggest.

You can see it clearly over the month. Your rate fell one point six percent. One point one points of that came from a firmer real, only zero point five points from a weaker euro. So two-thirds of the move against you is Brazilian, not European.

And yesterday something rare happened. The dollar got stronger worldwide — the dollar index rose for a fifth straight session — and the real strengthened right along with it. The reason is oil. Brent is trading near a hundred seven dollars, close to a four-month high, because a Saudi pipeline is shut down and Iranian blockades are choking the Persian Gulf. Brazil is a net oil exporter. The same oil shock that's forcing the Fed and the European Central Bank to hike is improving Brazil's trade balance.

On the decisions themselves: three independent pricing sources put the odds of a quarter-point American hike between eighty-seven and ninety-three percent. For Brazil's cut to thirteen point seven five percent, the market sees roughly ninety-six percent odds. So both are already priced in. What will actually move your rate is what sits alongside them — the Fed's dot plot and Copom's exact wording. Worth noting: the new Fed chair, Kevin Warsh, has signaled a deliberately quieter central bank. A chair who says less shifts the weight onto the paperwork.

One practical thing that's easy to miss: today's European Central Bank reference rate is measured around a quarter past nine, Brazil time — hours before either decision. So what you see today as the rate contains nothing from today yet. The reaction won't show up until Thursday's fix. Here's the good news: your personal rate gap between Europe and Brazil only loses twenty-five basis points today, not fifty, because the European Central Bank already made its move back on September tenth.

Now for the forecast. For today, I expect five point nine two to five point nine nine, most likely five point nine five. For this week, slightly higher, five point nine one to six point zero three. For the next thirty days, upward with a premium, five point nine to six point two — that window includes the first election round on October fourth, and every late poll shows a statistical dead heat in the runoff. Over three to six months, I expect a pullback after the election peak. Over twelve months, a wide, low-direction range from five point seven two to six point three eight, most likely six point zero zero.

Important caveat: not a single institution publishes a forecast for euro against real. They all forecast dollar against real; every number I give you is my own multiplication with a euro-dollar assumption. And the percentages are my confidence, not market prices. In practical terms, that means the window through mid-October is, in my assessment, probably your best exchange window of the next twelve months, because all three forces that push the rate down afterward only kick in after the election.
