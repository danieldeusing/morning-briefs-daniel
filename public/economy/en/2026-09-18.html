Where We Stand — and Why

The last 72 hours, broken down mechanically

Cross rate: EUR/BRL isn't traded directly anywhere — the rate comes from two prices: what a euro costs in dollars (EUR/USD) and what a dollar costs in reais (USD/BRL). Like a connecting flight: euro → dollar → real. So your rate can fall because the euro is weak, or because the real is strong — or, as right now, because both are happening at once.

On Wednesday, September 16, the Fed raised its target range by 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00% — unanimously, and for the first time since 2023. By the textbook, that should have strengthened the dollar and weakened the real. Thursday's ECB fix shows what actually happened, and the arithmetic checks out exactly:

Fed +25 bp, hawkish dot plot→ EUR/USD 1.1537 → 1.1481 (−0.49%)→ USD/BRL 5.1459 → 5.1307 (−0.30%)→ EUR/BRL 5.9368 → 5.8905 (−0.78%)

So the dollar gained against the euro while losing against the real at the same time: both legs moving in the same direction against you. A pure dollar shock mostly cancels out in the cross rate — a euro problem plus real strength adds up instead.

Over 30 trading days it's the same picture, just bigger: EUR/BRL fell from 6.0402 (Aug 17) to 5.8905, i.e. −2.48%. Of that, the real accounts for 1.53 percentage points (USD/BRL 5.2102 → 5.1307) and the euro for 0.97 percentage points (EUR/USD 1.1593 → 1.1481). Over twelve months, TradingEconomics puts it at −6.15%.

My reading, against the reflex: this isn't a dollar story. The dollar index gave up 1.39% over four weeks — the dollar got globally weaker — and the euro still lost almost a full percent against it in the same window. If the basket falls and your line item in it loses ground anyway, your line item is the problem. The ECB hiked on Sep 10, and its deposit rate has stood at 2.50% since Sep 16 — the euro has kept none of that gain: shortly after the decision EUR/USD was around 1.1610, now it's at 1.1481. ING called it a "dovish hike" in advance, pointing to the pressure on French government bonds ahead of budget season. So far, ING has been right.

Three Central Banks, Two Directions

Fed up, ECB up, Copom down — within 48 hours

[US] The Fed hiked unanimously to 3.75–4.00% on Sep 16. The dot plot signals one more hike this year; Danske Bank calls the dots "modestly hawkish." Chair Warsh still describes policy as growth-supportive nonetheless — meaning the Fed is hiking because inflation forces it to, not because it wants to slow things down.

Dot plot: each member of the Fed's board anonymously plots a dot showing where they see the policy rate in the coming years. Like a class voting by anonymous ballot — you see the mood, not who wrote what. If the dots come in higher than expected, the market immediately prices in more hikes and the dollar rises.

[BR] That same evening, Copom cut the Selic by 25 basis points to 13.75% — the fifth straight cut. Backing comes from the IPCA: −0.32% month over month for August, genuine deflation, and +4.22% over twelve months (July: 4.44%). The central bank left its next steps open, speaking of "serenidade e cautela" — calm and caution.

[EU] The ECB followed on Sep 10: deposit rate 2.50%, main refinancing rate 2.65%, marginal lending rate 2.90%, effective since Sep 16. The reason is August inflation of 3.3% — the highest in three years, well above the 2% target.

Why the real is gaining despite the cut: because the rate advantage stays absurdly large even after five cuts. Selic at 13.75% minus IPCA at 4.22% is roughly 9 percentage points of real interest; using the Focus survey's expected inflation (4.90% for 2026) instead, that's still 8.4 points. Another 25 basis points off doesn't change that order of magnitude — it's a rounding error against the yield hunger pulling capital into Brazil.

Carry and real interest: real interest is the rate after subtracting inflation — what actually leaves you with more purchasing power. Like a savings account paying 10% while everything in the store gets 4% pricier: you're left with 6%. A "carry trade" means borrowing where money is cheap (the eurozone, 2.50%) and investing where it pays well (Brazil, 13.75%). As long as the real doesn't collapse, the gap is your profit — and that exact money is what's propping up the real.

Positioning, Sentiment & Brazilian Rates

Thin today — and that's flagged here as such

DI curve and NTN-B: DI futures are bets on where Brazil's overnight rate will stand on a given date — like a betting slip on the rate in January 2029. The NTN-B is a government bond that strips out inflation: you get IPCA plus X%. That X is the real interest rate the market demands from the government — the higher it climbs, the less the market trusts the budget.

Three signals, all more than a week old and flagged accordingly: the DI curve closed on Aug 27 with DI1F27 at 13.630% and DI1F29 at 14.075% — the long end roughly 45 basis points above the short end, even as Copom is cutting. The market there isn't pricing cuts, it's pricing fiscal and duration risk. The NTN-B 2035 yielded 7.68% real on Sep 9 (price R$4,336.32). Brazil's 5-year CDS — the insurance premium against a sovereign default — was last verified at 126.27–126.90 basis points, as of week 28/2026 (July).

Not available today, so not estimated: CFTC positioning in BRL futures, the BCB's FX swap book, EWZ flows, today's DI close. The forecast above accordingly rests on spot, policy, and the calendar — not on positioning.

It's Friday, September eighteenth. The euro against the Brazilian real just hit its lowest point of the month, and that's the story costing you money today. Thursday's European Central Bank reference rate put EUR against BRL at five point eight nine zero five — the lowest reading in the last twenty-four trading days.

Here's the twist: this week was supposed to go the other way. On Wednesday, the Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate for the first time since twenty twenty-three, to a range of three point seven five to four percent, unanimously. That same evening, Brazil's central bank cut its own rate for the fifth straight time, to thirteen point seven five percent. Higher rates in the US, lower ones in Brazil — textbook says the real should have lost ground. It gained instead.

The math behind it adds up exactly, and it explains your problem. The euro lost almost half a percent against the dollar. The dollar, at the same time, lost three tenths of a percent against the real. Both moves point the same way against you — together, almost eight tenths of a percent in a single day. Over thirty trading days, that's two and a half percent, and of that, the strong real accounts for about one and a half points, the weak euro about one point.

My take, against the obvious read: this isn't a dollar story. The dollar index — the dollar against a basket of major currencies — fell one point four percent over four weeks. So the dollar got weaker globally, and the euro still lost ground against it. When the whole basket falls and your line item in it keeps losing, your line item is the problem. The European Central Bank hiked on September tenth, its deposit rate sits at two point five percent, and the euro has kept none of that gain. ING called it a dovish hike in advance, pointing to pressure on French government bonds. So far, ING's been right.

Why the real is shrugging off five rate cuts: because the interest-rate advantage stays enormous. Thirteen point seven five percent against inflation of four point two two percent is roughly nine percentage points of real return, after inflation. Using expected inflation instead, it's still eight point four. A quarter point less is a rounding error against a gap that size.

The real event of the month is the election on October fourth, sixteen days away. Real Time Big Data has Lula at thirty-eight percent and Flávio Bolsonaro at thirty. In a head-to-head runoff simulation, both sit at forty-four — a dead heat within the margin of error. The market isn't trading this as politics, but as fiscal policy: the real gained ground when polls showed Flávio ahead, because investors see him as fiscally conservative. Asset manager Kinea warns, though, that the race is closer than the daily poll tracker suggests, because non-voters aren't neutral.

On the bank forecasts, the fine print matters. The Brazilian central bank's official consensus survey, from Monday, puts the dollar at five point two zero reais by year-end — but it closed on September eleventh, before either the Fed hike or the Copom cut. Two other houses are worse off: Itaú saw five point three zero back in June, assuming the last rate cut would land in August. That assumption died on September sixteenth. And Bank of America, in March, modeled a Brazilian policy rate of eleven point seven five percent by the end of twenty twenty-six; it's actually thirteen point seven five. Both forecasts call for a weaker real, and both rest on rate assumptions reality has missed in the opposite direction.

Here's my read for you, and the odds are my own confidence, not a market price: today, sideways and weak, between five point eight six and five point nine two, at seventy percent. This week, five point eight four to five point nine six. Over thirty days, a wide five point seven eight to six point zero zero, because the election isn't a data point, it's a switch. Over three to six months, five point eight five to six point one five. Over twelve months, five point eight zero to six point three five, most likely six point zero five.

In practice, that means: if you don't have to sell euros today, don't. The counterargument I respect: if October fourth goes market-friendly, five point seven five becomes the next stop, and waiting gets more expensive, not cheaper. If you need reais in October, split the amount and convert part of it before the election.
