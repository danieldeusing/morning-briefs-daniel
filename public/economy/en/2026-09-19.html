More important than the low itself is who caused it. For four weeks it was mostly the strong real. This week it was the weak euro alone: the real actually lost ground against the dollar too — just less than the euro did.

Where We Stand — and Who Pushed This Time

The week, broken down mechanically

Cross rate: EUR/BRL isn't traded directly anywhere. The rate comes from two prices: what a euro costs in dollars (EUR/USD) and what a dollar costs in reais (USD/BRL). Like a connecting flight: euro → dollar → real. So your rate can fall because the euro is weak, or because the real is strong — and you need to know which of the two is happening right now, because the two effects hold up for completely different lengths of time.

This week it was clearly the former. Between Sep 11 and Sep 18, EUR/BRL fell 0.65%, from 5.9244 to 5.8857. Broken into its two legs:

Fed hikes to 3.75–4.00% on Sep 16→ Dollar broadly stronger→ EUR/USD 1.1592 → 1.1460 (−1.14%)→ USD/BRL 5.1108 → 5.1359 (+0.49%)→ EUR/BRL −0.65%

Read the chain carefully: the real also lost ground this week — about half a percentage point against the dollar. If that had been the only thing happening, your rate would have risen and the week would have been good for you. It fell only because the euro gave up ground twice as fast. The check adds up exactly: 1.1460 × 5.1359 = 5.8857, and 1.1592 × 5.1108 = 5.9244.

That's a regime change from the prior weeks. Over the full month (Aug 17 → Sep 18, −2.56%), both legs split the work almost evenly: roughly 1.43 points of real strength (USD/BRL 5.2102 → 5.1359) against 1.15 points of euro weakness (EUR/USD 1.1593 → 1.1460). This past week, the real leg didn't just stop helping — it actually worked slightly against you.

Why this matters in practice: real strength is a Brazilian story — carry, the election, capital inflows — and that can flip abruptly after Oct 4. Euro weakness against a dollar whose central bank is now projecting a full year without a single cut is a much stickier story. A rate that comes from euro weakness comes back more slowly than one that comes from real strength.

Correction to the Sep 18 brief That brief said the dollar index had "given up 1.39% over four weeks." The sign was wrong: TradingEconomics shows +1.40% for the same period, and the cross-check confirms the gain — EUR/USD fell 1.15% in the same window, and the euro makes up 57.6% of the basket. That also partly overturns the thesis built on it: it's both — the dollar got stronger, and the euro additionally lost ground within the basket.

Three Central Banks, One Winner

Fed up, ECB up, Copom down — and the dollar takes it all

[US] The Fed hiked by 25 basis points on Wed Sep 16 to a target range of 3.75–4.00% — the first US hike since 2023, the vote unanimous. More interesting than the decision is what the members wrote down for afterward. From the Fed's own projection table (not press summaries): the median policy rate stands at 4.1% for end-2026 and again 4.1% for end-2027. In June it read 3.8 and 3.6.

Projection table / dot plot: Four times a year, each of the Fed's 18 members anonymously writes down where they see the policy rate in the coming years — like a class voting by secret ballot: you see the mood, not the person. The higher the dots sit, the more expensive it gets to borrow dollars, and the more attractive it gets to hold them.

Two readings, both working against your rate. First: the 2026 range runs from 3.9 to 4.4%, and the central tendency starts at 4.1 — so at most three of the 18 members see no further hike at all (press reports cite 16 of 18, which fits). Second, the real news: 4.1% for both years means the Fed is projecting not a single cut for 2027. The reason is in the same table: median PCE inflation of 3.7% for 2026, core rate 3.4% — well above the 2% target.

[EU] The ECB also hiked, and that too is now confirmed at the primary source: deposit rate 2.50%, main refinancing rate 2.65%, marginal lending rate 2.90% — effective since Sep 16, 2026, up from 2.25 / 2.40 / 2.65 since Jun 17. The trigger is eurozone inflation of 3.3% in August, the highest in three years. And the euro is losing ground anyway. The reason is simple: 2.50 against 3.875 is roughly a 140-basis-point rate disadvantage, and the gap widens if the Fed hikes once more and the ECB follows more slowly.

[BR] On that same Wednesday, Copom cut the Selic unanimously by 25 basis points to 13.75% — the fifth straight cut, 1.25 points in total since March, when the central bank opened its "calibration cycle." 75 of 78 surveyed institutions had expected exactly that; Copom isn't committing to how far the cycle will ultimately go.

Why the real shrugs this off: because 25 basis points against Brazil's rate advantage is a rounding error. Selic at 13.75% against BTG's inflation forecast of 5.2% for 2026 is roughly 8.5 percentage points of real interest — interest after subtracting price increases, i.e., what's actually left in purchasing power. The headwind comes from elsewhere: the Bank of Japan hiked to 1.25%, a 31-year high. Cheap yen has for years been the seed capital for emerging-market carry trades; as it gets more expensive, the pool feeding the real shrinks.

October 4 — and the Curve That Decoupled

Why polls are effectively rate data right now

[BR] In 15 days, Brazil votes in round 1, with a possible runoff following on Oct 25. The current Datafolha poll shows a technical tie between President Lula (PT) and Senator Flávio Bolsonaro (PL-RJ). The August polls that were widely cited until recently are now about three weeks old and had Lula ahead by four to eight points — the race has tightened.

What came of that on Friday is unusual enough to deserve its own paragraph: Brazilian rates fell against the global grain. DI1F27 held steady at 13.555%, DI1F29 eased from 13.845 to 13.840%, DI1F31 from 14.061 to 14.020%; over the week the futures curve lost 1 to 3 basis points at the main vertices. At the same time, US yields rose: per O Povo's report on the same session, the 2-year T-note went from 4.683 to 4.743% and the 10-year from 4.937 to 4.993%. The direction is well corroborated, but the decimals are that one report's intraday snapshot — other sources show slightly different Friday levels (roughly 4.94 to 5.04% on the 10-year), and the US Treasury's own official daily fix couldn't be confirmed for this date. Citi's Iván Riveros expects the DIs to keep decoupling from the Fed's more hawkish stance — as they've done all September.

DI futures: Bets on where Brazil's overnight rate will stand on a given date — DI1F31 is the betting slip for January 2031. Like a weather forecast for interest rates: the further out the date, the more the number is uncertainty premium rather than prediction. That the long end (14.02%) sits above the short end (13.56%), even as Copom is cutting, is exactly that premium — the market demands more for long maturities because it doesn't trust Brazil's budget over ten years. When the long end falls, that distrust is easing.

My reading: Brazil's rate market isn't currently trading the Fed, it's trading the election outcome — and pricing the more fiscally friendly variant. A strong but fragile signal: this is positioning, not a fundamental data point, and if Oct 4 doesn't confirm it, it reverses in a single session. How thin the ice is showed up in that same session: the public prosecutor's office at the audit court requested the suspension of a 15% benefits increase.

The central bank is visibly leaning against this. On Sep 15 it sold $1 billion in the spot market plus 20,000 reverse-swap contracts (another $1 billion) against negative FX flow in August and early September. On Sep 18 it placed all 50,000 swap contracts on offer, rolling over $2.5 billion maturing Oct 1 — 38,000 into January 2027, 12,000 into December 2026. The same amount already went through once on Sep 14.

BCB FX swap: Not a dollar sale out of reserves, but a promise — the central bank pays the market the exchange-rate move and collects the interest rate in return. Like an insurance policy the state sells instead of delivering goods: the same effect as a dollar sale, without reserves shrinking. A rollover just extends an expiring policy — that stabilizes, but it isn't a new intervention.

It's Saturday, September nineteenth. The currency markets are closed, so there's no new rate today, and the number you're heading into the weekend with is Friday's European Central Bank reference rate: euro against the Brazilian real at five point eight eight five seven. That's the lowest reading of the period, lower than yesterday's month low.

But the real news isn't the new low — it's who's responsible for it. For four weeks running, it was mostly a strong Brazilian real. This week, it was the weak euro alone. And that distinction matters for your money.

Here's the arithmetic. Between September eleventh and eighteenth, your rate fell zero point six five percent. The euro lost one point one four percent against the dollar. The real, at the same time, also lost ground against the dollar — zero point four nine percent. Read that again: the real lost ground this week too. If that had been the only thing happening, your rate would have risen and the week would have been good for you. It fell only because the euro gave up ground twice as fast.

Here's why that matters in practice: real strength is a Brazilian story, and it can flip abruptly after the election on October fourth. Euro weakness against a dollar whose central bank has just written down a full year without a single rate cut is a much stickier story. A rate driven by a weak euro comes back more slowly than one driven by a strong real.

Which brings us to the Fed. For this brief I read the Fed's own projection table, not the press summaries. It says the median policy rate sits at four point one percent by the end of twenty twenty-six, and again four point one percent by the end of twenty twenty-seven. Back in June those numbers were three point eight and three point six. In plain terms: the Fed is projecting not a single cut for all of twenty twenty-seven. The reason is in the same table — inflation is expected at three point seven percent this year, well above the two percent target.

The European Central Bank hiked too — its deposit rate has stood at two point five percent since September sixteenth. And the euro is losing ground anyway, because the gap to the dollar is roughly one hundred forty basis points and it grows if the Fed hikes again. In Brazil, Copom cut on that same Wednesday for the fifth straight time, to thirteen point seven five percent. The real shrugs that off, because after subtracting expected inflation there's still roughly eight and a half percentage points of real return left over.

I also need to correct a number from yesterday's brief. It said the dollar index had given up one point four percent over four weeks. The sign was wrong — it actually rose one point four percent. The cross-check confirms it: the euro makes up more than half of that basket and fell in the same window. So the conclusion changes partly — it isn't just a weak euro, the dollar has genuinely gotten stronger too.

Something unusual happened in Brazil on Friday: Brazilian interest rates fell while American yields rose. The betting slip on Brazil's rate in January twenty thirty-one went from fourteen point zero six one down to fourteen point zero two zero percent, while the ten-year US yield climbed to four point nine nine three. The reason is the election: a new Datafolha poll shows a technical tie between Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro, and the market is positioning for the more fiscally friendly outcome. That's a strong signal, but a fragile one — it's positioning, not a fundamental data point. The central bank is backing the currency too, selling a billion dollars in the spot market on September fifteenth and rolling over two and a half billion dollars in swaps on the eighteenth.

Here's my call, and remember, the probabilities are my own confidence, not a market number. Nothing can move today; I see Monday's open between five point eight six and five point nine two. This week, five point eight two to five point nine five, driven by Wednesday's purchasing managers' surveys, since there isn't a single Brazilian date on the calendar this week. Over thirty days, five point seven five to six point zero five, with two peaks, because the election isn't a data point, it's a switch. Over three to six months, five point eight zero to six point one five. Over twelve months, five point eight zero to six point four zero, most likely six point zero five.

One more thing worth noting on the twelve-month view: the banks largely agree on the real and are almost maximally split on the euro. Goldman Sachs sees euro-dollar at one point one two in twelve months; SEB sees one point two three by the end of twenty twenty-seven. Multiply either by the same real forecast and you get five point nine one against six point four nine — a ten percent difference from a single variable. My lean is toward the lower half, because the Fed's own projection leaves the euro no argument through the end of twenty twenty-seven.

In practice: nobody who doesn't have to should convert euros this weekend.
