The Anchor — This Time With a Real Cross-Check

What a consistency check is worth depends on where the second number comes from

Yesterday's brief ran a control calculation that checked out — and still proved nothing: USD/BRL 5.1359 times EUR/USD 1.1460 gives 5.8857, a deviation of 0.001%. But the ECB only publishes euro-against-X rates; the USD/BRL figure was itself computed from two ECB numbers, so the identity was guaranteed to hold. It rules out a data-entry error, nothing more.

Today the real version is available. The Rio Times puts Friday's close on the Brazilian market at a dollar rate of 5.1445 reais (+0.30% on the day) — a non-ECB source, measured at a different place and a different time:

Market USD/BRL 5.1445× EUR/USD 1.1460= 5.8956vs. ECB fix 5.8857 → +0.168%

0.168% is clearly below the 0.5% threshold above which I'd flag a discrepancy as a problem. And the direction fits the explanation: the ECB fix is pulled around 14:15 CEST, and the Brazilian market keeps trading for hours after that — and the real gave up ground in exactly those hours. Your rate is better documented today than it was yesterday, then, not because it moved, but because a second, independent measurement confirms it.

Cross rate: EUR/BRL isn't traded directly anywhere. The rate comes from two prices — what a euro costs in dollars and what a dollar costs in reais. Like a connecting flight: euro → dollar → real. So your rate can fall because the euro is weak or because the real is strong — two stories with completely different half-lives.

The breakdown itself doesn't change: of last week's −0.65%, −1.14% came from the euro leg (EUR/USD 1.1592 → 1.1460) and +0.49% from real weakness (USD/BRL 5.1108 → 5.1359). So the real also lost ground last week — just half as fast as the euro. That remains the most important observation of the weekend, because it marks the change in driver: for four weeks the strong real pushed your rate down, this week it was the weak euro.

The Week Turns Brazilian

Correction to yesterday's brief — three BR dates, not zero

Correction to the Sep 19 brief It said: "There isn't a single Brazilian date on the entire week's calendar." That was a wrong conclusion drawn from a Europe-skewed calendar. The Brazilian weekly outlook lists three: the Focus bulletin Monday at 08:30 BRT, the Copom minutes Tuesday at 08:00 BRT, IPCA-15 Friday at 09:00 BRT. That shifts the weighting of the weekly forecast — details in scenario 2 below.

Monday, 08:30 BRT — Focus. Every Monday the Brazilian central bank surveys around a hundred institutions for their forecasts and publishes the medians. Like a weekly weather report that every meteorologist co-signs. Monday's Focus is the first one after the triple Wednesday: Fed +25bp, ECB +25bp, Copom −25bp, all in a single day. The interesting figure is the year-end dollar forecast. It has sat unchanged at R$5.20 for thirteen weeks, while spot trades at 5.14. If it moves, that's the first consensus reaction to the new rate world.

Tuesday, 08:00 BRT — Copom minutes. The reasoning behind the cut to 13.75%. What matters isn't the decision but the language: if the committee keeps saying "calibration" instead of "easing cycle," the carry advantage stays intact longer than the rate curve assumes.

Friday, 09:00 BRT — IPCA-15. The preliminary estimate of Brazilian consumer prices, consensus 4.5% (the source doesn't specify whether that's a 12-month or monthly rate; 4.5 reads unambiguously as an annual rate). There's a tension here I think is underrated: Focus sees full-year 2026 inflation at 4.90%, BTG even higher at 5.2%. If Friday comes in at 4.5%, current inflation would sit below what the same consensus expects for December — the market is assuming an acceleration in the fourth quarter. That's exactly why Copom is cutting cautiously, in 25bp steps rather than 50.

Abroad: Wednesday brings the flash PMIs for Germany, the eurozone, the UK, and the US — the week's biggest scheduled euro catalyst. Thursday: the SNB (hold at 0.00% expected), Banxico at 16:00 BRT (prior 6.5%), and a Trump–Xi meeting at the White House. Friday: US PCE for August, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, consensus around 2.7% (core 2.9%) — a core rate well above the 2% target is exactly the reading that carried the Sep 16 hike.

Fourteen Days — and a Coin Flip

What the polls actually say, and what the market makes of it

[BR] Datafolha surveyed 2,002 people on Sep 15 and 16. Round 1 result: President Lula 39%, Senator Flávio Bolsonaro 36%. Head-to-head: 46 to 44. Three things about this matter more than the numbers themselves.

First: the runoff is practically certain. Combining 39 and 36 percent leaves 25 percent for all remaining candidates — nobody clears 50% in round 1. Oct 4 therefore decides nothing, it just sorts. That pushes the timing of the biggest rate move later, into the window between Oct 5 and Oct 25.

Second: the tie isn't new, but the tightening is. StoneX already cited the exact same 46 to 44 for the runoff on Sep 7 — the head-to-head has stood still for at least two weeks. What's moved is round 1: Quaest still saw 37 to 29 in late August (fieldwork Aug 30–Sep 1), an eight-point lead where three now stand. A caveat on the trend line — two different pollsters, two methodologies — but the direction is the same.

Third: two points isn't a lead. With around 2,000 respondents, a gap of 46 to 44 sits within what a sample of this size can even resolve. "Lula is ahead" isn't a valid reading; "open race" is the only one.

How the market translates that, StoneX puts so precisely in its weekly overview that I'll quote it rather than paraphrase: bullish for the real is "the strengthening of the perception of a more balanced electoral race, increasing the possibility of government alternation and more conservative fiscal management, [which] may reduce the risk premium of national assets and favor the Brazilian real." Bearish, on the other hand: "the persistence of doubts regarding the trajectory of US inflation may increase investors' bets on further interest rate hikes by the Fed, putting pressure on Treasury yields and globally strengthening the dollar."

Risk premium: The markup investors demand just to be invested in a given country at all. Like a deposit: the less sure you are you'll get your sweater back, the more collateral you want held against it. When the premium falls, Brazilian money gets cheaper — the real rises, without anything about rates or the economy having changed.

And the cross-check from the stock market: the Ibovespa closed Friday at 185,229 points, −0.41% on the day and −1.06% on the week — the first losing week after four winning ones. The weakness was broad: Vale −1.80%, Bradesco −1.44%, Nu Holdings −1.37%, Itaú Unibanco −1.09%; CSN lost 12.16% over five sessions. My reading: the election trade is losing momentum before the election has even happened — positioning unwinding as a precaution, not capitulation.

Bolsa Família — R$5.8 Billion Today, R$22 Billion From 2027

The number that counts sits in the budget after next

[BR] On Sep 17 — seventeen days before the election — the government raised Bolsa Família by 15.04%. The minimum payment rises from R$600 to R$691, the average amount from R$675 to R$777; payouts start with the October pay cycle. The justification is technically clean: 15.04% matches the accumulated INPC consumer price index since the program's 2023 relaunch.

The politically interesting figure is a different one. Per deputy finance minister Dario Durigan, the measure costs R$5.8 billion this year — but roughly R$22 billion in 2027. The 2026 figure covers one quarter, the 2027 figure a full year, and it lands in exactly the budget the next government will be responsible for. On Sep 18, Supreme Court justice Gilmar Mendes ruled the decree lawful — which takes off the table the legal challenge the market was still pricing as a risk on Friday (per several Brazilian outlets; no STF primary source was pulled).

Why this matters for your rate: not because of the amount — R$22 billion is rounding error against a debt stock above 80% of GDP. It's the pattern. BTG raised its debt projection in early September from 82.6 to 83.2% of GDP (2026) and from 86.8 to 87.6% (2027). A permanent social expenditure, decided during a campaign and confirmed by the courts, is exactly the kind of building block such revisions are made of — and the longer the horizon, the more the debt line outweighs the rate advantage.
