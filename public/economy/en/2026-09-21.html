September 16 — When Both Central Banks Pulled in Opposite Directions

The rate advantage shrank; the real got firmer anyway

Two things happened on a single Wednesday. The Fed raised its policy rate for the first time in over three years, by 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00%, and did so unanimously, 12 to 0. The statement's reasoning, verbatim: "Inflation remains elevated. Today's policy action will support a timelier return to the Committee's 2 percent goal."[1] The same day, Brazil's Copom cut the Selic by 25 basis points to 13.75%.[2]

Basis point (bp): one hundredth of a percentage point. 25 bp = 0.25%. Central banks talk in this unit because rate decisions almost always come in quarters — the way a baker talks in grams instead of kilos.
Carry (rate advantage): borrow money where it's cheap, invest it where rates are high. The spread is your profit — as long as the currency you're invested in doesn't crash. Brazil has been the target of this trade for years.

On paper, that's a pincer move against the real: before the meeting, the nominal advantage was 14.00 minus 3.75 = 10.25 points; after, 13.75 minus 4.00 = 9.75 points. Half a point gone, in one day. By the textbook, the real should have weakened.

Fed +25 bp+ Copom −25 bp→ Carry 10.25 → 9.75 pp→ Theory: BRL weaker≠ Reality: EUR/BRL at a monthly low

It didn't. Since Sep 16, the fixes have run 5.9368 → 5.8905 → 5.8857 — three days, three lower prints. There are three documented reasons for that.

First: the real (inflation-adjusted) advantage grew, it didn't shrink. Anyone collecting Selic at 13.75% while expecting 4.90% inflation holds 8.85 points of genuine rate advantage — and that 4.90% is down from 5.02% in the Aug 24 survey.[3][4] In the US, the Fed explicitly calls inflation "elevated." Nominally the gap narrowed; in real terms it widened.

Second: the Fed delivered less than was priced in. StoneX expected a range of 4.00–4.25% two days earlier.[5] What landed was 3.75–4.00%. Anyone positioned for the higher band had to give dollars back.

Third: the central bank stepped in. On Sep 15–16, the BCB sold $1 billion spot plus 20,000 reverse swap contracts worth another $1 billion (cutoff rate 4.9250, maturity Oct 1) — against an outflow that had exceeded inflows through August and early September.[6]

Reverse FX swap (swap cambial reverso): the central bank commits, via a forward contract, to buy dollars instead of selling them — it pulls dollar demand out of the market without touching real reserves. Like a shopkeeper who opens a waiting list during a rush instead of emptying the shelves.

The Oil Window — Today's Real Story

The price that forced both hikes has fallen for four straight sessions

Brent is trading today at $101.98 a barrel, −1.82% on the day — the fourth straight losing session. Trigger, per the market report: hopes that intensified diplomacy ends the Middle East conflict and restores the region's energy supply.[7] The year-over-year comparison stays brutal: +53.19% versus a year ago, +10.64% on the month.

Why this leads: the oil shock is the shared cause of both Sep 16 hikes. Without it, the Fed wouldn't have reversed course after three years, and the ECB wouldn't be sitting at a 2.50% deposit rate instead of 2.00%.[8]

Brent +53% y/y→ Energy drives inflation in the US + euro zone→ Fed +25 bp & ECB +25 bp on the same day→ both currencies "hawkish" — net effect on EUR/USD ≈ 0

My thesis, and you're welcome to argue with it: if oil keeps falling, this regime unwinds on the euro side first. The ECB hike was the purely energy-driven one. Lagarde herself said wages showed "no material response to the energy shock at this point," and the ECB's wage tracker points to 2.7% negotiated wage growth for H1 2027.[9] A hike with no second-round effects is the first one to get reversed. The Fed, by contrast, is sitting on broadly "elevated" inflation and a unanimous vote.

For Brazil the opposite holds — the part most commentary misses: Brazil is a net oil exporter. BofA built its bullish case for the real explicitly on "stronger terms of trade, a larger oil trade surplus, and higher government revenue."[10] If oil falls, that argument falls with it.

Terms of Trade: the price ratio between what a country sells and what it buys. If your export prices rise faster than your import prices, you earn more from abroad per unit sold — your currency strengthens. Like a tradesman whose hourly rate rises while his tools stay the same price.

Both effects point the same direction: falling oil is net upward for EUR/BRL — a weaker euro hawkishness premium against a weaker Brazilian trade balance, with the second effect working more slowly than the first. This is the only solid argument for your rate recovering over the coming months that does not depend on the election.

It's Monday, September twenty-first. The European Central Bank's latest reference rate for the euro against the Brazilian real stands at five point eight eight five seven. That's the lowest reading in thirty days, and it's almost three percent below the monthly high set on August twentieth. For you, if you earn in euros and live on reais, that means simply: the same euro buys three percent less today than it did a month ago. A thousand euros bought six thousand sixty-six reais a month ago; today it buys five thousand eight hundred eighty-six.

That number checks out. Friday's close on the Brazilian market, five point one four five one reais per dollar, multiplied by the euro-dollar rate of one point one four six, works out to five point eight nine six three. That's an eighteen-hundredths of a percent gap from the reference rate — well under the threshold where I'd flag a discrepancy as a problem. The gap is just sampling-time drift, not an error.

The first big story is September sixteenth. On that one Wednesday, the American Federal Reserve raised rates for the first time in over three years, by a quarter point, unanimously, twelve to zero. And on the very same day, Brazil's central bank cut its own rate. On paper, that's a pincer move against the real: its rate advantage over the dollar shrank by half a percentage point in a single day, from ten point two five down to nine point seven five. By the textbook, the real should have weakened.

It didn't. Since the sixteenth, your rate has fallen for three straight days. There are three reasons. First: the advantage only shrank in nominal terms. Anyone earning thirteen point seven five percent in Brazil while expecting four point nine percent inflation is sitting on eight point eight five points of real advantage — and that inflation expectation is falling, while the American one, per the Fed, stays elevated. So in real terms, the gap actually widened, not narrowed. Second, the Fed delivered less than the market had priced in: StoneX had expected a higher target range just two days before. Third, Brazil's central bank stepped in, selling a billion dollars on the spot market on the fifteenth and sixteenth of September, plus swap contracts worth another billion.

The second story is today's real headline, and it comes from the oil market. Brent crude trades today at one hundred one dollars and ninety-eight cents a barrel, down nearly two percent — the fourth straight losing session. The trigger is hope for diplomatic progress on the Middle East conflict. Compared to a year ago, Brent is still up fifty-three percent.

Why that matters: this oil price is the shared cause behind both rate moves on the sixteenth. Without it, the Fed wouldn't have reversed course after three years, and the European Central Bank wouldn't be sitting at a two-and-a-half-percent deposit rate. My thesis, and you're welcome to disagree: if oil keeps falling, this regime unwinds on the euro side first. Europe's hike was the purely energy-driven one — Lagarde herself said wages showed no material response to the energy shock. A hike with no second-round effects is the first one to get walked back. Brazil is the opposite case, because it's a net oil exporter, and cheaper oil weakens its trade balance. Both effects point in the same direction for you: upward.

The third story is the gap between the market and the consensus. I ran every named year-end forecast for the dollar against the real through today's euro-dollar rate. The Focus median comes out to five point nine six, Bank of America six point zero two, Itaú six point zero seven, BTG six point one nine, Oz Câmbio six point three zero. Every single one sits above today's rate. So the market is trading the real stronger than any published house sees it. The honest caveat: all of these numbers predate the Fed hike, and I couldn't find a single revision since.

On the election: Brazil votes in thirteen days, and the pollsters don't agree on who's ahead. Datafolha has Lula at thirty-nine percent and Flávio Bolsonaro at thirty-six. Gerp has it the other way, Flávio at thirty-seven against Lula's thirty-four. Both are within the margin of error. Treat the race as a coin flip. The more important arithmetic is different: thirty-nine plus thirty-six is seventy-five percent, so nobody reaches an outright majority. October fourth won't produce a result — it produces a runoff on October twenty-fifth. Your window of uncertainty runs three weeks longer than the calendar suggests.

On the week ahead: Tuesday at eight a.m. Brasília time brings the minutes from the last rate meeting, Thursday the quarterly monetary policy report, Friday at nine the preliminary inflation reading, with a consensus of zero point five five percent month over month. One counterintuitive tip: you should be hoping for a weak inflation number. A soft print lets Brazil's central bank cut faster, the rate advantage shrinks, the real weakens — and your euro buys more.

My assessment — and these probabilities are my own confidence, not a market number drawn from options pricing. Today: five point eight five to five point nine three. This week: five point eight zero to five point nine six. Over thirty days: five point seven five to six point one zero, with two peaks because of the election. Over three to six months: five point eight five to six point two five. Over twelve months: five point eight five to six point five zero, most likely six point one five.
