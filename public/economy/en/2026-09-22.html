The 0.44% From Yesterday to Today — and Who's Behind It

FX in detail · three-quarters real, one-quarter euro

EUR/BRL isn't its own market. It's a cross-rate — a price nobody trades directly, but one that's derived from two others: how many dollars a euro costs, times how many reais a dollar costs. That's why it pays to break every move down into its two legs. From yesterday's ECB fix (5.8920) to today's live cross-rate (5.8661), that's −0.44%. Of that:

USD/BRL 5.1279 → 5.1110=−0.33 points·EUR/USD 1.1490 → 1.1477=−0.11 points

Three-quarters of the move, then, is Brazilian strength, one-quarter European weakness. That matters because the two legs react differently: the European side hangs on politics (a CDU setback in state elections, calls for Merz to resign, a downgraded France outlook) and turns fast and unpredictably. The Brazilian side hangs on the rate advantage — and that turns slowly and predictably.

Carry (rate advantage): Brazil pays 13.75% a year on short-term money, the euro zone 2.50% — an advantage of 11.25 percentage points. Picture two piggy banks: the Brazilian one fills up eleven times faster. As long as the real doesn't depreciate by more than that advantage, the foreign investor comes out ahead — and it's exactly that capital that pushes the real up. On Sep 16, the advantage shrank from 11.75 to 11.25 points, because the ECB hiked and Brazil cut: not enough to turn the flow around.

That explains why the real has gotten firmer since the double rate move, even though its advantage got smaller. TradingEconomics names additional drivers: lower-than-expected inflation data and positioning that's betting on more fiscally conservative candidates.

As a cross-check: the FX script's automated consistency check reports a 0.001% deviation today — that sounds good and means nothing, because all three legs of that check come from the same ECB family. It confirms arithmetic, not source agreement. The real cross-check is the one above: two TradingEconomics legs against the ECB fix, −0.44%. Under the flag, but not zero.

What Today's 8 a.m. Minutes Need to Answer

The week's most important domestic event

Ata (Copom minutes): Brazil's central bank publishes meeting minutes a few business days after every rate decision. Like a receipt after a purchase: you already knew the decision, but only here do you find out what was debated and what's planned next. For the rate, tone matters more than the number.

The Sep 16 decision was unanimous among all seven voting members: the fifth straight 0.25-point cut, taking the rate from 14.75% to 13.75% since April. The statement said inflation and core rates have slowed and fallen below the upper bound of the tolerance band — but remain above target, with risks elevated on both sides and tilted upward. Projection for Q1 2028: 3.2%.

The open question: does the cycle continue past the November meeting? The Focus median sees the Selic at 13.50% by end-2026 — meaning one more cut. BTG Pactual says the opposite: a hold at 13.75% through year-end after September.

Why this affects you: every further cut shrinks the carry, makes the real less attractive, and pushes EUR/BRL up. So the reading of the minutes that's good for you is the dovish one.

Where I Disagree With the Consensus

Where I think the street is too slow

The houses are probably right on the euro leg and probably wrong on the real leg. Their USD/BRL targets of 5.20 to 5.40 come from a world where the Fed was cutting and the Selic sat at 14.00 to 14.75%. Since Sep 16 it's the reverse — the Fed is hiking, Brazil is cutting — which argues for a weaker real. And yet the market has been trading stronger than every published house for weeks.

Focus itself shows who's actually moving: the headline median stuck at 5.20 for the 14th week, the sub-median of the most recently updated estimates down to 5.16. So I'm weighting the lower half of the consensus range — and I don't trust Itaú (Jun 26) or BofA (Mar 26) here at all, because both predate the September rate moves. Either the street is too slow to revise, or the market is underestimating election risk; I think the former is more likely, but not by enough that I'd stake an entire year's currency exchange on it.

Positioning & Brazilian Risk

Two markets that disagree

CDS (Credit Default Swap): insurance against a sovereign default. 121 basis points means a 1.21% annual premium on the insured amount. Like homeowner's insurance whose premium rises when break-ins get more common on the street — the number doesn't measure whether Brazil will default, but how nervous the market is about it.

Brazil's 5-year CDS closed Monday at 121.12 basis points, a jump of 9.33 bp or 8.34% in a single day — a lot for an otherwise sluggish instrument. Caveat: the same source page also renders a previous-close figure of 190.23 that doesn't reconcile with the 121.12; so I'm using the level and the change, not the previous-close figure.

The Ibovespa closed Friday at 185,229.17 points (−0.41%), ending a four-week winning streak. Cross-checked: Thursday's 185,992 points times 0.9959 works out to exactly 185,229 — two reports that agree.

My read: the CDS is pricing election headlines, the FX market is pricing the carry inflow. In setups like this, the flow almost always wins on a daily and weekly view, credit more often wins on a monthly view. Today's firm real is real — but it says nothing about what November will look like.

Practical Notes for Converting

Understanding, not a recommendation

5.87 is the worst rate of the month, and there's no single trigger you could wait for — it's the slow work of an eleven-and-a-half-point rate advantage. At the same time, Oct 4 and Oct 25 give you two real events inside the 30-day window, after which fundamentals rather than polls will set the rate again: an identifiable edge instead of pure hope. What you do with that is your call.

It's Tuesday, September twenty-second. The euro's reference rate against the Brazilian real from yesterday sits at five point eight nine two zero, but today's independently calculated live cross-rate is five point eight six six one — zero point four four percent lower, and below every European Central Bank fix of the past thirty days.

The easy explanation would be that the euro is weak. That's true for about a quarter of it. I broke the move down into its two legs: zero point three three percentage points come from the real getting firmer, only zero point one one from the euro giving ground. So this is Brazilian strength, not European weakness. And that distinction matters, because the two legs behave very differently. The European side hangs on politics — a weak state-election result for the CDU, calls for Chancellor Merz to resign, a downgraded outlook for France. Politics turns fast. The Brazilian side hangs on the interest-rate gap, and that turns slowly.

On that rate gap: Brazil pays thirteen point seven five percent on short-term money, the euro zone two point five. That's an eleven point two five percentage-point advantage. On September sixteenth, that gap narrowed, because the European Central Bank hiked while Brazil's central bank cut — but only by half a point, from eleven and a half. Not enough to turn the flow of capital around. That's why the real keeps getting firmer even though its edge got smaller.

Today at eight a.m. Brasília time, the minutes from the last rate meeting come out. That decision was unanimous, all seven voting members, the fifth straight cut since April. The open question is whether the cutting cycle continues past the November meeting. The Focus survey's median says yes, and sees the policy rate at thirteen point five percent by year-end. BTG Pactual says no, expecting a hold at thirteen point seven five through December. For you, the good reading of the minutes is the dovish one: every further cut shrinks the rate advantage, makes the real less attractive, and pushes your rate up.

On consensus: I converted every named year-end forecast for the dollar against the real into your rate, all using the same euro-dollar leg of one point one six. BNP Paribas comes out to six point zero three, the Focus median also six point zero three, Itaú six point one five, BTG six point two six. Every one of them sits above today's rate of five point eight seven. But there's a tell: the Focus survey's headline median has been stuck at five twenty for fourteen straight weeks, while the sub-median of the most recently updated forecasts has already dropped to five point one six. The freshest opinions are moving toward the market.

Two numbers that disagree with each other: Brazil's five-year default insurance jumped eight point three four percent on Monday, to a hundred twenty-one basis points — a big one-day move for an otherwise sluggish market. At the same time, the currency market is pricing the real firmer. My read: the credit market is pricing election headlines, the currency market is pricing the interest-rate inflow. On a day-to-day and week-to-week view, the inflow usually wins; over a month, credit more often does. Today's firm real is real, but it doesn't tell you what November looks like.

My own forecast, and again, these percentages are my confidence, not a market-derived probability. Today: five point eight four to five point nine zero. This week: five point eight zero to five point nine five. Over the next thirty days: five point seven eight to six point one zero, with two peaks because of the election. Over three to six months: five point nine zero to six point two five. Over twelve months: five point eight zero to six point four zero, most likely six point zero five.

Practically speaking: five point eight seven is the worst rate this month, and there's no single trigger to wait for — it's just the slow grind of a huge interest-rate gap. But two real events sit inside the next thirty days, October fourth and October twenty-fifth, after which fundamentals take back over from polling.
