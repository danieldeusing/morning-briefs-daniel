Before the Election, It's the Euro Moving Your Rate — Not the Real

Today is the last trading day before the presidential election on Sunday. The ECB fix stands at 5.8610, just under 1% below Monday's weekly high. The convenient story would be "election jitters are weighing on the real." The numbers say something else: since Monday the real has actually lost ground against the dollar — your rate fell because the euro slid to a one-year low. Only this midday's weak US jobs data gave it some support.

The Euro Fell, Not the Real

FX in detail · the week in two legs

Cross-rate: almost nobody trades EUR/BRL directly. The price comes from two others multiplied together: dollars per euro times reais per dollar. Like a detour through a middleman — if either leg gets pricier, your final price changes, no matter what the other one does.

From Monday's fix (5.9186) to today's fix (5.8610), that's −0.97%. Broken down with the ECB series:

EUR/USD 1.1378 → 1.1225=−1.34%·USD/BRL 5.2018 → 5.2214=+0.38%⇒EUR/BRL −0.97%

So the real lost ground against the dollar too — just less than the euro lost against the dollar. Ten days ago it was the other way around: on Sep 22, three-quarters of the move came from real strength. Since Sep 21, the tally stands at EUR/USD −2.3% and USD/BRL +1.8%. The pacesetter has changed.

Why the euro fell. The chain runs through US interest rates:

Market prices Fed hikes for Oct. + Dec.→ US 10-year hits 5.287% (Sep 30)→ dollar assets more attractive→ EUR/USD one-year low→ EUR/BRL −1%

The euro side didn't push back: inflation jumped to 3.8%, but almost entirely because of energy (+18.8%) — core sits at 2.5%. Standard Chartered reads that as "no hike in October"; the market cut the probability from around 60 to about 20–30%.

Why the real lost only a little. On Thursday the dollar jumped 0.88% to R$5.22 — oil above $102, risk aversion over possible US strikes on Iran. But the peso, the Chilean peso, and the rand fell 1.2–1.7%. The rate advantage (Selic 13.75% versus the ECB's 2.50%) cushions the blow.

And today? Payrolls came out at 9:30 a.m. BRT — about fifteen minutes after the ECB fix. Just 29,000 new jobs, plus a 60,000 downward revision to prior months: that chips away at the case for further Fed hikes. DXY −0.18%, EUR/USD live at 1.1258 versus 1.1225 in the fix. That's why the live cross-rate at 5.8756 sits 0.25% above the fix: almost entirely the euro leg. The real is barely moving today (+0.03%).

As a cross-check: the script's consistency check reports 0.0% deviation — meaningless, because all the legs come from the same ECB fix. The real cross-check is the independent TradingEconomics legs: +0.25%, explained by the timing gap to the payrolls data.

Sunday: What the Election Means for Your Rate

The month's most important date

Datafolha (Sep 28–Oct 1, 2,506 respondents, ±2 points) puts Lula at 45% of valid votes, Flávio Bolsonaro at 40%, followed by Cury 4, Caiado 3, Renan Santos 3. Winning in the first round takes more than half of valid votes — on these numbers, a runoff on Oct 25 is the clear base case. For the runoff: Datafolha has Lula 48 to 45, Atlas/Bloomberg has Flávio 47.7 to 47.6, Quaest 42 to 42. A wide-open race.

Risk premium: the markup investors demand because they aren't sure Brazil's public finances will hold up. Like a higher interest rate for a borrower with a shaky job: as worry rises, so does the markup — and foreign money pulls out, the real falls.

The market reads the election as a fiscal question. Goldman Sachs wrote explicitly on Oct 1 that post-election policy, more than the result itself, is what drives the real: USD/BRL 4.50–4.80 under a favorable fiscal path, ~5.40 at the Sept. 2024 premium, ~5.80 if it snaps back to the December 2024 high. JPMorgan (Sep 28) sees 4.90 to 5.50. Converted at today's EUR/USD of 1.1258:

For you it works in reverse: if the market reads the result as fiscally strict, that strengthens the real and lowers your rate; read as a spending path, it raises it. The market associates the former with Flávio, the latter with Lula — BTG Pactual cited ~5.00 USD/BRL for Flávio versus 5.70–6.00 for Lula. Caveat: the date of these BTG scenarios isn't verified; BTG's dated base case (4.90 by end-2026) is from May 8 and is stale.

How big could the swing get? Options prices imply about 6.5% of movement across both rounds combined, per JPMorgan. On €1,000, that's roughly R$380 in one direction or the other.

Where I Disagree With the Consensus

The election isn't the only risk to your rate

The street treats the election as the driver. For USD/BRL, that's true. For EUR/BRL, though, this week was decided by the euro leg: −1.34% against the dollar, more than the entire EUR/BRL move. A "good" election outcome for you (a weak real) could therefore get swallowed by a further-falling euro — and a softer Fed could lift the euro even if the real firms up after the election.

My take: today's payrolls data is almost as important for the next 30 days as Sunday is. If the US labor market is genuinely turning, the EUR/USD one-year low is more likely a floor than a waypoint — that's why my monthly range skews slightly upward. I could be wrong: a single weak report has misled before.

Positioning & Brazilian Risk

Credit is getting nervous, the rate curve isn't

CDS (Credit Default Swap): insurance against a sovereign default; 131 basis points means a 1.31% annual premium. Like homeowner's insurance that gets pricier when break-ins become more common on the street — it measures nervousness, not the default itself.
  • 5-year CDS 131.88 bp (Oct 1, delayed), up from 121.12 on Sep 21 — about +10 bp in ten days. 52-week range 110.62–159.67. Caveat: the source page shows a nonsensical previous-close figure (190.23); I'm using only the level and the range.
  • The Ibovespa volatility index hit 31.84 on Sep 28, a record since its launch in March 2024 — the stock market is paying more for hedging than ever.
  • DI futures (close Sep 30): DI1F27 13.555%, DI1F29 13.790%, DI1F31 13.96%. The curve is calm — panic looks different.
  • BCB swaps: the central bank fully rolled the $2.5 billion maturing on Oct 1; the roll of the November maturities begins on Oct 5. No new net swaps were reported. I couldn't verify the total outstanding stock.

My read: hedging, yes; a stampede, no. The DI futures — bets on the future interbank rate, like a fixed-price power contract whose price reveals expected rates — show no fiscal shock; the options market is pricing in a possible one.

Practical Notes for Converting

Understanding, not a recommendation

Today is the last chance to convert at a rate that doesn't yet know the election outcome: roughly 5.86–5.88. Converting now means knowing your price. Waiting until after Sunday means taking on a bet with two outcomes — per the options market, roughly ±R$380 per €1,000 across both rounds, and the euro leg can push that bet in either direction. What you do with that is your call.

It's Friday, October second — the last trading day before Brazil's presidential election on Sunday. The European Central Bank's reference rate stands at five point eight six one zero, just under one percent below Monday's weekly high. The independently calculated live rate is five point eight seven five six.

The convenient story would be that election jitters are moving the real. The numbers say otherwise. Since Monday, the euro has lost one point three four percent against the dollar, while the real has given up only about zero point four. Your rate fell because the euro fell, not because of Brazil. The reason is in America: the ten-year Treasury yield climbed above five point two eight seven percent, reportedly its highest level since two thousand two, and the market priced in further Fed rate hikes. That pulled money into the dollar and pushed the euro to a one-year low.

This afternoon brought a wrinkle in that story. The US added only twenty-nine thousand jobs in September, versus an expected ninety thousand or so, and prior months were revised down. The dollar index eased, and the euro recovered a bit. Because the reference rate was set just before that data came out, the live rate now sits about a quarter of a percentage point above it.

Now, the election. Datafolha has Lula at forty-five percent of valid votes, Flávio Bolsonaro at forty. Neither is enough for a first-round win, so a runoff on October twenty-fifth is the expected outcome. For the runoff itself, the polls show a wide-open race. The market reads the election as a question about Brazil's public finances. A result that looks fiscally strict strengthens the real and lowers your rate. A result that looks like more spending weakens the real and raises it. Options pricing implies about six and a half percent of movement across both rounds, according to JPMorgan — on a thousand euros, that's roughly three hundred eighty reais in one direction or the other.

Brazilian inflation is heating back up. The September preview came in at zero point seven percent instead of the expected zero point five three, and the central bank now puts the odds at ninety percent that inflation ends the year above the target ceiling. That slows down further rate cuts. For you, it's a paradox: high rates keep the real firm, and your rate tends to stay lower.

Here's my own forecast, and again, these probabilities are my own confidence, not a market number. Today: five point eight four to five point nine zero, most likely five point eight seven. This week: five point seven two to six point zero five, with two peaks because of the election. Over thirty days: five point six five to six point two five. Over three to six months: five point eight five to six point three zero. Over twelve months: five point seven five to six point five zero — most likely around six point zero five in both of those longer horizons.
