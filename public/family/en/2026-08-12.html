RSV Remains the Reason for Caution [9]

SARI and RSV: SARI (SRAG in Portuguese) is Brazil's umbrella term for "severe acute respiratory illness" — any infection bad enough that someone needs to go to the hospital. RSV (VSR in Portuguese) is a common-cold virus that barely fazes adults, but in infants it swells the fine airways shut: think of our airways as a thick straw and an infant's as thin as a strand of spaghetti — the same amount of swelling closes theirs completely.

The Fiocruz InfoGripe bulletin for SE 30/2026 (07/26–08/01, published 08/06) reports a declining trend nationwide — and still lists Paraná among the states with incidence at alert, risk, or high-risk level. In the four-week window, 54.1% of positive SARI cases are RSV. Nationwide, 2026 has counted 130,054 SARI cases so far, of which 69,895 (53.7%) tested lab-positive for a respiratory virus.

Concretely, for the youngest child As long as RSV accounts for more than half of severe cases and Paraná stays in the alert band, the simple rule applies for a four-week-old: no crowds indoors, and anyone with a cold doesn't pick the baby up. RSV spreads mainly through hands and surfaces — washing hands before touching the baby does more than any mask outdoors. For next week's EFAPI, that means: the three older kids, yes; the baby, better not in the hall. The next bulletin (SE 31) is expected Thursday — inferred from the rhythm, not announced.
Multivacinação — 08/22 is the convenient catch-all date The national campaign runs 08/03–09/01/2026, and "Dia D" is Saturday, 08/22. In Paraná, over 1,800 vaccination sites open across all 399 municipalities offering 20 vaccines from the national calendar — among them polio, MMR, hepatitis A and B, meningitis, yellow fever, influenza, and dengue. Target group: everyone under 15; the point is filling gaps in the caderneta (vaccination card). For the three older kids, the practical route; for the newborn, nothing changes — they stay on the regular schedule.

German-Brazilian Corner: Malasartes Is Our Till Eulenspiegel

Anyone listening to Pedro Malasartes this afternoon is hearing a character who exists back home too: the trickster who never works, always schemes, and still comes out ahead. In Germany he's called Till Eulenspiegel — same role, different backdrop. That's an easy bridge for the ride home: hear the story in Portuguese, retell it in German that evening, and lay the Eulenspiegel version next to it.
