Health: Paraná Stays in the Alert Band [8]

SARI and RSV: SARI (SRAG in Portuguese) is Brazil's umbrella term for "severe acute respiratory illness" — any infection bad enough that someone needs to go to the hospital. RSV is a cold virus that barely fazes adults but, in infants, swells the fine airways shut: think of our airways as a thick straw, an infant's as thin as a strand of spaghetti — the same swelling closes theirs completely.

The latest Fiocruz InfoGripe bulletin remains the one for SE 30/2026 (07/26–08/01, published 08/06). It reports a declining trend nationwide but still lists Paraná among the 14 states with incidence at alert, risk, or high-risk level. For 2026, 130,054 SARI cases have been reported nationwide, of which 69,895 (53.7%) tested lab-positive. Among those positive cases, 42.1% are RSV, 30% rhinoviruses, 18.9% influenza A, 5.1% influenza B, and 4.1% Sars-CoV-2. For Paraná specifically, the bulletin notes "sinais de desaceleração" for influenza B — the curve is climbing more slowly, but it isn't falling yet.

The expected SE-31 bulletin hadn't appeared as of this morning — the Thursday expectation noted here yesterday was inferred from the rhythm, not announced, and it didn't hold up.

Concretely, for the youngest child As long as RSV remains the single biggest group among severe cases and Paraná stays in the alert band, the simple rule applies for a six-week-old: no crowds indoors, and anyone with a cold doesn't pick the baby up. RSV spreads mainly through hands and surfaces — washing hands before touching the baby does more than any mask outdoors. For this week, that means in practical terms: the packed Auditório for the storytelling and the EnCena halls starting on the 21st are for the older kids; Sunday's Cavalgada is outdoors and thus the far more harmless setting.

German-Brazilian Corner: The Leonhardiritt of Assaí

What happens in Assaí on Sunday has an equivalent back home — just with a different saint. The Bênção do Tropeiro is a horse blessing before the procession sets off; in Bavaria the same thing is called the Leonhardiritt, where the cavalcade rides to the chapel of Saint Leonard, patron of horses. Two continents, the same idea: whoever makes a living alongside an animal has it blessed once a year.

The tropeiros were the mule drivers who moved livestock and goods across the interior long before there were roads — Brazil's answer to the wagon drivers of old. Their trail rations are still on the menu today: feijão tropeiro, beans with manioc flour and bacon. Good conversation fodder for the ride home — and, with the Concurso do Prato Típico in mind, maybe even a hint of where your own entry could start.
