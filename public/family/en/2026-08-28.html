Two Portals, Four Days, Not a Single Matching Rain Figure [1][2][3]

Yesterday the dispute was limited to the weekend. Today it's everywhere. Climatempo shows Ribeirão Claro from today through Monday at 0% and 0.0 mm four times over, every day with the same description: "Sol com muitas nuvens durante o dia e períodos de céu nublado." theweather.com reads the exact same place completely differently — today 30% "light rain," Sat 50%, Sun 70% thunderstorms, Mon 60%. And it has raised its numbers since yesterday, not lowered them: Saturday stood at 40% there yesterday, today it's 50%; today's Friday was still listed at 0% yesterday.

On temperature, the picture is much better. Converted, theweather.com shows today at 18.9–30.0°C against climatempo's 18–31, Saturday 21.1–31.7 against 23–32, Monday 18.9–32.2 against 21–32 — three of four days within two degrees, so a match. Only Sunday stands out: climatempo 34°C, theweather.com 30°C. Not a contradiction, but the logical result — anyone expecting a thunderstorm also expects four degrees less at the peak.

What makes it into the brief This newsletter's two-portal rule is strict: a forecast only makes it in if two ladder services agree. Today it's met — climatempo and SIMEPAR, Paraná's state weather service, both show 0% and 0.0 mm for the 50 km neighboring town of Jacarezinho. For Saturday, Sunday, and Monday it isn't. That's why the weather card above shows only the temperature for those days, no rain figure, and why the weekend pick has the line about rain jackets. Ironic footnote: the only day the two portals converge is Tuesday, Sep 1 — climatempo 82%, theweather.com 90%. In agreement that it'll be wet.
Chance of rain: "70% rain" doesn't mean "it rains 70% of the day," and it doesn't mean "over 70% of the area." It means: in seven out of ten model runs with this weather pattern, measurable rain fell at this point. Like rolling dice — the same starting conditions, different outcomes. And because each portal rolls a different die (different model, different computational grid, different update rhythm), two reputable services can put out 0% and 70% for the same place without either one lying. That's exactly why the number that counts here isn't the nicer one, but the one where they agree.

On top of that, the official layer, which needs to be read correctly for the second time this week: INMET's registry lists two active advisories covering Paraná today — "Vendaval" with "Vento variando entre 40 km/h e 60 km/h" (Paraná and Mato Grosso do Sul) and "Tempestade" with "Chuva entre 20 e 30 mm/h ou até 50 mm/dia" (Região Sul), both "Perigo Potencial" level, both valid today from 12:00am to 11:59pm. Checking the coverage lists defuses it like yesterday: Jacarezinho, Ribeirão Claro, Londrina, and Ourinhos aren't on either one. But it explains why one portal lands on 30% for today at all — the ingredients for a thunderstorm are present in the state, just not over the Norte Pioneiro.

This Week's Trap: A Festa Julina That Doesn't Exist [4]

This would have been a good item: a Festa Julina at the Praça da Bandeira in Londrina, postponed due to rain, Monday, Aug 31, and Tuesday, Sep 1, from 4:00pm, free admission, with the kids' program "Arraial Show da Turma da Boiadeirinha." It just isn't true.

The source text on the Prefeitura Londrina blog is dated July 30, 2026, and reads "nesta sexta (31) e sábado (1º), a partir das 16h, em ambos os dias" — this Friday the 31st and Saturday the 1st. Checking the calendar: July 31, 2026, was a Friday, August 1 a Saturday. August 31, by contrast, is a Monday, September 1 a Tuesday — only the July date fits the weekdays named in the article. The festival happened four weeks ago and is over.

Why this is here Not as a curiosity, but as a pattern. An article with no year in the body text, plus a search engine that surfaces it as fresh, adds up to an event that doesn't exist — and in the worst case, a family standing on an empty plaza on Monday evening. The check costs ten seconds: does the weekday named in the text match the assumed date? If "Friday the 31st" doesn't fall on a Friday, the date is wrong — or the month is.

Health: The Missing Bulletin Is Out — and It's Mostly Good News [6][7]

For a week, this brief said "week 33 unavailable." Today it's here. The InfoGripe summary for Epidemiological Week 33/2026 (data Aug 16–22, 2026) turns the key number in the right direction: only 3 of the 27 states still have an alert-, risk-, or high-risk-level incidence with a growth signal — Alagoas, Mato Grosso, and Roraima. Two weeks ago, eleven states carried an alert classification overall.

What applies to Paraná It's not a full all-clear. Paraná is in the second group: "8 UFs também apresentam incidência de SRAG em níveis de alerta, risco ou alto risco, porém sem sinal de crescimento" — Amazonas, Mato Grosso do Sul, Minas Gerais, Paraná, Rio Grande do Sul, Rio de Janeiro, Santa Catarina, Sergipe. In other words: elevated level, but no longer growing. For the virus that matters most for infants, here's the sentence that counts: "Os casos de SRAG por VSR continuam caindo em todo o país" — and for Paraná: "mesmo com importante queda, ainda estão um pouco elevados para essa época do ano." "Still high" (week 32) has become "a bit elevated." And metapneumovirus, which was still climbing in week 32 across the entire South region including Paraná, no longer appears on the week 33 list for Paraná.
SRAG: "Síndrome Respiratória Aguda Grave" (Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome) — that isn't every cold, only the part severe enough to land someone in the hospital. So InfoGripe doesn't count runny noses, it counts beds. Like a fire department report that doesn't count scorched pots, only the calls it responded to. That's why "Paraná without a growth signal" only means: the hospitals aren't filling up further — not that no one is getting sick anymore.

One point that's newly emerged and directly concerns this household: nationally, week 33 reports "um leve sinal de aumento dos casos de SRAG nas crianças e adolescentes de 2 a 14 anos, associado principalmente ao rinovírus" — and Paraná is on the list of states with rising rhinovirus admissions. Rhinovirus is the classic cold virus; for a school-age kid, that's a runny nose, for an infant in the same household, it's the transmission route. So the rule stays exactly as it was: avoid crowded closed rooms, wash hands before anyone handles the baby, keep siblings with colds at a distance. Don't wait until tomorrow for noticeably fast breathing, chest retractions between the ribs, or a clear inability to feed.

Dengue is unchanged: the official SESA/PR bulletin list, rechecked today, still shows Informe Epidemiológico No. 25 from Aug 18, 2026 as the latest, and there's no No. 26. That keeps statewide figures for Paraná (weeks 01–32/2026) at 60,551 notifications, 5,878 confirmed cases, 5 deaths — and for our own region, 19th Health Region Jacarezinho: 102 confirmed cases, zero deaths, incidence 27.97 per 100,000. Still, ahead of Tuesday's rain, the yard sweep against standing water is worth doing.

Scorpions During Dry Spells Tabajara Notícias reports a ten-year-old in Jacarezinho who received antiscorpion serum after a sting on Aug 23, 2026. That's a single case, not a trend — but four dry, 30-degree days are exactly the conditions that drive the animals into houses. Costs nothing: shake out shoes before putting them on, don't leave towels on the floor, shake out bedding briefly in the evening. For the two-year-old who picks everything up off the floor, this is the most relevant line in this section.

It's Friday, August twenty-eighth, and this weekend finally has a real anchor. On Saturday, Londrina runs the third Festival das Diversidades from ten in the morning until ten at night — twelve hours of programming, free admission, open to all ages. The catch isn't the lineup, it's the sky.

The dispute between the two weather portals, which was limited to the weekend as recently as yesterday, now covers every single day. Climatempo shows a zero percent chance of rain four days running, from today through Monday. Theweather dot com shows thirty percent today, fifty on Saturday, seventy on Sunday, and sixty on Monday — and it's been raising those numbers since yesterday, not lowering them.

On temperature, the picture is much better. For today, Saturday, and Monday, the two services land within two degrees of each other, which counts as agreement. Only Sunday breaks that pattern: climatempo says thirty-four degrees, the other portal says thirty — not a contradiction, just the logical result of expecting a thunderstorm. For today, the dry forecast holds up especially well, because a third source backs it: Simepar, Paraná's state weather service, sees zero percent for the neighboring town of Jacarezinho, fifty kilometers away, too. That doesn't hold for Saturday, Sunday, or Monday, which is why this brief's weather card shows only temperatures for those days, no rain figure. One irony worth noting: the single day the two portals actually agree on is next Tuesday — and they agree it'll be wet.

A quick word on what a rain forecast actually means. Seventy percent doesn't mean it rains seventy percent of the day. It means that in seven out of ten model runs with this weather pattern, measurable rain fell at that spot. Because each portal uses a different model and a different computing grid, two reputable services can put out zero and seventy for the same town without either one being wrong. That's exactly why the number that matters here isn't the nicer one — it's the one both services agree on.

On the weekend pick: the festival runs at the Praça da Juventude in Londrina's south zone, a hundred eighty-five kilometers and almost two hours forty minutes each way. The skate workshop and beach volleyball are built for the two school-age kids, and the market and food stalls carry the two-year-old along just fine. The honest advice on timing: not midday. At thirty-two degrees, the early afternoon on a plaza with no reliable shade is no place for a four-week-old. Arriving around four in the afternoon still leaves six hours of programming and much more comfortable temperatures. Anyone who needs a weatherproof backup should take Sunday at the Sesc Cadeião instead, with four free kids' events under a roof.

A word of caution belongs here too. A Festa Julina supposedly postponed to the end of August, at the Praça da Bandeira in Londrina, has been circulating in search results all week. It doesn't exist. The source article is dated July thirtieth and says the festival runs this Friday the thirty-first and Saturday the first. July thirty-first was in fact a Friday, and August first a Saturday. August thirty-first, though, is a Monday. The festival happened four weeks ago. The check takes ten seconds: does the weekday named in the text actually match the assumed date?

The best news of the day comes from the health section. The InfoGripe bulletin for epidemiological week thirty-three, missing for a week, is finally out, covering data from August sixteenth through the twenty-second. Only three of the twenty-seven states still show a growing alert-level incidence, and Paraná isn't one of them. The key sentence for a four-week-old: severe RSV cases are falling nationwide. For Paraná specifically, the bulletin says they're still a bit elevated for the time of year, despite a significant drop — a step down from the "still high" wording two weeks ago.

It isn't a full all-clear, though. Paraná sits in the group of eight states with an elevated level that's no longer growing. New this week: a slight uptick in severe cases among kids two to fourteen, driven mostly by rhinovirus, which is also rising in Paraná. Rhinovirus is the classic cold bug — a runny nose for a school-age kid, but the transmission route straight to a baby in the same house. So the rule holds: avoid crowded closed rooms, wash hands before anyone picks up the baby, keep siblings with colds at a distance. On dengue, nothing moved — the state's bulletin list still shows Informe number twenty-five from August eighteenth as the latest, and the Jacarezinho health region stays at a hundred two confirmed cases and zero deaths. The often-cited year-over-year comparison, a ninety-six percent drop, stays deliberately out: the primary page refuses access, and the figure carries no epidemiological week stamp. No week stamp, no health number.
