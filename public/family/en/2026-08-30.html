48 vs. 80 — and Why Both Numbers Can Be Right [1][2][3]

For days, two weather portals have been arguing in this brief. Today a third one joins in, and it makes things clearer, not murkier. theweather.com puts today's rain probability at 80% with thunderstorms, and climadobrasil's hourly overview arrives at the same peak of 80% — two independently retrieved portals, the exact same number. climatempo stands at 48% and 0.7 mm. This brief's rule is clear here: two matching portals beat a single outlier. Today, 80 is the number.

What's notable, though, is that the two old rivals have moved toward each other. Yesterday it was 35% against 90%; today it's 48% against 80% — the gap has shrunk from 55 to 32 points, and from both sides. That's not a coincidence, it's the normal behavior of weather models as the date draws closer.

Rain probability: "48% rain" doesn't mean it rains 48% of the day, and it doesn't mean 48% of the area gets wet. It means: out of a hundred days that look meteorologically like this one, 48 had measurable rain at this location. Like rolling dice — the number describes how often this roll comes up, not how hard. That's why 48% and 80% can both be "right": they're calculated over different time windows, different grid points, and different model ensembles. The practical takeaway isn't to pick one number, it's to ask: when does the rain fall, and how much? Both answers are in the next paragraph — and both matter more for planning than the percentage.

And that's exactly where the real news is. climadobrasil breaks the day down hour by hour, and the trend goes from 0% in the morning up to 80% over the course of the day, with the largest single-hour amount being 8.7 mm at 2:00pm. climatempo, by contrast, still writes "Sol e muitas nuvens à tarde. À noite ocorrem pancadas de chuva" (sun with lots of clouds in the afternoon; rain showers occur at night) — meaning rain only after dark. That's a four-to-six-hour difference, and for a family with a 2:40-hour drive home, that's the whole ballgame. The cautious reading wins out: expect rain from midday on, not just from sunset.

German-Brazilian: the Vocabulary of Semana da Pátria

The coming week is called Semana da Pátria across Brazil, "Week of the Fatherland," and it brings four words you'll see everywhere starting Monday. Desfile — the parade; not Carnival, but an orderly procession of schools, the fire department, military police, and marching bands down the street, closer to a small-town civic parade than to a Mardi Gras float parade. Fanfarra — a school's marching band, drums and brass; the loud centerpiece of every desfile, and the reason small kids often prefer to stand a bit farther back. Cívico-militar — the official category label: civic and military, both are part of it. And feriado prolongado — the "extended weekend," when a holiday falls on a Monday or Friday. That's exactly what happens this year: Sete de Setembro, Independence Day, falls on a Monday in 2026.

Correction: the FLOu Isn't Happening This September [7]

In the Aug 28 and Aug 29 editions, the Event Radar carried the FLOu — Festa Literária de Ourinhos with the date "Sep 20–27, book fair Sep 23–27." That line was wrong, and it's hereby retracted.

The reason is one of the nastiest traps in web research: Ourinhos's Prefeitura page carries a fresh timestamp up top — "Portal atualizado em: 28/08/2026" (portal updated on Aug 28, 2026) — while the article underneath is years old. The text with the dates September 20–27 is from September 18, 2015, and describes the fifth edition. A second article, which surfaces prominently in search and announces the 14th edition, September 8 to 12, is from September 5, 2025. The fact that September 20 and 27, 2026 happen to fall on Sundays again, just as they did in 2015, made the mix-up look even more plausible.

Where Things Stand Now For FLOu 2026, no date has been published that this brief could confirm. The line has been removed from the Event Radar. If the Prefeitura announces a 15th edition, it will reappear here — with the article's date attached. And a lesson for future reading: an "updated on" date in a page header says nothing about the age of the article underneath it. The only thing you can trust is the timestamp on the text itself — and the cross-check of whether the stated weekday matches the stated date. "Segunda-feira, dia 8" (Monday the 8th) makes sense for 2025; in 2026, September 8 is a Tuesday.

On top of that, a slight increase among children ages 2 to 14, mainly driven by rhinovirus — Paraná is on this list.
SRAG and RSV: in the Brazilian reporting system, SRAG is the category "severe acute respiratory syndrome" — not every cold gets counted, only the cases that land someone in the hospital. Like a fire department's incident log: it doesn't list every bit of kitchen smoke, only the calls someone had to respond to. RSV is respiratory syncytial virus, the most common trigger for this category in babies — a runny nose for an adult, but for the smallest airways a narrowing that makes drinking and breathing hard work. That's exactly why this line runs every week: for a school-age kid it's a runny nose; for a four-week-old, it's the one figure in this whole brief that can end in a hospital stay.

For the household with the four-week-old, nothing changes here: avoid crowded closed rooms, wash hands before anyone touches her, keep siblings with colds at a distance. Concretely for today, that means: classic cars by the lake and the forest park, yes; a packed library hall at 4:00pm with the baby in your arms, less so. And after Tuesday's 7.2 mm, the yard sweep against standing water is due: saucers, buckets, gutters, tarps. This season's steep dengue decline doesn't change the fact that 4,890 of the 5,878 cases originated within the state itself.

Two of the three portals now land on exactly the same number, eighty percent, so that's the figure for today, and climatempo's forty-eight is the outlier.

A word on that number, because it's almost always misread. Forty-eight percent rain doesn't mean it rains forty-eight percent of the day, and it doesn't mean forty-eight percent of the area gets wet. It means: out of a hundred days that look meteorologically like this one, forty-eight had measurable rain at this spot. That's why forty-eight and eighty can both be right — they're calculated over different time windows and different models. What matters more than the percentage is when the rain falls and how much.
