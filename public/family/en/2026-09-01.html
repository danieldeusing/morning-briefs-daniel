Two Portals, One Number: Eighteen Millimeters Today [1][2][3]

Both portals were retrieved this morning specifically for Ribeirão Claro — not for Jacarezinho, as the first research pass had done. That's not a minor detail on a stormy day in the Norte Pioneiro: 50 km here can be the difference between a wet day and a very wet day. And the result is unusually clear. climatempo: 95% rain probability, 18.4 mm. theweather.com: 90%, 0.709 inches — converted, 18.0 mm. A four-tenths-of-a-millimeter gap. Two models that calculated independently of each other land on practically the same rainfall total; that happens rarely, and it means: this figure is solid.

Today's revision from yesterday concerns the afternoon Yesterday, this spot said — based on climatempo's text at the time — that it would clear up in the afternoon: "à tarde pode chuviscar e o tempo abre" (in the afternoon it may drizzle at most, and it clears up). That no longer holds today. The current text reads: "Nublado com temporal de manhã e à tarde. À noite as nuvens diminuem e não chove." So storms in the morning and afternoon, and it only clears in the evening. The amount has also risen — yesterday the model showed 14.9 mm, today 18.4 mm. Anyone holding onto yesterday's "just go in the afternoon, then" advice: the afternoon is no better a window than the morning today.
"Perigo potencial" (INMET): The national weather service ranks its warnings in three colors, and the lowest is called perigo potencial — yellow, literally "possible danger." Like a yellow blinking light at an intersection: it doesn't say "stop," it says "pay attention." Yellow means rain and wind that can cause everyday damage — snapped branches, brief power outages, overflowing gutters. The tiers above it, perigo (orange) and grande perigo (red), are the ones where you cancel plans. Today is yellow. For this household, that doesn't mean "stay inside" — it means: secure the trampoline and patio chairs, don't park the car under the big tree, and push planned trips into the evening if they can be pushed.

The INMET warning itself comes with a caveat. Per today's available reporting, it covers all 396 municipalities of Paraná — the Norte Pioneiro included — and names up to 50 mm over the day, gusts of 40–60 km/h, and hail risk. The catch: the report was not directly accessible today — the underlying page returns HTTP 403, and the content comes from a search summary. This brief therefore carries it as a softer confirmation. It changes nothing: the two directly read portals carry the day on their own. And a trap was caught along the way: an almost word-for-word article about a storm warning for "396 cidades do Paraná" turned out, on direct checking, to be dated Aug 26, 2026 — a week old. Same figures, different day; it isn't used here.

German-Brazilian: the Words of the Semana da Pátria [7]

This week the kids come home with vocabulary that has no clean English equivalent either — Londrina's schools are covering civic sense, history, and the national symbols through Monday, and the north of Paraná is doing much the same across the board. Hasteamento das bandeiras — the flag-raising that opened the week today at 8:00am; a ritual with no equivalent back home, because here it's part of the regular school routine, not a special occasion. Comitiva — the contingent that marches in the parade as a self-contained group; 57 of them are registered in Londrina. Fanfarra — the school marching band of drums and brass, a serious institution in Brazil with its own competitions; 13 are marching on Monday. And Hino da Independência — alongside the national anthem, the second anthem sung today, written specifically for September 7.

The holiday itself commemorates September 7, 1822, the day independence from Portugal was declared. For the eight-year-old and the seven-year-old, that's the most natural bridge from the classroom to the kitchen table: in Germany, there's no day when the entire school marches down the street in uniform — and that's likely to be the exact sentence that needs explaining this week.

The Multivacinação Ends Today — and It's Less Urgent Than It Sounds [4]

The Campanha Nacional de Multivacinação 2026 has been running since August 3 and closes today, September 1. That's what this brief has said for two weeks, and it holds: no extension. The campaign targets reconciling the caderneta de vacinação for everyone under 15; over its course, per today's available reporting, Paraná has administered more than 400,000 doses.

The most important thing today: the deadline isn't one As the campaign ends, the Sesa explicitly clarifies that the salas de vacina at health posts across Paraná stay open and stocked — for anyone who hasn't yet updated the vaccination record. That reverses yesterday's recommendation: there's no reason to head out today with four kids in 18 millimeters of rain. It's Tuesday, September first. Yesterday, this spot carried a piece of advice — and today that advice gets walked back, for a good reason.

The advice was: if anything's still missing from the kids' vaccination records, go today, because today is the last day of the vaccination campaign. The first half of that is still true. The Multivacinação, which began on August third, does close today. No extension has been announced.

But as the campaign wraps up, the state health department clarified something that erases all the time pressure. The vaccination rooms at the health posts stay open and stocked, explicitly for anyone who hasn't updated their record yet. So it's the campaign that ends, not the opportunity. As of today, the vaccination record has no deadline.

And that's genuinely good news, because today would have been the worst day of the week to do it. Two weather portals were checked independently this morning specifically for Ribeirão Claro, and they land on practically the same rainfall total: eighteen point four millimeters from one, eighteen point zero from the other. Four tenths of a millimeter apart. That's about as close as two weather models ever get, and it means this number can be trusted.

There's also a correction in the details. Yesterday, the forecast said it would clear up by afternoon. Today's text says something different: storms in the morning and the afternoon, with the clouds only breaking up in the evening. So anyone thinking they'd just go in the afternoon instead — the afternoon isn't a better window today. Wednesday, on the other hand, shows zero percent chance of rain. The trip to the health post works just as well then, only dry, and with a quieter waiting room.

The national weather service also issued a yellow warning for all of Paraná today, with up to fifty millimeters of rain over the day, gusts up to sixty kilometers an hour, and hail risk. Two things about that: yellow doesn't mean cancel your plans, it means pay attention — secure the patio furniture, don't park under the big tree. And today the report was only reachable through a search summary; the underlying page blocks direct access. So this brief treats it as a softer confirmation. The two directly checked portals carry the day on their own anyway.

Now, the long weekend — because that's the real planning question here. September seventh falls on a Monday this year, which makes for three connected days off, Saturday through Monday. And the two portals paint a remarkably clear picture of which of those days is actually worth planning around.

Saturday is wet — both agree on that, just not on how much. Sunday is the sharpest source conflict in weeks: one portal says zero percent and not a drop, the other says eighty percent and almost twenty millimeters of thunderstorms. Those aren't two similar forecasts, they're two different days. This brief doesn't quietly pick a side in a dispute like that — it prints both numbers — and the practical takeaway is simple: don't lock in plans for September sixth.

Monday, on the other hand, holds steady. Eleven to nineteen degrees, zero percent rain, early fog followed by clear skies — and today, for the first time, independently confirmed by the second portal too. It's the only day of this long weekend both sources list as dry. If an outing happens, it happens on the holiday itself.

And there's finally a time to go with it. Londrina has published the details of its parade, which this brief has been waiting on since August twenty-first: troop review at eight-thirty, parade start at nine, on Avenida Leste-Oeste. Four thousand nine hundred participants are registered across fifty-seven contingents, plus ninety vehicles, fifteen animals, and thirteen marching bands. Free, and streets close all around. For the eight-year-old and the seven-year-old, that's exactly the right pitch. For the two-year-old it'll be loud, and for the baby, too loud — a street parade also means standing around with no shade and nowhere to change a diaper. The quiet alternative on the same day, in the same city: the botanical garden, which is open on holidays too, eight to six, free, with shade and grass.

Anyone driving to Londrina should know there's roadwork on the route in. On the BR-369, there are lane closures near Uraí and Cambé, plus the seven detour points from the widening project between Jacarezinho and Santo Antônio da Platina. Routine work, no full closure, but build in some extra time.
