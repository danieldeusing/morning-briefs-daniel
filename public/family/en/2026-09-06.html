Percent and millimeters don't measure the same thing: The percentage says how likely it is to rain at all — 91% means "as good as certain," but it says nothing about the amount. The millimeters say how deep the water would sit if it stayed put: 1 mm = 1 liter per square meter. So 23.7 mm on the yard is nearly 24 liters per square meter — you won't see it as a puddle, because it drains and soaks in, but every open bucket, saucer, and tarp ends up with water in it afterward. That's exactly why this brief doesn't stop at the percentage. Rule of thumb for the week: percent tells you whether, millimeters tell you how much cleanup waits in the yard afterward.

The figure doesn't add up Today's research again prominently turned up the claim that Paraná had cut dengue cases by "96.1%" — attached to it were the figures 88,488 cases through Aug 19, 2025 against 5,878 through Aug 18, 2026. But those two figures work out to −93.4%, not −96.1% (checked: 5,878 ÷ 88,488 = 6.6% remaining share). The percentage simply doesn't match the case numbers sitting next to it. Where it comes from: an official Sesa announcement from Apr 15, 2026 covering weeks 01–14/2026 — that one went from 52,193 to 2,028 cases, which is exactly −96.1%. A summer-period figure slipped into an August article. The cross-check backs the 93.4%: the bulletin PDF lists 5,962 against 89,829 cases for weeks 01–34 — also −93.4%. Two independent cutoffs, the same result. None of this changes the picture: 2026 is an unusually calm dengue year. Only the circulating percentage isn't part of it.

Respiratory illness, as of week 31/2026. The most recent InfoGripe bulletin found from Fiocruz (reporting week Aug 2–8, 2026, published Aug 13) names four states with a growth signal — Rondônia, Mato Grosso, Bahia, Sergipe. Paraná isn't among them, and for influenza B the bulletin explicitly records a decline in Paraná. What remains open is RSV: "os casos de SRAG por VSR continuam altos em … Paraná …" — RSV cases remain high. In the virus mix of the four weeks prior, RSV accounts for 50.4% of all positive findings, ahead of rhinovirus (29%), influenza B (9.2%), influenza A (6.5%), and SARS-CoV-2 (1.6%). The report is about four weeks old — nothing newer could be found today.

RSV: a cold virus that adults shrug off with a runny nose and cough, but that swells the fine airways shut in infants under six months — like a straw thickening from the inside: the air still has to pass through the same tube, only it's suddenly half as wide. That's why it matters for a one-month-old baby and not for the eight-year-old. It spreads through hands and droplets, not through the air in a room. In practice, for Monday that means: wash hands before anyone handles the baby — and on a festival ground, the baby can stay in the carrier instead of being passed around. That's the whole trick, and it works.
Heads up Tuesday is the yard-round day. After today's 23.7 mm, water is sitting in saucers, buckets, tarps, tires, and gutters — and in two to three weeks that turns into mosquitoes. Tuesday is the only reliably dry day of the week: rain returns Wednesday, and comes back much harder Thursday. Push the round to "sometime this week" and it lands between two rain fronts, or not at all. Second point: anyone driving toward Cornélio Procópio on the holiday is entering the district with Paraná's highest dengue incidence — 232.98 against 29.64 at home, nearly eight times as much. Repellent for the older kids; for the infant, a mosquito net over the stroller and carrier instead — repellent doesn't belong on a baby's skin.

Matsuri finale and parade: why both land on Monday [7][8][9][10][11]

The Londrina Matsuri is in its 22nd edition, running Friday through Monday at Parque Ney Braga. Today is day three, tomorrow the last. Across the four days, the city says, more than 60 program items are spread over two stages; the festival also marks 118 years of Japanese immigration to Brazil and 92 years of Londrina.

What appeals to kids is better documented today than in previous editions: taiko groups (large Japanese drums, including Ryukyu Koku Matsuri Daiko from Okinawa and multiple-time Brazilian champion Ishindaiko), the Yosakoi Soran dance competition, a cosplay competition with a "cospobre" category using simple materials, a games area, and the Beco dos Artistas with handmade items to browse and buy. A dedicated "Espaço Criança" like in 2025 is still not confirmed for this year — the drums, costumes, and games are.

German-Brazilian angle: what September 7 is actually celebrating [8]

On September 7, 1822, Dom Pedro I declared independence from Portugal on the banks of the Ipiranga stream in São Paulo — hence "Grito do Ipiranga," the Cry of Ipiranga. The Museu do Ipiranga stands on that exact spot today, which is why it holds its family festival on Monday of all days. To German eyes, the most striking difference from October 3 is this: here, people march. Despite its name, the desfile cívico-militar is mostly a school event — classes, marching bands, the fire department, scouts, with the "Tiro de Guerra" military-prep group mixed in, and parents lining the street filming. Anyone who grew up in Germany has to reframe this a bit; for the kids, it's simply the loudest and most fun thing the school year has to offer.

Six words for Monday: pátria = fatherland, hence Semana da Pátria, the festival week leading up to September 7. desfile = parade. fanfarra = marching band, usually a school band with drums and brass. hasteamento da bandeira = the flag-raising that opens a desfile. And for the other half of the day: matsuri is simply the Japanese word for festival, taiko the large drum whose beat you feel in your chest. Anyone who knows these words will follow the announcements along the street — and understand, at the cosplay competition, why half the crowd is in costume.

It's Sunday, September sixth, and today is the day you plan nothing. Ninety-one percent chance of rain, nearly twenty-four millimeters, thirteen to twenty degrees Celsius. The forecast text calls it rainy through the day — no gaps to work around. It only clears up after dark.

Back on Saturday's forecast pull, today was only showing about sixteen millimeters. Overnight, that number climbed by more than half.

The holiday, though, is holding. Eleven to eighteen degrees, no rain — and for the first time, both weather portals say so explicitly. The second one simply calls Monday "partly cloudy" with no rain figure at all, while it's calling for ninety percent today, Wednesday, and Thursday. That's six forecast pulls since the first of September, six times the same answer. About as sure as a forecast gets.

And everything this week has to offer falls on that one dry day. The Londrina Matsuri, in its twenty-second edition, runs today and tomorrow from ten in the morning to eleven at night at Parque Ney Braga. Sixteen reais to get in, eight at half price, thirty for parking. Monday at five-thirty in the afternoon, singer Nanase Aikawa closes out the festival.

What's new today is what's actually in it for the kids. The city lists more than sixty attractions across two stages: taiko drumming, including a group from Okinawa and Brazil's reigning champion, a dance competition, a cosplay competition, a games area, and an alley of handmade crafts. A dedicated kids' zone like last year's still isn't confirmed for this one.

That same Monday morning, the big Independence Day parade runs through the same city — troop review at eight-thirty, start at nine, on Avenida Leste-Oeste, and it's free. Catching both means leaving by six in the morning, at eleven degrees, with a one-month-old infant. Honestly, that's a lot. The easier version: leave around eight-thirty, be on the festival grounds by eleven, head home in the late afternoon. For all six of us, that runs somewhere between seventy-eight and ninety-four reais, plus gas for the three-hundred-seventy-kilometer round trip.

Anyone for whom Londrina's hundred eighty-five kilometers is too far has a shorter option: Santo Antônio da Platina, just sixty kilometers away, runs its own parade Monday morning, also free. Only the start time still hasn't been published — anyone who wants to go should ask around locally today.

Today, Londrina also has a car festival with free admission for kids under twelve and a free play area. But it's outdoors, at a racetrack, on exactly the day it's pouring. This time, that's not really a judgment call — it's a no.

Two things on health. Our own area stays calm: a hundred eight confirmed dengue cases in the Jacarezinho region, zero deaths, through epidemiological week thirty-four. And a number making the rounds doesn't hold up. Research turned up, again, the claim that Paraná cut dengue cases by ninety-six point one percent, with roughly eighty-eight thousand cases against just under six thousand right next to it. Do the math on those two figures and you get ninety-three point four percent. The higher number comes from an official April announcement that only covers the first fourteen weeks of the year. Both figures are accurate on their own — they just don't belong together.

On respiratory illness, the latest report available from Fiocruz is about four weeks old, covering epidemiological week thirty-one. Paraná isn't one of the four states with rising case counts, but RSV cases here remain high, and RSV makes up half of all positive test results. For a four-week-old baby, that comes down to one simple rule: wash your hands before anyone holds the baby, and at the festival, keep the baby in the carrier instead of passing it around.

Finally, the week's most important appointment — and it's an unglamorous one. Tuesday is the only weekday that's reliably dry, because rain comes back Wednesday and hits harder Thursday — one portal says fifty-two millimeters for Thursday, the other only fourteen, and the two only agree on the thunderstorms. After today's rain, water will be sitting in saucers, buckets, and gutters, and in two to three weeks that turns into mosquitoes. So: Tuesday, not whenever there's time.

Tomorrow's holiday brief brings the last weather check before the drive — and, if it finally surfaces, the start time for the parade in Santo Antônio da Platina.
