Why the millimeters wobble and the percentages don't: The percentage answers a rough yes/no question — does it rain at all that day? Weather models commit to that early, which is why Thursday has sat at 92–93% for days. The millimeters answer a much finer question: how much water falls exactly over this spot? That depends on whether a storm cell passes twenty kilometers to the east or west — and the model simply doesn't know those twenty kilometers three days out. That's why the Thursday amount swung between 52.3, 48.8, and 42.0mm over two days, while the probability barely moved. Practical takeaway: you can schedule plans around the percentages. The millimeters only answer the question "empty the bucket or check the gutter?" — and the answer is the same at all three values.

The RSV wave is turning — and today that matters for a four-week-old baby [4][5][6]

This is the real news of the day, and it's good news. The latest InfoGripe bulletin from Fiocruz for Epidemiological Week 34/2026 — reporting week Aug 23–29, 2026, data as of SIVEP-Gripe Aug 29 — is four weeks fresher than the status this brief last used, and it flips the picture for the youngest. In the bulletin's own words: "a diminuição dos casos de SRAG nas crianças menores de dois anos se deve a queda das hospitalizações por VSR" — the decline in severe respiratory (SRAG) cases in children under two comes from falling RSV hospitalizations.

The virus mix shows the same shift in numbers. Over the last four weeks, RSV accounts for only 31.9% of positive findings — the bulletin for week 31 still had it at 50.4%. Rhinovirus has moved to the top instead, at 44.3% — the ordinary cold virus — and it's exactly what's driving a nationwide rise among 2- to 14-year-olds, the age group of our two older kids. Influenza A is past its season (3.6%), influenza B sits at 6.9%, and Covid-19 at 2.1%, low in every state.

Where Paraná stands: Three states show elevated SRAG incidence with a growth signal, per the bulletin — Alagoas, Goiás, and Paraíba. Paraná is not among them. It's on the second list: ten states at alarm, risk, or high-risk level, but without growth. And on RSV, the wording now is only that cases in Paraná are "ainda um pouco elevados para essa época do ano" — still a bit elevated for the season. Three weeks ago it read "continuam altos" — simply "remain high." That's the same finding, one notch softer.

What "SRAG" counts — and what it doesn't: SRAG stands for Síndrome Respiratória Aguda Grave, severe acute respiratory syndrome. Someone only gets counted here once they're in the hospital — shortness of breath, needing oxygen, admission. A kid with a runny nose, a cough, and three days on the couch never shows up in this statistic. That's why the curve looks so sluggish even though someone is coughing in every daycare right now: it doesn't measure how many are sick, but how many are hit hard enough to need a hospital. That's exactly why a falling RSV share among under-twos is the number that matters for a newborn — it's the number about severe cases, not about runny noses.
Heads up An all-clear this is not. A falling share doesn't mean zero, and Paraná still sits on both lists — elevated SRAG incidence and an RSV level above what belongs to this time of year. So the same three rules still apply on the festival grounds today, and all three are cheap: the baby stays in the carrier and isn't passed around, wash or sanitize hands before anyone touches it, and keep a distance from kids with obvious colds — two-year-olds with runny noses are unavoidable at a Matsuri, but the half-meter gap between them and the baby isn't. RSV spreads through hands and droplets, not through room air. That's the whole trick, and it works.

School, the holiday, and a German perspective on September 7 [7]

School is back tomorrow. September 7 falls on a Monday in 2026, so there's no bridge day after it — Tuesday runs normally. That's practically relevant because it means backpacks, uniforms, and homework are back on the list tonight, no matter how late we get back from Londrina. The next candidate for a bridge day on the school calendar sits in October; once Paraná's official PDF confirms it, the figure will appear here.

And since today is the day for it: on September 7, 1822, Dom Pedro I declared independence from Portugal on the banks of the Ipiranga stream in São Paulo — hence "Grito do Ipiranga," the Cry of Ipiranga. The Museu do Ipiranga stands on that exact spot today, which is why it holds its family festival on this day of all days. What surprises a German father most isn't the occasion but the form: here, people march, and specifically school classes. Despite its name, the desfile cívico-militar is mostly a school event — classes, marching bands, the fire department, scouts, with parents lining the street filming. Germany's October 3 has nothing comparable; for the kids here, it's simply the loudest and most fun thing the school year has to offer.

Four words for today: independência = independence, the occasion for the holiday. desfile = parade; fanfarra = marching band, usually a school band with drums and brass. And for the other half of the day: matsuri is simply the Japanese word for festival, taiko the large drum. Anyone who knows these four will follow today's announcements — on the street as much as on the festival grounds.

Add it up and that's sixty-five millimeters in three days, against a climatological September average of ninety-nine millimeters for the whole month — nearly two-thirds of a month's rain packed into three days. That's why Tuesday keeps coming up in this brief: it's the last dry day, and after that water sits in buckets, saucers, and gutters, turning into mosquitoes within two to three weeks. The yard round belongs on today or tomorrow, not sometime later.
