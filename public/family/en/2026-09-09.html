The Warning: What "Perigo Potencial" Says — and Doesn't Say [1][2]

INMET has placed a storm warning over all 399 municipalities of Paraná for today, 9:03am to 11:59pm BRT, explicitly including the Norte Pioneiro. The content: precipitation of 20 to 30mm per hour, daily totals up to 50mm, wind gusts between 40 and 60km/h, hail possible. The institute itself rates the probability of power outages and flooding as "baixa" — low. The same warning applies today to a total of twelve states across the South and Southeast.

"Perigo potencial" — why a 50mm warning and a 2mm forecast aren't a contradiction: INMET works with three levels: perigo potencial (yellow, "it could happen"), perigo (orange), and grande perigo (red). Yellow is the lowest. And it doesn't describe a forecast for one location — it describes a ceiling for an entire area. Think of it like a school class where the announcement is: "someone will get an A today." That doesn't mean every kid gets an A — it means it'll happen somewhere in that class. The 50mm is the figure for the hardest-hit cell somewhere between Foz do Iguaçu and Curitiba. What applies to our farm is the point forecast: 2.1mm. Both are true at the same time. In practice, that means: no reason to cancel the day — but a reason not to stand under trees during gusts, and not to schedule the drive back for right at midnight.

The official safety guidance is brief and translates into two sentences for this household: don't stand under trees during gusts, don't park next to power poles or billboards, unplug electrical devices during thunderstorms. Emergency numbers: Defesa Civil 199, Fire Department 193.

This brief's two-portal rule is therefore satisfied — the figures above are as solid as five-day-out daily totals can ever be. The one notable difference: theweather.com puts Saturday's low at 14°C, noticeably cooler than climatempo's 20°C. Dressing warm costs nothing.
Why 13–16°C on Monday means something different for a baby than for us: A five-week-old loses heat much faster than an adult — not because she's more sensitive, but because of the geometry. Relative to her weight, she has roughly three times the skin surface through which heat escapes, and her head alone accounts for about a fifth of that. On top of that, shivering — the way adults generate heat — barely works in newborns. In practice, that doesn't mean "don't go out" on Monday, it means the old rule of thumb: one more layer than the adult is wearing, plus a hat, and in the car, take the thick jacket off before buckling in (thick jackets leave the harness loose) and put a blanket over her instead.

Heads up What's changed since yesterday isn't the number, it's the ground. Yesterday's advice here was: do the yard round today, while it's still dry. That day is gone. Now 177mm are on the way, and the round becomes wet work — it's still worth doing, even more so. Aedes eggs cling to the dry inner walls of saucers, buckets, and gutters and only hatch once water floods over them; from there it takes seven to ten days to become a flying mosquito. This week's rain is therefore the mosquito wave of late September. The window is Tuesday the 15th and Wednesday the 16th: the first two dry days after it, before the next front. Empty saucers, flip over buckets, pull tarps taut, stand up old tires, clear out gutters.

It's Wednesday, September ninth. For the first time in this rainy stretch, there's an official warning over the Norte Pioneiro, not just a forecast from a weather portal. Brazil's national weather agency, INMET, has placed a storm warning over all three hundred ninety-nine cities in Paraná, running from nine oh three this morning until just before midnight, and it explicitly includes our region.

The warning sounds more dramatic than today is likely to feel here. It calls for twenty to thirty millimeters of rain an hour, daily totals up to fifty millimeters, gusts between forty and sixty kilometers an hour, and possible hail. But it's the lowest of three levels, colored yellow, and it doesn't forecast conditions at one spot — it sets a ceiling for an entire region. For Ribeirão Claro itself, climatempo calls for just two point one millimeters at sixteen to twenty-four degrees. Both numbers are true at once.

The morning's second story is quieter but matters more for planning. The rain block hasn't gotten shorter — it's reshuffled. Today turned noticeably drier, down from eight point six to two point one millimeters. In exchange, Thursday and Saturday became the two peaks. Thursday now sits at forty-seven millimeters, and Saturday doubled overnight, from twenty-one point eight to forty-four point two. Wednesday through Monday now adds up to one hundred seventy-seven millimeters. The climatological normal for an entire September is ninety-nine.

There is good news too, and for the first time in a week it has a date. The block ends. Tuesday the fifteenth brings just nine-tenths of a millimeter, and Wednesday after that is dry. Anyone with outdoor plans this week should put them there, not on the weekend. Before that, though, Monday brings a cold snap, with a daytime high of just thirteen to sixteen degrees — the most relevant number of the week for a five-week-old, since newborns lose heat through the skin roughly three times faster than adults do.

This week's pick falls on a Wednesday — tonight. At seven-thirty, the Cia dos Palhaços performs the music-comedy show "Concerto em Ri Maior" at the Teatro Sesi in Santo Antônio da Platina, about sixty kilometers away, indoors, seated, suitable for all ages. The catch isn't the show, it's the drive home: the highway — BR one fifty-three — has seven detour points along this stretch, and the warning window runs until midnight. So: leave early, and head straight back after the show. No price is listed by the source, and the numbers floating around online belong to a different theater in Curitiba.

On health, there's no new number today, and that's good news. Paraná's dengue report for weeks one through thirty-four still stands as the latest: just under six thousand confirmed cases statewide and six deaths, more than ninety-five percent fewer deaths than at this point last year. In our own health region, Jacarezinho, that's one hundred eight cases and zero deaths. The weekly flu-and-cold bulletin for week thirty-five still hasn't come out either, so week thirty-four still applies: RSV is falling, and the common cold — rhinovirus — is taking over, hitting two- to fourteen-year-olds hardest.

One more thing worth mentioning, and it ties into the election. Paraná's health department news page is currently blocking its dengue article, citing the election period. Brazilian agencies are barred from certain public communications ahead of a vote, and portals tend to shut down entire news sections as a result. Practically, that means official health updates could be delayed clear into October. The underlying bulletin PDFs themselves are still coming through fine.

As for the weekend, the honest call is to skip it. The book fair in São Paulo, the Bienal do Livro, runs only through Sunday, and kids under twelve get in free — but four to six hours of driving each way with a five-week-old, on roads facing ninety percent rain odds, into a hall expecting over seven hundred thousand visitors, right in the middle of a cold-virus wave: not worth it. In the core ring, nothing is published for the third time running — confirmed, not assumed.
