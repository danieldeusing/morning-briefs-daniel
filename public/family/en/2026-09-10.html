Red Over Paraná — and the Question of Whether We're Inside It [1][2][3][4][5][8]

INMET has placed a warning at the "grande perigo" level — great danger, the highest of three levels — over Paraná for today, 12:01am to 11:59pm BRT, a jump of two levels from yesterday. By the reports' count, 265 of the state's 399 municipalities are affected. The content: precipitation of over 60mm in a single hour or over 100mm over the course of the day, gusts over 100km/h, and hail. The institute names the risks unusually concretely: "danos em edificações, interrupção no fornecimento de energia elétrica, prejuízos em plantações, quedas de árvores, alagamentos e transtornos no transporte rodoviário" — building damage, power outages, crop damage, falling trees, flooding, road-traffic disruption.

Unresolved Is Ribeirão Claro inside the red warning? The sources contradict each other, and I can't resolve it today. INMET's own warning database (Aviso 28222, fetched directly this morning) lists "Norte Pioneiro Paranaense" in its mesoregion list and explicitly names Jacarezinho and Ribeirão Claro in its municipality list. Three independent reports that read the same bulletin on 09/09 instead list nine mesoregions — Oeste, Sudoeste, Noroeste, Norte Central, Centro-Ocidental, Centro-Sul, Sudeste, Centro-Oriental, RM Curitiba — and the only one missing is the Norte Pioneiro; none of our neighboring cities appears in the published 265-city list. Both are possible: a later overnight expansion (in which case the API applies) or a generous polygon geometry in the raw file (in which case the newspapers apply). Undisputed is the level below: the yellow warning "perigo potencial" applies statewide from Wednesday to Friday and has today and tomorrow as its peak window. In practice, that means: plan today as if the red warning applied — that costs one day indoors and not one cent.
"Tornado-favorable conditions" — why that's not a tornado forecast: The reports speak of "condições favoráveis à formação de tornados" for today and tomorrow. That sounds like an announcement, but it's something else. Picture it like a kitchen where flour, eggs, and sugar are sitting on the table: a cake is now possible. Whether one gets baked, that doesn't say. The "ingredients" here are warm, moist air below, cold, dry air above, and wind that changes direction with altitude. When they line up, a storm cell can start to rotate. That happens a few times a season in Paraná and then hits an area of just a few kilometers — the odds that it's our valley are very small. What follows from that isn't fear, then, but a rule: at the first sign of a thunderstorm, go inside, stay away from windows, and don't get in the car to go "take a look." The gusts over 100km/h are the more likely problem anyway — an ordinary thunderstorm is enough for that.

What 100mm actually does here is documented — though from February 2023, not this week: back then, per Folha Extra, over 100mm fell on Jacarezinho, there were desabrigados in the Comunidade Pedreira, flooded schools and health posts, and the water supply was disrupted; Santo Antônio da Platina reported nearly 40mm overnight and flooding on the BR-153 at the PRF station, with trees down on the PR-439. That's the past, not a forecast — but it's the only honest answer to the question of what the figure "100mm" means for our region. It means: roads closed, power out, schools shut.

For today specifically: don't stay outdoors once it starts; unplug electrical devices and, if possible, the main breaker; don't park next to trees, power poles, or billboards; schedule drives on the BR-153 and BR-369 for the morning gap or push them to tomorrow. Defesa Civil 199, Fire Department 193. No suspension of classes at our schools had been published as of this morning.

For context: the climatological September normal for Jacarezinho is 99.2mm for the entire month — climatempo's five days would be 1.7 times that, theweather's five days 0.6 times. The difference between "extraordinary" and "a lot, but normal" therefore depends entirely on which portal you believe.

Monday is the new problem day. 34.6mm at a 14–17°C daily high — wet and cold at once, and for a five-week-old that's a more unpleasant combination than cold alone, because wet clothing conducts heat away roughly twenty-five times faster than dry clothing. Yesterday's rule of thumb still applies: one more layer than the adult is wearing, a hat, and in the car, the thick jacket off before buckling in. What's new is just this: on Monday, also bring a dry change of clothes, even for a ten-minute trip.

Heads up This week's rain is the mosquito wave of late September — and the window to counter it now has an exact date. Aedes eggs cling dry to the inner walls of saucers, buckets, gutters, and old tires and survive there for months. They only hatch once water floods over them; from there it takes seven to ten days to become a flying mosquito. Between 59 and 170mm of rain by Monday is filling every vessel in the yard at once, right now. The window is Tuesday the 15th, Wednesday the 16th, and Thursday the 17th — the three dry days, exactly within the deadline before the first generation takes flight. Empty saucers, flip over buckets, pull tarps taut, stand up old tires, clear out gutters. After that, it takes ten times the effort.

It's Thursday, September tenth. Yesterday, the lowest warning level covered this region. Today it's the highest. Brazil's national weather service, INMET, has declared a "grande perigo" warning over Paraná — great danger, the top of three levels — running from just after midnight to just before midnight tonight. Two hundred sixty-five of the state's three hundred ninety-nine cities are affected.

The numbers behind it are unusually large: more than sixty millimeters of rain in a single hour, or more than a hundred millimeters over the course of the day. Gusts above a hundred kilometers an hour. Hail. The institute specifically warns of building damage, power outages, crop losses, falling trees, flooding, and road disruptions. On top of that, there's a tornado advisory — conditions favorable for tornado formation over Paraná, today and tomorrow.

And then there's the question this brief hangs on today: are we actually inside the warning zone? The weather service's own database says yes — it lists the Norte Pioneiro region and names Jacarezinho and Ribeirão Claro directly. But three newspapers that read the same bulletin count nine affected regions, and ours is the one missing from their list. Both versions are in the brief today, unresolved, because that's more honest than a guess I can't back up. What isn't in dispute is the level below it, the yellow warning, which has today and tomorrow as its peak window. In practice: plan today as if the red warning applies. That costs one day indoors and not a cent.

The tornado advisory is worth putting in context, because the word lands harder than it's meant to. "Favorable conditions" is like a kitchen with flour, eggs, and sugar out on the counter — a cake is now possible, not guaranteed. And if a storm cell does start rotating, it typically touches down over just a few kilometers. The odds it's our valley are small. The practical rule stays the same either way: at the first thunderclap, go inside, stay away from windows, and don't get in the car to go take a look. The gusts above a hundred kilometers an hour are the more likely danger anyway — an ordinary thunderstorm delivers those.

What a hundred millimeters actually does here is documented, though from February of twenty twenty-three, not this week. Back then, more than a hundred millimeters fell on Jacarezinho: people were left homeless in the Comunidade Pedreira, schools and health posts flooded, water service was disrupted, and a federal highway went under water. That's history, not a forecast — but it's the most honest answer to what that number means for this region.

The morning's second story is the friendlier one. The wet stretch finally has a hard end. Starting Tuesday, three days in a row show zero percent chance of rain — the fifteenth, sixteenth, and seventeenth. Before that, though, five wet days remain, and Monday just doubled overnight, from about seventeen to nearly thirty-five millimeters, with a high of only fourteen to seventeen degrees. Wet and cold together is a rougher combination for a five-week-old than cold alone, since damp clothing pulls heat away far faster than dry. So Monday now calls for a dry change of clothes along, even for a ten-minute errand.

On the rain total, there's a real disagreement to flag. The two weather portals this brief relies on differ by a factor of three for Thursday through Monday: climatempo adds up to about a hundred seventy millimeters, theweather dot com to about fifty-nine. Temperature and rain odds line up fine between them — it's only the amount that splits. For context, the normal rainfall for the entire month of September here is under a hundred millimeters, so believe whichever portal you like, and either way it's a lot.

This week's pick isn't the weekend — Saturday and Sunday alone bring almost eighty millimeters of rain, an indoor stretch either way. It's Tuesday instead, the first of three dry days: laundry outside, kids in the yard, and the piece that actually matters — a walk around the property to empty out anything holding standing water. This week's rain is exactly what wakes up the dengue mosquito by late September, and Tuesday through Thursday is the narrow window to clear saucers, buckets, gutters, and old tires before the eggs already sitting in them can hatch.

On health, nothing new today, in either direction. The Fiocruz flu-and-cold bulletin for week thirty-five still hasn't appeared — a third straight day with no update, on what's normally its release day. So the data on file is now twelve days old: the common cold virus, rhinovirus, leads at over forty percent, RSV is fading, and severe cases keep climbing among two- to fourteen-year-olds — exactly our two older kids' age range. Dengue remains the good story: a hundred eight confirmed cases and zero deaths in our own health region since the year began.

In the core ring, nothing's been published for a fourth straight check — hardly a surprise given the forecast. The São Paulo book fair wraps up Sunday and stays a pass, now for a fourth reason: hours of driving each way, a storm warning on the roads, and a packed hall in the middle of a rhinovirus wave. Mark the calendar for the next long weekend, October twelfth and thirteenth. And the next brief should bring the storm's aftermath — and, hopefully, the missing flu bulletin at last.
