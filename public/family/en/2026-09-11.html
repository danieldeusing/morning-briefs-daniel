Yesterday's Warning Resolves — Downward [1][2][3]

Yesterday, this section carried an open contradiction: INMET's own database listed Ribeirão Claro in the red warning, while three newspapers listed nine mesoregions and left out the Norte Pioneiro of all places. Today the case is clear, and it resolves in the reassuring direction. INMET's active warning file carries Aviso 28227 for today, Friday. Its mesoregion list names "Norte Pioneiro Paranaense," and its municipality list includes Ribeirão Claro (geocode 4121802). So we're inside it — but at the "perigo potencial" level, color code yellow, the lowest of three. What that level means: 20 to 30mm of rain per hour, or up to 50mm over the day, plus gusts of 40 to 60km/h. That's a strong thunderstorm, not a state of emergency.

The three INMET levels — what they mean in practice: Think of it as a traffic light for weather. Yellow ("perigo potencial") means: it might get rough, plan around it — laundry in, drive in the morning if you can, kids indoors during lightning. Orange ("perigo") means: it will get rough, expect disruptions — power out, wet roads, branches down. Red ("grande perigo") — yesterday's level — means: damage is likely, not just possible, and plans get canceled. The jump between levels isn't in the chance of rain, it's in the intensity per hour: 20–30mm/h at yellow, over 60mm/h at red. The difference is between a full rain barrel and a street turning into a stream.

And that now lines up with the statewide reports that clashed with it yesterday. Per Brasil de Fato, Paraná's Defesa Civil is calling today's storms the "tempestades mais intensas do ano" — the most intense of the year, with "chuva intensa, vendavais, tempestades severas, tornados." Read the same report's regional breakdown closely, though, and the highest alert level sits over Oeste, Sudoeste, and Campos Gerais; the Norte runs there as high risk — one tier behind. That's exactly what the yellow INMET warning says too. The two classes of sources no longer contradict each other; they're describing the same tiering.

That the warning is meant seriously shows in the west. Wednesday evening and overnight into Thursday, two tornadoes touched down there — in Francisco Alves (Wed., ~5pm) and Espigão Alto do Iguaçu (overnight into Thu., ~12:40am), with damage to school buildings and fields; no injuries were reported. The Defesa Civil coordinator on it: "O cenário previsto, que é o cenário do El Niño, já está trazendo impactos no estado." Both towns are over 400km west of us, right in the zone with the highest level. For us, that's not a forecast, it's the reason the state agency has been this loud this week.

For today specifically: schedule drives on the BR-153 and BR-369 for the morning — seven detour points and lane closures have been running there for weeks anyway. At the first lightning and thunder, go inside and away from windows, unplug devices. Defesa Civil 199, Fire Department 193, EPR emergency line 0800 277 0153. No suspension of classes at our schools had been published as of this morning.

Health: EW 35 Is Out, Paraná Isn't on the List [6][7]

Respiratory: the bulletin that had been missing for three days has appeared. On Sep 10, 2026, Fiocruz published the InfoGripe status for Epidemiological Week 35/2026 — reporting week Aug 30 to Sep 5, 2026. The finding for us is the best possible one: five states are flagged at warning level with a rising SRAG trend — Goiás, Maranhão, Mato Grosso, Mato Grosso do Sul, and Rio de Janeiro. Paraná isn't one of them. Nationwide, 2026 has totaled 147,868 SRAG cases so far, of which 79,761 (53.9%) tested positive in the lab for a respiratory virus. For this region, that means: no acute respiratory alert.

SRAG: Síndrome Respiratória Aguda Grave — the Brazilian umbrella term for a respiratory infection severe enough to land someone in the hospital. It isn't a disease of its own but a threshold: flu, RSV, rhinovirus, and COVID all count once someone is admitted for it. Think of it like the water level at a gauge — what's measured isn't which stream fed it, but how high it stands. "Five states with a rising trend" means: the gauge is rising there. Not here.

What the bulletin says about age and virus breakdown from EW 35 isn't available to me; the last read status on that remains EW 34/2026, with rhinovirus at 44.3%, RSV at 31.9%, and a growing signal among 2- to 14-year-olds — the age range of our two older kids. Two wet days are two indoor days: wash hands before any contact with the baby, no kisses on the face with a runny nose, ten minutes of cross-breeze per room even in the rain.

Dengue: unchanged, and that's good. Sesa's bulletin overview again lists the Informe Epidemiológico No. 26 from Sep 1, 2026, as the latest issue today — so the status still stands at Epidemiological Weeks 01–34/2026: statewide 5,962 confirmed cases and 6 deaths (versus 141 deaths in the same period last year, −95.7%), in our own 19th Regional de Saúde Jacarezinho 108 confirmed cases and zero deaths. Heads up The window against the mosquito wave has shrunk from three days to two. Aedes eggs cling dry to the inner walls of saucers, buckets, gutters, and old tires and survive there for months. They only hatch once water floods over them — after that, it takes seven to ten days to become a flying mosquito. The 60 to 83mm expected by Monday is filling every vessel in the yard at once, right now. Yesterday, by contrast, three dry days stood on the calendar; Thursday Sep 17 has today slipped to 40% / 4.0mm. That leaves Tuesday the 15th and Wednesday Sep 16 — both confirmed by two portals, both still within the hatching deadline. Empty saucers, flip over buckets, pull tarps taut, stand up old tires, clear out gutters. And wherever it's possible already this afternoon: before, not after.

It's Friday, September eleventh. Yesterday, this brief carried a question without an answer: is Ribeirão Claro inside the red storm warning or not? Today it's answered, and the answer is the friendly version.

Brazil's national weather service names our town directly today. Ribeirão Claro is on the municipality list, and the Norte Pioneiro region is on the regional list. But the level is "perigo potencial" — potential danger, the lowest of three, color-coded yellow. In practical terms: twenty to thirty millimeters of rain an hour, or up to fifty millimeters over the day, plus gusts of forty to sixty kilometers an hour. That's a strong thunderstorm. It is not a state of emergency.

That also brings the statewide reports back into line with what was contradictory yesterday. The civil defense agency is calling today's storms the most intense of the year across Paraná. But read their regional breakdown closely, and the highest alert level covers the west, the southwest, and the Campos Gerais region. The north runs as high risk there — one tier behind. That's exactly what the yellow warning says too. The sources no longer disagree; they're describing the same tiering.

That the warning is still meant seriously shows in the west. Two tornadoes touched down there Wednesday evening and overnight into Thursday, in Francisco Alves and in Espigão Alto do Iguaçu, damaging school buildings and fields. No injuries were reported. Both towns sit over four hundred kilometers west of us, right in the zone under the highest level.

The morning's second story is a good one. The weekend has gotten noticeably drier overnight. For Friday through Monday, climatempo now expects just under eighty-three millimeters — yesterday it was a hundred thirty-seven for those same four days. That's a forty percent drop. Monday sees the biggest swing: from nearly thirty-five millimeters down to seven point six. Yesterday's problem day is gone. It stays cold, fifteen to seventeen degrees, but the unpleasant wet-and-cold combination is off the calendar.

And the disagreement between the two weather portals, which stood at a factor of three yesterday, has shrunk to just one point four. On temperature, rain probability, and — most importantly — the dry days, the two line up cleanly. This brief's two-portal rule is fully satisfied today, not just half satisfied like yesterday.

Today itself brings seventeen to twenty-eight degrees and six point nine millimeters — and the rain isn't due until evening. That makes this afternoon the last window outdoors before the weekend, and it becomes today's pick. One hour in the yard, doing two jobs at once. First, storm prep: clear anything loose, don't park under trees, charge flashlights, fill up water. Second, half the mosquito round: empty saucers, flip over buckets, stand up old tires. Whatever's already dry today won't even fill up over the weekend. One caveat: the other portal already sees thunderstorms during the day. So if it starts by noon, that portal was simply right.

Saturday and Sunday are then spoken for. Together, just under seventy millimeters, with a storm system Sunday night. It's an indoor weekend, and for the fifth time running, nothing's been published for the core ring — hardly news given this weather. What's new, though, is something ongoing right on the doorstep: Jacarezinho is offering free guitar workshops for children and teens. The report doesn't give dates or how to sign up — that's worth a phone call.

On health, there are two quiet updates. The Fiocruz flu bulletin, missing for three days, is finally out. Five states are flagged with a rising trend in severe respiratory cases — Goiás, Maranhão, Mato Grosso, Mato Grosso do Sul, and Rio de Janeiro. Paraná isn't one of them. And on dengue, the good standing holds: in our own health region of Jacarezinho, a hundred eight confirmed cases and not a single death.

One thing has gotten worse, though, and it's about the mosquito. Next week's dry window has shrunk from three days to two. Thursday now sits at forty percent rain instead of zero. That leaves Tuesday and Wednesday — falling exactly within the deadline before the eggs this weekend's rain floods can hatch into the first generation. Two days, both confirmed by both portals.
