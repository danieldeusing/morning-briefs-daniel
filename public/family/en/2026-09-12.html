Why "97% rain" and "38mm" are two completely different statements: Probability answers "does any water come down at all?"; amount answers "how much?" Picture two watering cans: both will be tipped over with 97% certainty — nobody just knows whether an espresso cup or a bucket is hanging over the flowerbed. That's exactly why two weather services can agree at 90 vs. 97% and disagree at 13 vs. 38mm: the first number comes from the frequency of comparable weather patterns, the second from the precipitation model itself, which is the much harder part. Rule of thumb for the yard: under 5mm you barely notice, around 20mm the ground gets saturated, over 30mm a day leaves puddles that stick around — and those are exactly the dengue story further down.

Indoors, but Not Uneventful

68 millimeters over two days means the weekend's program happens in the living room, and it's worth shaping that actively instead of letting it just happen. Four kids between six weeks and eight years old on a heavy-rain day work better with a routine than with improvisation.

What works today
  • Build a fort — blankets, chairs, clothespins. Keeps the two older kids (8 and 7) occupied on their own for an hour; the two-year-old gets to live inside it.
  • The kitchen as activity, not chore: dough works in any weather and kneads fine with four hands.
  • Reading aloud in Portuguese — the rainy day is the week's most honest PT lesson, because nobody runs off.
  • Ten minutes of cross-ventilation per room, even in the rain. Four people in closed rooms at 20°C is exactly the setup where respiratory viruses travel.
What doesn't work today
  • Any car trip that can wait. After 38mm the side roads are flooded, and the BR-153 stretch toward Ourinhos is slow anyway because of the duplication work.
  • Playground, yard, dirt road — not just wet, also slick on the slope.
  • The Aedes yard round. Today it's pointless: whatever you empty is full again within the hour. That's a Tuesday job.
  • Big plans for tomorrow morning. The window is short; a half-hour walk is a win, an outing it will not be.

Respiratory: level elevated, trend is not. For early September, Fiocruz's InfoGripe analysis lists eight states in the "alerta, risco ou alto risco" group for severe respiratory infections — Paraná is one of them, alongside Amazonas, Mato Grosso do Sul, Minas Gerais, Rio Grande do Sul, Rio de Janeiro, Santa Catarina, and Sergipe. The word that matters is the qualifier: these eight sit without a rising trend, unlike the three states that are climbing.

Level vs. trend — why "alert level" isn't panic here: SRAG is Brazil's catch-all threshold for a respiratory infection severe enough to require hospitalization — flu, RSV, rhinovirus, and COVID all count. The bulletin reports two different things about it, and they're easily confused. The level is the water mark on the gauge: high, because we're at the tail end of the Brazilian winter. The trend is whether the gauge is still rising. For Paraná, the answer is: high, but no longer rising — winter is winding down. In practice that means, for the six-week-old, not "alarm" but the same as last week: wash hands before every contact, no kisses on the face with a runny nose, and anyone with a cold keeps their distance instead of just being considerate.
Heads up The window against the next mosquito generation is Tuesday and Wednesday. Aedes eggs stick dry to the inner walls of saucers, buckets, gutters, and old tires, and survive there for months. They only hatch once water floods over them — after that it takes seven to ten days to a flying mosquito. This weekend's 68.2mm is filling every container in the yard at once. Going after it today is pointless, since everything refills instantly. Tue the 15th and Wed Sep 16 are both forecast at 0% and 0.0mm and land exactly inside the hatching deadline: empty saucers, flip buckets, tighten tarps, stand up old tires, clear gutters. Caveat: this run only has climatempo confirming the two dry days — the second portal didn't reach that far out.

The one task this rain creates has a deadline.
