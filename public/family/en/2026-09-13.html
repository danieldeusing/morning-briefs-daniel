Three Days at Warning Level — Today the Last One [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8]

What ran over Paraná this weekend wasn't just bad weather, but a continuous official warning spanning three days — and the Norte Pioneiro was included on the worst day. On Friday, Sep 11, INMET issued the red level: grande perigo for thunderstorms, valid through 11:59pm, with rain above 60mm per hour or 100mm in a day, gusts above 100km/h, and hail. Three coastal municipalities were exempted — 181 of the state's 184 municipalities were under the warning.

On Saturday, Sep 12, it dropped a level but widened its reach: Orange ("Perigo") for 207 to 208 municipalities from midnight to 11:59pm, with 30–60mm/h or up to 100mm in a day and gusts of 60 to 100km/h, plus a yellow level for 318 municipalities. The regional list names the Norte Pioneiro explicitly, and both Jacarezinho and Ribeirão Claro appear in the full municipality lists. Both windows have expired — for today, this run couldn't document any new warning.

Heads up Paraná has registered its thirteenth tornado of 2026. The most recent hit the rural zone near Goioerê, Rancho Alegre d'Oeste, and Quarto Centenário on Friday — that's the western part of the state, roughly 400km from here, so not immediate neighborhood. The same week saw damage in Francisco Alves (Sep 9) and Espigão Alto do Iguaçu (Sep 10, two schools destroyed), hail and downbursts in Sabáudia, Maringá, and the Vale do Ivaí. Simepar and INMET confirmed the events; no deaths, but significant property damage. The point here isn't panic, it's calibration: when INMET writes "red" in a situation like this, it isn't rhetorical red.
What INMET's traffic-light colors actually mean: The warning levels don't measure how hard it's raining, but how likely damage is. Yellow means "could get unpleasant" — broken branches, overflowing storm drains. Orange means "expect outages" — power cuts, blown-over billboards, flooded underpasses. Red means "people can get hurt": roofs, trees, flying debris. Like a fever thermometer for a child: 37.5 is worth noting, 39.0 changes the day's plan, 41.0 means the car and the hospital — the same thing, just a different order of magnitude. Practical translation for the yard: from Orange on, nothing light stays loose outside; from Red on, stay away from windows and large trees.
Open conflict The sources disagree about today — not on whether, but on how bad. The meteorological outlook in the regional press says the system is pulling away on Sunday: still rain in all regions, heaviest in the northern half and the east, but with lower thunderstorm risk and concentrated in the morning. climatempo, by contrast, writes literally "Tarde e noite com temporal" for Ribeirão Claro — storms afternoon and evening — and has raised the amount to 44.6mm at 99%, plus a banner reading "Muito forte – acima de 50 mm." The third portal, tempo.com, sits in the middle: 19/13°C, 90% rain, "chuva moderada" — and explicitly no storm warning. So two out of three see a wet but not dangerous day. This brief's two-portal rule is satisfied for temperature and rain probability (90 vs. 99%, 19–20°C high); it isn't for the question of "afternoon thunderstorms or not." Planning recommendation: treat the day like a rain day and skip the drive to Londrina — then the difference between the three readings is practically moot.

What's missing: SIMEPAR, Paraná's state weather service and, per this brief's source ladder, the first-choice address, keeps answering with HTTP 403 — again today. And: this brief has no measured rain reading. The roughly 83mm floated for Saturday and Sunday is the sum of two forecasts (38.2mm from yesterday's retrieval plus 44.6mm today), not a gauge reading. What actually fell, this run can't document.

Indoors Today — and Why the Aedes Yard Round Falls on Wednesday

A fourth straight rain day is harder on four kids between one month and eight years old than the first one was. Yesterday's list of ideas still stands; what's new today is mainly that the morning no longer works as an escape hatch.

What works today
  • A change of scenery indoors. After three days in the same living room, a rearranged room does more than a new game — mattress on the floor, table pushed aside, done.
  • The rain itself as the activity: get out a measuring cup, measure the rainfall in the yard, and compare it to the forecast's 44.6mm. For an eight-year-old and a seven-year-old, that's real science.
  • Reading aloud in Portuguese — the fourth day running, and that's exactly the point: repetition beats variety for language acquisition.
  • Ten minutes of cross-ventilation per room, even in the rain. With RSV levels elevated and an infant in the house, stagnant indoor air is the real risk, not the damp air outside.
What doesn't work today
  • Londrina. 110km on roads that have been draining water since Friday — for a free rock festival whose family-friendliness nobody has confirmed.
  • Yesterday's morning walk. The forecast text has changed; that window no longer exists.
  • The Aedes yard round. Pointless today: whatever you empty is full again within the hour.
  • Any drive on the BR-153/BR-369 that can wait — duplication work with detours, extra slow in the wet.
Heads up The window against the next mosquito generation is Tuesday and Wednesday — this time with extra emphasis. Aedes eggs stick dry to the inner walls of saucers, buckets, gutters, and old tires and survive there for months. They only hatch once water floods over them; after that it takes seven to ten days to a flying mosquito. So roughly 80mm of rain over two days fills essentially every container in the yard at the same time — a synchronized start for the entire next generation. Wed Sep 16 sits at 0% / 0.0mm and lands cleanly inside the hatching deadline: empty saucers, flip buckets, tighten tarps, stand up old tires, clear gutters. That Paraná's dengue situation is currently historically good (right) doesn't change any of this — it's good because exactly this was done everywhere.

Dengue: the Bulletin Is Finally Readable — and the Number Is Good [9]

Update since Sat The Sesa Paraná Informe Epidemiológico "Arboviroses Urbanas" No. 26, released Sep 1, 2026, was still behind a 403 yesterday. Today it opened — so instead of an April snapshot, the brief finally has the current season tally.

For weeks 01 through 34 of 2026, Paraná counts 5,962 confirmed dengue cases. In the same window of 2025, it was 89,829. That's a decline of 93.4% — the figure the bulletin itself lists in its comparison column. On deaths: 6 this season against 141 last year, minus 95.7%. Of the 5,962 cases, 4,960 are classified as autochthonous, meaning acquired within the state itself; that works out to an incidence of 41.71 per 100,000 residents.

Even more telling than the year-over-year comparison is the movement from bulletin to bulletin. Informe No. 25 from Aug 18 put weeks 01–32 at 5,878 cases and 5 deaths. Two weeks later, in No. 26, it's 5,962 and 6 — meaning 84 new cases over two weeks, a rise of 1.43%, and one more death. That's not a swelling curve, it's a nearly flat line. The 2025 outbreak is finally history as a comparison benchmark.

Why a good dengue season still isn't the all-clear: Dengue runs in annual waves, and the wave follows the rain, not the calendar. The mosquito needs standing water to reproduce — and the shift from dry winter into wet spring is exactly the moment it restocks. What we're seeing right now is therefore the end of a very quiet season, not proof that the next one will be quiet too. Like wildfire risk: that there was no fire this summer says nothing about next summer — it only says the fire prevention worked. For dengue, fire prevention means no standing water within a hundred meters of the house. The number 5,962 is the result of that work, not a substitute for it.

Worth noting for a household with an infant is a second sentence: rhinoviruses rank as the second-leading cause of death among children under two — meaning the ordinary cold, not just the dramatic diagnosis.

A later bulletin (EW 33, released Aug 27) reports rising case numbers only in Alagoas, Mato Grosso, and Roraima; Paraná isn't among the growing states there. That status, though, comes only from search-engine summaries — the bulletin itself wasn't opened this run and therefore stands as context, not as a documented figure.

Heads up August health figures don't steer September. For an infant a good month old, that changes nothing in practice, because the precautions are the same whether the level is "elevated" or "unknown": wash hands before every contact, no kisses on the face, anyone with a runny nose keeps their distance instead of just being considerate, and the two school-age kids change clothes after school before touching the baby. What this brief explicitly does not do: extrapolate a current trend from a four-week-old bulletin.

It's Sunday, September thirteenth. Paraná has been under an official storm warning for three straight days, and today is the last one.

On Friday, INMET issued the red level — grande perigo, the highest danger tier. A hundred and eighty-one of the state's hundred and eighty-four municipalities were covered, with only three coastal towns exempted. On Saturday it eased to orange, but spread wider — over two hundred seven municipalities, plus a yellow level for three hundred eighteen more. And this time the Norte Pioneiro was named explicitly. So were Jacarezinho and Ribeirão Claro. Both warning windows expired at midnight, and this run couldn't find a new one for today.

How serious this was shows up in another number: Paraná has now logged its thirteenth tornado of the year. The most recent hit Friday near Goioerê, out west, roughly four hundred kilometers from here. Simepar and INMET confirmed it. Two schools were destroyed elsewhere the same week. Nobody died. When INMET writes red in a situation like this, it isn't rhetorical red.

For today, though, there's an open disagreement, and the brief leaves it open on purpose. Meteorologists say the system is pulling away to the east — still rain, heaviest in the northern half, but a lower thunderstorm risk and concentrated in the morning. Climatempo, on the other hand, raised its overnight forecast for Ribeirão Claro from thirty to forty-four point six millimeters and still calls for storms in the afternoon and evening. The third portal splits the difference — ninety percent chance of rain, moderate rain, and explicitly no storm warning. So two out of three see a wet but not dangerous day. In practice, the difference barely matters if you just treat today as a rain day.

That also means yesterday's pick is gone, and it's worth saying plainly. Yesterday's brief flagged Sunday morning as the only outdoor window, because the forecast text at the time promised sun. That text isn't there anymore — now it's already raining by mid-morning. The replacement is better documented than the loss, though: on Wednesday, both portals independently call for twenty-two degrees and zero rain — the same high from two separate models, which hadn't happened all week. That's the day for laundry, for the first real stretch outside, and for the baby, the first day in days with sun on the skin.

Wednesday has a second job, too. Roughly eighty millimeters of rain over two days is filling every container in the yard at once, and mosquito eggs stick dry to the inside of saucers, buckets, and gutters until water floods over them. After that, it's seven to ten days to a flying mosquito. Going after it today would be pointless — everything refills instantly. Wednesday lands right inside that window.

And now, the best news of the day. Paraná's health department finally got its dengue bulletin, Informe number twenty-six, to open today after being blocked yesterday. For weeks one through thirty-four of this year, Paraná counts five thousand nine hundred sixty-two confirmed dengue cases. In the same period last year, it was eighty-nine thousand eight hundred twenty-nine. That's a decline of ninety-three point four percent. Deaths: six this season against a hundred forty-one last year, down ninety-five point seven percent.

Even more telling is the bulletin-to-bulletin movement. Two weeks ago, the count stood at five thousand eight hundred seventy-eight cases. Now it's five thousand nine hundred sixty-two — eighty-four new cases in two weeks, a rise of just one point four three percent. That's not a swelling curve, it's a nearly flat line. Still, the number is the result of the prevention work, not a substitute for it — the wave follows the rain, and we're heading into the wet spring.

On respiratory illness, there's finally a week number today, but with a catch. The freshest InfoGripe bulletin this run could reach covers week thirty-one, from early August, released August thirteenth — a month old. It lists Paraná at alert or risk level, with RSV-driven severe cases in the state still called high. One line is worth flagging: rhinovirus, the ordinary cold, ranks as the second-leading cause of death among children under two. For the month-old baby at home, the data gap changes nothing in practice: wash hands before every contact, no kisses on the face with a runny nose, and the two older kids change clothes after school before touching the baby.
