Cross-Check They agree on whether, not on how much. For Saturday climatempo says 1.9mm, tempo.com says 14mm; for Sunday 5.5mm against 15mm. The tiebreaker is still missing: SIMEPAR, per this brief's source ladder the first-choice address for Paraná, has answered with HTTP 403 for the fifth day running.[4] In practice that means: for planning, the probability is what counts (both above 84% for the weekend — that's enough for "indoors"), but for the question "will the yard flood?" there's no solid answer today.
Percent and millimeters measure two different things: the percentage answers "does it rain at all?" — 84% means that in 84 out of 100 comparable weather setups, this location got wet. The millimeters answer "how much, then?" Picture someone tipping a bucket over the garden: the percentage says how likely they are to do it — the millimeters say how big the bucket is. The difference is practically relevant this week. 1.9mm is a shower that settles the dust and evaporates within an hour. 14mm fills every saucer, bucket, and tarp on the property to the brim — exactly the amount that matters for the Aedes round. Whoever empties before Saturday starts back at zero afterward; whoever starts after is two rain days behind.

Health: Respiratory Illness Eases, Dengue Waits on No. 27 [7][8]

The InfoGripe report for Epidemiological Week 35/2026 (Aug 30–Sep 5, published Sep 10) names five states with alert-level incidence and a rising trend: Goiás, Maranhão, Mato Grosso, Mato Grosso do Sul, Rio de Janeiro. Paraná isn't among them. On the overall picture, the bulletin writes that SRAG cases from RSV "continuam em queda, mas permanecem levemente elevados para esta época do ano" — declining, but slightly elevated for the season. Nationwide, the report counts 147,868 SRAG cases and 6,512 deaths for the current year. The decline fits the season: Brazil's RSV wave runs through the southern winter, May to August, and that's over.

RSV and SRAG — the two terms behind this report: RSV is a cold virus. In adults it causes a runny nose and a cough, nothing more. In an infant, the same virus hits the very smallest airways and clogs them — that's bronchiolitis. The difference is pure geometry: the same amount of mucus that's barely noticeable in a garden hose completely blocks a drinking straw. What makes school-age kids cranky for a few days is a very different matter for a four-week-old baby. SRAG is Brazil's reporting category for "severe acute respiratory illness": it counts anyone admitted to a hospital with breathing distress, regardless of the pathogen. So the curve doesn't show how many people catch something, but how many get hit hard enough to need a hospital. In practice: curve trending down, Paraná in no alert group — no reason to keep the baby away from normal life. The two standing rules remain: wash hands before handling the baby, and a school kid with a cold skips cuddling the baby for these weeks.
