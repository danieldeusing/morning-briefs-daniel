What an Orange Warning Actually Means [1][2][3][12][13]

INMET warning colors: The national weather service ranks storms into three colors — yellow means "danger possible," orange means "danger likely, damage expected," red means "major danger." Like a traffic light at an intersection: yellow means watch out, orange means the car is actually coming now. For orange, INMET names concrete thresholds: 30 to 60mm of rain per hour or 50 to 100mm per day, wind gusts up to 100km/h, and hail risk.

The setup: a cyclone is forming near Uruguay and pushing moisture across southern Brazil. For today, Paraná has one orange and one yellow warning in effect; for tomorrow, four — three yellow and one orange. So the warning is escalating toward Sunday, not easing off.[1]

And it means us — with no softening. Today's orange warning zone is spelled out by name: "O aviso laranja alcança o Norte Pioneiro, Região Metropolitana de Curitiba, Centro Ocidental, Noroeste, Oeste, Sudoeste, Sudeste e Centro-Sul."[12] The Norte Pioneiro is our mesoregion — Ribeirão Claro and Jacarezinho sit inside it. For Londrina, our destination, the same orange warning is additionally spelled out as its own point, valid Sep 19, 00:00 through Sep 19, 23:59, with 30–60mm/h and gusts up to 100km/h.[13] So both the outbound and return legs fall entirely inside the warning window. This is not a distant statewide notice.

So why does the forecast show just 4.1mm? Because area and point answer two different questions. climatempo puts today at 82% with 4.1mm and explicitly places the showers at night ("Noite com pancadas de chuva"), while tempo.com comes in at 60% with the exact same 4.1mm but titles the day "Trovoada" — thunderstorm.[2][3] A warning describes what a single storm cell in the area can do; the point forecast describes what's expected on average for our coordinates. Both are true at the same time. In practice, that means: the day will most likely be usable — but if it does hit us, it'll hit hard and in minutes, not as steady rain. So for outdoors today: don't plan anything you can't get under a solid roof from within ten minutes.

So: leave before 1pm, start back by 5pm at the latest, and arrive while it's still light — instead of staying until 6pm. If a thunderstorm is already sitting over Jacarezinho when you're about to leave, or a cell is sitting on the route: cancel. An afternoon is replaceable, a drive with four kids through hail isn't.

Paraná Drops from "Alerta" to "Segurança" [5][6]

SRAG & InfoGripe: SRAG is Brazil's umbrella category for severe respiratory illness — the cases that land in the hospital, whether flu, COVID, or RSV is behind them. InfoGripe is Fiocruz's weekly early-warning bulletin on it: it doesn't just count, it ranks each state on a traffic-light scale. Like a river gauge — not "how much water fell," but "is it above the mark where you act."

And that traffic light just switched for us. In the bulletin for EW 36/2026 (Sep 6–12), Paraná moved from "Alerta" to "Segurança," with "tendência de Estabilidade para longo prazo" — making it the only southern state at that level. Year-to-date, that's 19,779 SRAG cases against 22,823 in the same period last year, a drop of 13.3%.[5]

Why this is the most important number of the day: there's a one-month-old girl in the house. For infants under six months, exactly this category — severe respiratory infection needing hospital care — is the real risk, not the sniffles. The state stepping down from the alert level doesn't mean "over," but it does mean the pressure on pediatric wards is easing, and the environment is safer right now than it was in August.

RSV: Respiratory syncytial virus. In adults, it's a cold; in infants, it's the most common cause of bronchiolitis — the smallest airways swell shut, and the baby struggles to breathe. That's why RSV shows up in this brief and ordinary colds otherwise don't.

Nationwide, RSV still accounted for 22.2% of positive respiratory samples over the four weeks through EW 36/2026. The same bulletin, though, explicitly places Paraná among the states with low incidence.[6] In short: the virus is still circulating nationally, at a low level here. The practical takeaway stays the boring and effective one — wash hands before touching the baby, postpone visits from anyone with a cold.

Dengue Bulletin No. 27: A Slow Rise, Not a Jump [7]

The current Informe Epidemiológico No. 27 from SESA/PR, dated Sep 15, 2026, covers EW 01–36/2026 and reports 6,060 confirmed cases statewide, of which 5,043 are autochthonous (acquired locally, not brought in), 6 deaths, and an incidence of 42.41 per 100,000 residents.[7]

The direction matters more than the level: compared with EW 34, 83 autochthonous cases were added, a rise of 1.67% in two weeks, and the death toll stays unchanged at six. That's a slow creep at a low level, not an outbreak — the season is only just starting to pick up again.

After the rain This is exactly why it belongs here today: two days of 4 to 11mm, with possible heavy-rain spikes, leave behind new pools of standing water. Aedes aegypti needs roughly seven to ten days of standing water for a full cycle from egg to mosquito — which is why the round is effective done after the rain, not stressful beforehand: saucers, buckets, tarps, gutters, tires, toys left in the yard. Anyone who tips out on Monday what filled up over the weekend breaks the cycle before it starts.
