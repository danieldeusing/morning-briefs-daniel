Two numbers, one direction

The Freelancer Compass 2026 usually gets reduced to a single number — €103 average hourly rate. The more interesting line on the same page is a different one: 49% of respondents report a weaker order situation than a year ago.[1] The survey ran between Nov 17, 2025 and Feb 8, 2026 among roughly 5,400 freelancers and self-employed professionals. This isn't a forecast or a mood question — it's a look back at people's actual order books, and it comes out negative for nearly half of them.

The second number comes from the demand side and points the same way, even though at first glance it sounds like a talent shortage. Bitkom puts the number of unfilled IT positions in Germany at roughly 109,000 — and adds, verbatim: "That's significantly fewer than two years ago, when it was 149,000."[3] The gap is real, but it has shrunk by a good quarter in two years. For the price of an hour of work, what matters isn't the absolute shortage but its direction — and that direction points down.

Honest about how current this is: the Bitkom figure comes from a phone survey of 855 companies with three or more employees, conducted in weeks 20 to 27 of 2025 and published Aug 7, 2025 — roughly a year old, and still the most recent figure the association has published. Anyone citing it should cite the date along with it. In the same report, 85% of companies reported a shortage and 79% expected it to worsen: the shortage is still felt, just in smaller numbers.

What's explicitly not being claimed today: several summaries of the Freelancer Compass state that the average hourly rate fell for the first time since the survey began in 2016.[2] That would be the stronger headline — except this line doesn't appear on freelancermap's own Compass page (checked Jul 25, 2026). It comes from a third-party summary, and the publisher itself doesn't carry it. Until that changes, it stands here as: unconfirmed, not citable. The same source also names 43% of freelancers without a secure order outlook for the coming months — a different question from the 49%, and likewise only backed by a secondary source.

What this means for negotiation. Not: "lower the rate." Rather: the argument shifts from price to commitment. In a market where nearly half report fewer contracts, a longer project costs less negotiating capital than a higher hourly rate. That's exactly why the iSAX listing, with its 48-month term, is structurally more interesting today than any 90-day mandate with five more euros on the table.

Why "49% report fewer contracts" and "121 open listings" aren't a contradiction: Picture two shop windows at the same bakery. The first one displays every cake ever offered — it keeps getting fuller because nothing ever comes down. The second shows how many cakes actually sold this week. The board is the first window: it counts postings, and recruiters rarely take them down again (today: 14 added, none removed). The survey is the second window: it asks people whether they had more or less work over the past year than before. A growing board alongside falling utilization isn't a data error — it's the normal state when many recruiters are all chasing the same small pool of end clients.

Employee: the Swiss threshold is a top-band threshold

Sixteen roles sit in the employee lane today, one of them new: ti&m AG is hiring a Full-Stack Software Engineer for Java and Angular/React in Zurich (Spring Boot, Docker/OpenShift, microservices/DDD), posted Jun 26, 2026, Fit 77. The reason for the comparatively high score is in the fine print of the posting: Claude Code, Cursor and GitHub Copilot are explicitly named as a plus — a direct hit on Daniel's agentic way of working, not just his stack. Still leading, unchanged: ELCA informatique (Senior Java Engineer, focus on Agentic AI, Zurich, Fit 81, posted Jun 9, 2026), Camunda with its "work from anywhere" Kubernetes role (Fit 80, US$ 126,800–190,100 — openly listed) and AXA with its agentic-AI platform in Zurich/Winterthur (Fit 80).

Now for the uncomfortable math. The employee lane sets a mandatory Switzerland floor of CHF 150,000 gross per year. Every pay source available for Zurich today comes in below that, and they disagree with each other too: SwissDevJobs puts the average for software engineers in Zurich at CHF 111,000 and the median at CHF 112,500, with a range of 85,000 to 135,000; Glassdoor around CHF 122,000; ERI SalaryExpert CHF 128,391; Levels.fyi a very wide range of 112,621 to 222,007.[5] The spread between these sources runs 15 to 20% — they're measuring different panels, not different markets. No single point value can be derived from that.

What can be derived is the direction, and it's unambiguous: every one of these all-levels figures sits below CHF 150,000. Even the senior average, for eight-plus years of experience, lands around CHF 144,400 — also below it. This brief's CHF 150k threshold, then, isn't a market-average threshold but a top-band one: it filters for the upper end of Zurich's distribution. As a filter, that's the right call — it keeps the lane selective — but it also explains why so little survives in this lane, and why none of the three Swiss entries today comes with a posted figure. The CHF bands on the board are researched estimates and therefore carry an asterisk; swissdevjobs.ch again answered with HTTP 403 today and couldn't be cross-checked.

Why CHF 150,000 isn't just "€150,000 with a different symbol": a Swiss gross wage and a German gross salary don't describe the same thing. In Germany, the employer pays half of health, long-term-care, pension and unemployment insurance on top of the stated gross; in Switzerland, employees buy their own health insurance as a private flat premium — it never shows up on a payslip. Add to that Zurich's noticeably higher cost of living. The net effect is still usually positive, but "CHF 150,000 in Zurich" in real terms lands closer to a solid German senior salary than to one-and-a-half times it. That's why this lane's threshold is set deliberately high: it's meant to mark the real jump, not the exchange rate.

Two side notes. Smartsheet openly lists €87,500–112,500 for its Forward Deployed AI Engineer role in Munich — the low end sits below Germany's mandatory €100k floor, only the upper band saves the listing. Grafana Labs is the only employer in the field with posted bands for Germany-remote (€109,000–137,000 and €109,709–131,651) — both above the threshold, both with Go rather than Java as the core language. In the intriguing lane, JetBrains (Principal Forward Deployed Engineer, Fit 82) remains the only entry above 80.
