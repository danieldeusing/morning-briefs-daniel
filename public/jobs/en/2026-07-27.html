What the market says on the rare occasion it talks

Almost every agency withholds the price. That's exactly why the small minority that states it is the only hard pricing information this board produces at all. Since July 1, 19 different freelance entries in the archive have disclosed their own compensation. Two of those are annual salaries (Experis, "up to €80,000/year," temporary staffing), one comes from Italy and is an offshore outlier at €230/day. That leaves 16 solid price points, twelve of them tagged Java, Spring Boot, Angular, or TypeScript.

The board's default assumption for unstated projects is €90–115/h. That's 29% above the observed median at the low end and 44% above the observed high at the top.

The four data points above €80/h across the whole July archive aren't Java full-stack: Franklin Fitch paid €700–850/day (€88–106/h) for a Senior DevOps Engineer in Berlin, sms Consulting €90/h for Terraform/Kubernetes — though that one requires German SÜ2 security clearance, which locks it out for a Brazilian residence anyway — ProTIP paid €680/day for Python/FastAPI/Go at DB Systel, and today Randstad pays €85/h for Python & AI in Darmstadt. The pattern is consistent: above €85/h in this archive, it's infrastructure or Python/AI, not Java full-stack. On visible price, Daniel's primary stack is the weaker of the two.

Why stated rates run systematically lower — agency margin and temp staffing: Almost none of the names in the table is the actual client. FERCHAU, Randstad, Experis, Right People, Optimus are agencies: they hold the contract with the end client and are looking for someone to execute it. So the rate in the listing isn't what the project costs — it's what reaches you after the agency's margin is deducted. Think of a resold concert ticket: you only ever see the one price, never both. Temporary staffing (Arbeitnehmerüberlassung, or ANÜ) is the sharper version: you're not contracted as a company, but "loaned out" temporarily as a person, and you're employed under labor law by the staffing agency. That's tightly regulated, caps the rate in practice, and is why Experis lists an annual salary instead of an hourly rate. The practical takeaway: a stated rate of €70 doesn't mean the project is worth €70 — it means someone sits between you and the budget.

Fairness requires the other direction too: whoever pays €120/h has no reason to write that in the listing — stating a price is itself a signal, usually a signal of a tight budget. So the twelve rates are probably the lower end of the distribution, not its middle. Even so, the finding stays uncomfortable: across four weeks and 39 to 150 entries per day, not a single Java project that talked about money reached €90/h. Combined with the Malt anchor of €699/day ≈ €87/h for 8–15 years' experience, a fairly clear order emerges: observed agency prices 70 · platform benchmark 87 · self-reported panels 102. This profile's €100 target isn't a standard price in the agency market. It's a price that has to be negotiated around the agency — or earned through the niche that carries the premium.

60 listings, zero dropped — and why that isn't a good sign

Monday's pull brings 60 listings: 39 freelance, 16 employee, 5 intriguing. After yesterday's 49, that looks like a recovery. A closer look at the file shows something more precise: all 49 of yesterday's entries are back today — byte-for-byte identical, right down to the wording of the score rationale. Eleven entries are new, none has disappeared.

Byte-identity is the decisive finding. A freely worded rationale like "stack 40 (Java+Spring/Spring Boot+…) + loc 20 + rate 13 + intrigue 5 + track 10 = 88" never comes out word-for-word twice from a genuine re-scoring. So these 49 records were carried over, not freshly gathered. That's how the process is designed — the aggregator receives the prior day's file to deduplicate across days — but it has a consequence worth knowing when reading the board: how long an entry has sat on the board says nothing about how long the listing has been open. It only says how long nobody has removed it.

A two-week look at the archive confirms the pattern: from Jul 15 to Jul 21 the board grew from 61 to 150, and on every single day all of the prior day's entries were carried over — fully byte-identical on four of those days. The streak broke only twice, on Jul 22 (150 → 69) and Jul 26 (121 → 49) — exactly the days the sweep itself faltered. Structurally, the board is a reservoir: it accumulates until a coverage failure drains it.

Employees: the Swiss threshold isn't a market average

16 employee entries, still led by ELCA informatique in Zurich (Senior Java Engineer, focus on agentic AI, Fit 81, posted Jun 9, 2026), followed by Camunda's Kubernetes role (80) and its Analytics role (74). Four entries sit in Switzerland: ELCA, Eraneos Zurich (69), LGT Private Banking (58), and a Wavestone freelance mandate in Zurich (60). None of them states a figure.

That's worth a reality check, because this lane's mandatory threshold for Switzerland is CHF 150,000 a year. lohnanalyse.ch puts the Swiss median for "Senior Software Engineer" at CHF 109,000, within an overall range of CHF 75,600 to CHF 200,000.[8] So the threshold sits 37.6% above the median and squarely in the upper half of the surveyed band. It's not a claim on the average — it's a claim on the upper market segment, which explains why the employee lane is so thinly populated and why the CHF bands on the board consistently carry an asterisk.

A second, noticeably higher figure circulates for the same title: a Glassdoor-derived average of around CHF 130,000 nationwide and CHF 141,000 for Zurich. The site itself returned HTTP 403 on direct access; the figures come only from a search-engine summary. They're listed here as a directional signal, not an anchor — the roughly 20% gap between the two sources is too large to silently average.

Median, average, and why the gap here is 20%: The median is the person right in the middle: line up every Senior Software Engineer in Switzerland by salary, and the person in the middle earns CHF 109,000 — half earn less, half earn more. The average instead divides the total sum by the headcount, and a single top earner on CHF 300,000 pulls it noticeably upward while leaving the median untouched. For salaries, the average is therefore almost always higher than the median. On top of that comes the question of who's even counted: a wage statistic captures every reported salary, while a self-reporting platform skews toward younger, well-paid city dwellers who bother comparing salaries in the first place. Two different populations produce two different numbers — and neither one is wrong because of it.
