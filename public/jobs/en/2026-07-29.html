An EU tender that's looking for exactly one person

For the first time, the order of magnitude is right

This brief's TED lane has so far produced exactly one result: tenders whose subject matter fit and whose eligibility check couldn't be cleared. govdigital requires €5 million in annual revenue from AI software development each. Magdeburg University Hospital requires two references at hospitals with at least 300 beds plus ISO 27001 and ISO 9001. The Hessian Center for Data Processing tendered application development in Java, .NET, and Angular across five lots and €113.64 million this spring — with minimum staffing of 6 to 30 developers per lot. Stack match everywhere, no bid anywhere.

Today the counter-case is here. The Oberfinanzdirektion Baden-Württemberg (Landeszentrum für Datenverarbeitung, Karlsruhe) published a notice for „Dienstleistungen TCK Testautomation" on Jul 24, 2026, TED ID 512867-2026, Official Journal S 141/2026. Open procedure, main CPV 72262000 (software development), secondary CPV 72254000 (software testing) and 72266000 (software consulting). Two lots, and — this is the decisive sentence — one external person per lot. Volume: 160 person-days in the 2026/27 period, then 240 person-days each through 2029/30. Base term eight months, up to three annual renewals through Jun 30, 2030, contract start approximately Nov 1, 2026. Submission deadline Aug 25, 2026, 11:00 CEST, bidder questions by Aug 13, binding period 59 days, procedure language German, submission electronically via the state's procurement platform.[1] Client, title, both deadlines, and all three CPV codes are independently confirmed on the state's official procurement platform (procurement ID CXUEYY6YT21S02EH), which also serves as the submission channel there.[1a]

What's notable is what the notice does not say. The eligibility criteria listed in the mirror include no minimum annual revenue, no minimum headcount, no required number of reference projects, no ISO certificate, no security clearance, and no mandatory on-site presence. What's required, it says, is one line: „Vorlage einer Bankbestätigung als Dritterklärung oder alternativ Vorlage eines Nachweises einer bestehenden Berufs-/Betriebshaftpflichtversicherung". The mirror explicitly lists the procedure as suitable for small businesses, freelancers, and sole proprietors.

But this one-line reading doesn't survive a cross-check. The document list on the official procurement platform carries, for the same procedure, among other things „C1b_Liste Referenzen_Testautomation" (a subject-specific reference list), „C1a_Wertungsmatrix_Interview" (i.e., an interview stage that appears nowhere in the notice), a separate „C3e_Eignungsmatrix" beyond the simple self-declaration, and „C3b_Muster_Einwilligungserklärung Zuverlässigkeitsüberprüfung Fremdpersonal" — which directly contradicts the claim of „no security clearance."[1a] Whether these are hard exclusion criteria or optional forms can't be told from the outside: the full texts sit behind the participation registration. What's certain is only that the real eligibility bar is probably higher than the mirror's one line. The verdict therefore reads ⚠️ direct — pending the documents: the order of magnitude (one person per lot) fits for the first time, but the eligibility depth is unverified.

Eligibility check vs. contract award: a public tender runs two checks back to back. First eligibility: is this bidder even allowed to play — is it financially stable, does it have staff, has it done comparable work before? Only after that comes the award: whose bid is best on substance and price? Like club sports — first the medical check, then the game. For a one-person business, almost everything is decided at the medical check: if it says "minimum revenue at double the contract value" or "three comparable references," the game is over before quality or price even come up. That's exactly why the content of the eligibility section decides whether you're even allowed to compete — and that's exactly why the question of whether this procedure really only asks for a liability policy, or additionally a reference list, an eligibility matrix, and a reliability check, isn't a formality. It's the whole decision.

Four caveats, and they're meant seriously. First, the TED full text wasn't readable today: the detail page delivers a JavaScript shell with no server-side HTML, and the PDF pull returns an empty document body — the same failure as on Jul 27. Volume, lot count, the staffing figure, and the eligibility line all come solely from the notice mirror ausschreibungen-deutschland.de, which itself lists the TED ID and Official Journal number; only the client, title, deadlines, and CPV are confirmed on the official portal. Second — and this is the most serious point — that very document list on the portal shows that the notice's one-line eligibility probably understates the requirements: a reference list, an interview scoring matrix, an eligibility matrix, and a reliability-check consent form appear there as separate documents. What they actually require is visible only to those who register to participate — and that registration is the first step, not the bid itself. Third, the stack is unsubstantiated — for a procedure in the tax-administration IT space, Java is plausible, but plausibility isn't proof. Fourth, the time math also needs a sober look: 240 person-days per year works out to about 20 days a month, so effectively full-time utilization per lot over four years.

Residency as a filter — in both directions today

The board sorts by professional fit, but today's most effective filter isn't a technology, it's a registered address. Four patterns show up in the board simultaneously today.

German residency mandatory. Three listings require a security clearance tied to residency and consequently fail hard: nemensis is looking for Java/Spring Boot/Camunda in Düsseldorf with a 90% remote share — professionally one of the strongest stack matches of the day (30 of 45 stack points), but only Fit 43 because of the SÜ2 condition. Levaro (AWS/Kubernetes/ArgoCD) lands at 37, a British listing with active SC clearance at 21. In these cases, the algorithm sets both location and track to zero; the entries stay in the board only for transparency.

German residency excluded. Hays is looking for a „Nearshore AI Engineer (Wohnsitz nur außerhalb DE)" — 100% remote, start immediately, „6 MM+" term. Requirements are Python with FastAPI and async patterns, OpenAI SDK, LangChain and LangGraph, PostgreSQL, REST and event-driven architectures, CI/CD and Docker — and explicitly familiarity with GitHub Copilot, Codex, and Claude Code. No hourly rate, no posting date on the page. Fit 73, and the location component earns a full 20 points here, because the condition that excludes elsewhere is a prerequisite here.[4]

Brazil explicitly allowed. Oscilar is looking for a Sr./Staff Backend Engineer for an AI-powered risk platform, with Java and Kafka at its core, and lists Brazil alongside the US, Canada, and Poland as an allowed remote location. Fit 69 — held back not by the stack, but by the fact that the US employer misses the employee lane's geography criterion (DE/CH).

And the twist: the only freelance entry today whose rate reaches the Swiss target band — Wavestone, Zurich, Cypress/Java/Spring/Angular, CHF 140–170/h* — requires „Remote nur innerhalb der Schweiz." The board's best price sits behind exactly the door that residency keeps shut. Fit 60.

Five entries via temporary staffing. Yesterday there were two, today five: Computer Futures/SThree (Java/Spring, 100% remote, Fit 68), nemensis (Lead Backend Python, 65), SThree Germany (Lead DevOps Hamburg, 60), Bankpower (Java insurance Cologne, 60), and SECUSTAFF (Test Automation Berlin, 58). All five carry the ANÜ marker in the board, all five get only 7 of 10 track points. The discount is too small: for twiceD, an ANÜ contract isn't a project, it's employment with a middleman.

ANÜ (temporary staffing) and SÜ2: in an Arbeitnehmerüberlassung, the agency hires you and loans you out to its client — legally the agency is your employer, but day to day the client directs your work. Like a loan player in soccer: the club pays your salary, but you train with the other team. What gets paid for is one person's time, not one company's deliverable — a German employment contract, German social insurance, and the client holds the right to direct your work. For a German GmbH whose managing director sits in Brazil, that's the wrong contract type, no matter how well the project fits. SÜ2 is the second tier of Germany's state security clearance — the authority vets the person and their environment before granting access to classified material. The three listings turn that into a residency condition themselves: they explicitly require residency in Germany. For an applicant living in Brazil, that ends the process before the first conversation.

Practical consequence: the only pattern that turns Brazilian residency from a disadvantage into a feature is called nearshore — and two of the 17 new entries are built exactly that way. Besides Hays, there's ProTIP for DB Systel: Python, FastAPI, and Go, 100% remote over a 24-month term with a total of 20 on-site days, capped at €680/day.[5] One states no rate, the other pays no more than the market. What they offer isn't money, it's the only contract form that doesn't turn residency into a disqualifier.

It's Wednesday, July twenty-ninth. For the first time since this column started tracking European software tenders, one of them actually matches the size of shop we're looking for: it wants exactly one person per lot.

Every previous check of a European tender ended the same way — the subject matter fit, but the eligibility bar didn't. Five million euros in minimum revenue at one, two hospital references with at least three hundred beds plus two ISO certifications at another, six to thirty developers per lot at the biggest one. Today brings something different.

A regional tax authority in Baden-Württemberg published a notice on July twenty-fourth for what it calls TCK test-automation services. It's an open procedure, filed under software development. There are two lots, and each one calls for exactly one outside contractor. The volume is a hundred sixty person-days in the first stretch, then two hundred forty person-days a year after that, with renewal options running through the middle of twenty thirty. The bid deadline is August twenty-fifth, eleven in the morning Central European Summer Time.

What stands out first is what's missing from the notice. There's no minimum revenue requirement, no minimum headcount, no set number of reference projects, no certificate, no security clearance, and no on-site requirement. All that's asked for, on paper, is one thing: a bank confirmation, or proof of existing professional liability insurance. The notice even flags the procedure as suited to small businesses, freelancers, and sole proprietors.

But that one-line reading doesn't survive a closer look. The state of Baden-Württemberg's own procurement portal — the same site bidders submit through — lists a much longer document set for this same tender: a reference list for test automation, a scoring matrix for an interview, a separate eligibility matrix, and a template consent form for a background check on outside personnel. That last one directly contradicts the claim that no security clearance is required. What those documents actually demand is visible only to bidders who register to take part. So the real eligibility bar is probably higher than that one line suggests, and the verdict here is: promising, but pending the paperwork.

Four caveats go with that. First, the official EU tender text still wasn't readable today — the detail page is a JavaScript shell, and the PDF download comes back empty, so the volume, lot count, and staffing figure all come from a notice-mirror site rather than the primary source. Second, that document list just mentioned suggests the real bar is steeper than advertised. Third, nobody knows yet what TCK stands for or which technologies the work actually requires — that's buried in the service description that only comes with the full tender package. And fourth, two hundred forty person-days a year works out to roughly a full-time commitment per lot. Anyone who wants to bid needs to pull the documents first and get their questions in by August thirteenth.

The second story today is about addresses. The most powerful filter on the board isn't a technology, it's a home address — and it cuts both ways. Three listings require German residency because of a security-clearance requirement. One of them would otherwise be one of the strongest matches of the day — Java, Spring Boot, and Camunda, ninety percent remote — but that single condition drops its fit score all the way down to forty-three.

Another listing demands exactly the opposite. Staffing firm Hays is hiring a nearshore AI engineer, and the job title literally says residency outside Germany only. It's fully remote, starts immediately, runs at least six months. The requirements are Python with FastAPI, the OpenAI SDK, LangChain and LangGraph, Postgres, Docker — and explicit experience with GitHub Copilot, Codex, and Claude Code. Its fit score is seventy-three. The condition that disqualifies elsewhere is a requirement here. A third listing names Brazil by name as an approved work location: Oscilar, building a risk platform in Java and Kafka.

Switzerland delivers the punch line. The only freelance listing whose rate actually hits the Swiss target band — a hundred forty to a hundred seventy francs an hour — requires remote work from inside Switzerland only. The board's best-paying gig sits behind the exact door that residency keeps locked.

There's also a pattern that's growing: five listings now run exclusively through temp-staffing agencies, up from two yesterday. Under that setup, the agency hires you and loans you to its client — it's paying for one person's time, not a company's deliverable. For a company run out of Brazil, that's simply the wrong contract structure, no matter how well the project fits otherwise.

Here's where the numbers land. Eighty-seven listings: sixty-three freelance, seventeen full-time, seven intriguing. Seventeen are new, none dropped, and the board has now grown for four days straight. Ten entries state a rate themselves, four of them freelance: sixty to seventy, seventy-five, eighty-five, and a maximum of six hundred eighty euros a day. The average freelancermap reports for Java freelancers in its own directory is eighty-seven euros an hour. Not one stated rate reaches it. And the board's top entry is now on its twenty-second day: the telematics mandate with a fit score of eighty-eight, hand-checked again today, still open — its project start is this Saturday. Tomorrow's Thursday pull will show whether the board just keeps growing.
