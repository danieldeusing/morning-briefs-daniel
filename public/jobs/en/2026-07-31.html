The rate falls one percent, income falls twenty-one

Today's rate research delivers the first genuinely new number in days — and it's more uncomfortable than it looks. The publisher of the DACH freelancer study reports an average hourly rate of €103/h for 2026, down from €104/h the year before, and calls it the first decline since the series began. In the same breath, it reports average monthly income of €6,653, down from €8,432 — a decline of roughly 21%.[1]

Together, these two numbers are the real story. A price that gives up 1% doesn't explain a 21% income loss. Arithmetically, the rest can only come from billed volume: reading the €6,653 as monthly revenue, that's roughly 65 billed hours a month at €103/h, against roughly 81 the year before at €104/h — about −20%. That's arithmetic derived from the two study figures, not a figure the study itself states, and it only holds if both numbers refer to the same population and basis.

Rate versus utilization: the rate is the price per hour; utilization is the number of hours that actually get paid. Think of a taxi: the per-kilometer price can stay the same, and the driver still earns half as much if he spends half the day waiting at the stand. Negotiations almost always focus on the price, because it's the number in every listing. The money gets lost through waiting time — the weeks between two mandates. Practical consequence: a mandate at €95/h over twelve months beats one at €115/h over four months, once you count the gap that follows. That's why, as utilization falls, contract length in board entries matters more than the rate band itself.

And the board's own rates confirm the finding from below. Exactly two of the 19 freelance entries state compensation themselves: ProTIP ProjektService for DB Systel at up to €680/day (roughly €85/h, 24 months, 100% remote with 20 on-site days across the entire term) and OC Recruitment at a stated €75/h for five months of AI platform hardening and AWS migration. Both sit below the €90/h median that the same source reports for software and web development — the bottom band of its specialization breakdown, while SAP/ERP leads at €120/h.[2] The €75 case visibly drags down its fit score: 3 of 15 rate points, overall fit 58 despite a solid cloud match.

Of the 32 entries, 6 in total state compensation themselves — besides the two freelance cases, those are Camunda (US$126,800–190,100), Grafana Labs twice (€109,000–137,000 and €97,034–116,441), and Smartsheet (€87,500–112,500). One (JetBrains) states nothing at all; the remaining 25 bands are a researched benchmark and carry the asterisk accordingly. Today, 1 of 32 entries carries a real publication date.

The ifo employment barometer rises in July to 93.0, up from 92.2 — cuts are slowing, but that's not growth.[3] Job postings sit 39% below the spring-2022 peak, salary growth slid from 3.4% (March) to 2.8% (June), staffing agencies posted 1.1 million ads in the second quarter, 12% fewer than the year before[4], and industry is shedding roughly 15,000 jobs a month[5]. One confirmed deadline, one unconfirmed stack

Yesterday this space carried a tender whose subject matter was, word for word, this profile's own core stack — and which was missing, of all things, the bid deadline. Today brings the mirror-image case: a notice with a cleanly confirmed deadline and plenty of lead time, where the subject matter instead stays in the dark.

The Federal Office for Procurement of the Federal Ministry of the Interior (Beschaffungsamt des BMI) is tendering the „Weiterentwicklung – eID Testbed." Confirmed from the notice: CPV 72000000 (IT services, consulting, software development), place of performance Bonn, procedure type open procedure under the UVgO, tender type national, submission deadline Aug 21, 2026. That's 21 calendar days from today and well past this lane's five-business-day rule — the first tender find in weeks where the deadline isn't a disqualifying factor.[9]

UVgO and "open procedure": public contracting authorities in Germany award contracts under two rulebooks. Above the EU thresholds, European procurement law applies, with mandatory publication in the EU database; below that, the Unterschwellenvergabeordnung (UVgO) applies — a national, leaner procedure. Think of the difference between an international tender and a notice pinned to the agency's bulletin board: the same fairness logic, less formalism. For a one-person business, both facts are actually good news: a UVgO procedure is, by definition, below the EU threshold, so the volume is capped on the upside, and "open procedure" (Öffentliche Ausschreibung) means anyone can bid directly — no pre-screening round, no consortium requirement. The price: such procedures don't appear in TED and can only be found via the federal and eVergabe portals.

What's missing is anything technical. The accessible overview page lists neither a scope description nor an estimated contract value, no publication date, no contract term. So it's not verified whether Java, Angular, or applied AI are required — the subject area (eID, i.e., the electronic identity credential, plus "testbed") sits close to the IAM and OAuth2 experience in this profile, but "sits close" isn't a stack match. Also unconfirmed: minimum revenue, headcount, references, certifications — and the question of a security clearance. On a BMI procedure for identity infrastructure, that last point is the condition this case is most likely to fail on, since a clearance requiring German residency conflicts with a Brazilian residence.
