Average vs. median for hourly rates: The average adds up all the rates and divides by the count; the median is the rate exactly in the middle once you line everyone up in order. Like ten people in a bar: if a millionaire walks in, the average net worth jumps dramatically, the median barely moves. For rate benchmarks that means: a handful of specialists at €200/h pulls the average up without the typical Java freelancer earning a cent more. The survey cited today phrases its €95/h as "charge an hourly rate of" — whether it means average or median isn't stated clearly. So the number appears here as an order of magnitude, not a target.

Sunday's plan was to pull the tender documents today. The attempt failed: the direct link to the documents returns HTTP 400. They're apparently only reachable through a logged-in session on the procurement portal. Anyone who wants to see the eligibility thresholds has to register there first — which makes that the first concrete work step of the week, not a reading task.

CPV code and "open procedure": A CPV code is the Europe-wide product number for public contracts — like the category in a classifieds listing, only binding. 72* means IT services, 72230000 specifically "custom software development," 48* is packaged software. Because every agency has to assign this code, all of Europe can be filtered by it — the code is this track's search grid. "Open procedure" means: any company may submit a bid directly, with no pre-selection round. That sounds like a low bar, but it's only the entry ticket — the eligibility criteria (minimum revenue, headcount, reference projects, certificates) still get checked, just later, at the bid stage. For a two-person operation, that's the real question, and it appears in none of the five notices.

The shortage is a year old, the decline is six months old

Two figures shape how people talk about the German IT job market, and both are routinely cited without a date. The first: roughly 109,000 missing IT specialists in the German economy. It comes from an industry-association release dated 08/07/2025, based on phone interviews with 855 companies, and was already a decline from 149,000 two years earlier at the time.[2] No newer figure exists as of today — the number that's used in job postings and recruiter emails as evidence of a candidate's market turns a year old tomorrow.

The second: demand for highly qualified IT experts dropped by nearly 20%, according to an industry analysis dated 02/13/2026 citing the same underlying data.[3] Both are true at the same time, and the contradiction is only apparent: the gap is structural — too few people with the right skills overall — while current hiring appetite is cyclically collapsing. A company can be permanently unable to fill a position and have just decided not to fill it for now.

Two mechanisms sit behind this, both named in the same analysis: simple coding tasks are moving to AI assistants, so entry-level hires happen less often; and even officially posted positions frequently stay unfilled for months.[3] The second mechanism is directly visible on this board: 14 of the 82 listings state a project start in their title that has already passed — four of them from January and February. A tender whose start date is six months in the past and that's still online is exactly the kind of listing this sentence is talking about.

SAP has frozen most hiring and non-essential travel to fund a major AI investment.[4] Bosch is cutting roughly 22,000 jobs in its German supplier division; the agreements run through 2029 to 2032, so this isn't a headline event but a downsizing programmed over years.[5] For Aumovio, Continental's spun-off automotive division, another cut figure is circulating; it couldn't be verified against a primary source today and so appears here without a number. The sober takeaway for this profile: the German corporate job market isn't a growth area as a buyer on the current horizon, and this brief's second track — public-sector clients — has just put five deadlines on the calendar.
