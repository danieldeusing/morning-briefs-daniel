The average falls, and Java sits below it

For the first time in this series, there's a price anchor that names not just a level but a direction. The Freelancer-Kompass 2026 — DACH-wide, 5,412 respondents — reports an average hourly rate of €103/h against €104/h the prior year, nominally one percent less and in real terms considerably more. More important than the value is the intent behind it: 62% plan no price increase for 2026, 9% want to actively lower their rates.[2] Seven in ten freelancers in the German-speaking region are entering the year with an unchanged or lowered price.

The second figure from the same survey is the uncomfortable one. Reported monthly income falls from €8,432 to €6,653 — roughly 21% less.[2] The hourly rate gives up one percent, income twenty-one. The difference isn't the price — it's utilization.

Rate times utilization: The hourly rate is the price on the tag; utilization is how many hours anyone actually buys. Like a restaurant: the price per dish can stay the same, and revenue still halves if only half as many guests show up. Running these two figures from the survey against each other — €6,653 divided by €103/h — gives roughly 65 billed hours per month, or about 40% of full utilization. Caution with this division: the survey doesn't say whether "monthly income" means revenue or profit, or whether both figures come from the same subsample. The number works as a sense of scale for the problem — not as a measured utilization rate.

Java sits below this average, not above it. For its own Java directory, freelancermap cites €87/hr[1] — and freelancermap supplies 80 of the 96 entries on this board. The two figures are explicitly not methodologically comparable: €103/h is the survey average across all freelancers in all industries, €87/hr is the directory average of one platform for one technology. As a directional signal it still stands: specializing in Java carries no premium in these surveys.

The board confirms this wherever it names prices. Seven of the 96 entries carry pay without an asterisk, but only three of those are hourly rates: ProTIP ProjektService at up to €680/day, roughly €85/h; OC Recruitment at €75/h for an AI-platform project in Darmstadt; Right People Group at €70/h for an Angular mandate. All three fall below the lower end of the researched band of €90/h and below the Java-directory average of €87/hr. The other four are annual salaries (three at Grafana Labs, one at Anthropic in US dollars for San Francisco). The sober finding: every real hourly rate on this board sits below the target mark — the asterisked bands say what this profile should be charging, not what's being paid here.

The Freelancer-Kompass 2026 is a platform survey (n = 5,412, fielded late 2025 to early 2026), not an official statistic; cited here via a trade-article summary, not from the study PDF. freelancermap's Java directory reflects self-reported rates from registered freelancers, i.e. asking prices, not closed deals. A second Java-specific source was unreachable today (stay-digital.de returned HTTP 403), so the comparison rests on a single source.

One candidate is still worth running the numbers on. Notice 86435-2026 from Bavaria advertises exactly what this profile builds: Java software development plus web-based frontends in the identity and access management space. Confirmed are a minimum duration of 2 years + 2 × 1 year option and a volume of 640 to 2,560 person-days; the contract value is redacted under § 39(6) VgV.[5] Not confirmed is the deadline — and the notice numbering suggests it has long since passed: TED numbers sequentially, and in early August 2026 the current notices were already in the 506,000 range. A number around 86,435 corresponds to a publication around February 2026; with a typical bid deadline of around 30 days, it would have closed roughly five months ago. This dating is an estimate from the number, not a fetched date — enough to keep this tender listed as not biddable, not enough to declare it dead.

Person-days (PT) in a tender: A person-day is one workday of one person — the unit public IT framework contracts use to measure volume, because the agency doesn't yet know at the start how much it will actually draw down. Like a punch card at a swimming pool: a minimum is guaranteed, a maximum is capped, and you draw down in between. For the question "can twiceD do this?", exactly that range is the point. 640 person-days at roughly 200 working days a year is about 3.2 person-years, spread over four years that's 0.8 full-time positions — solo-feasible. 2,560 person-days is 12.8 person-years, spread out 3.2 full-time positions ongoing — more than one and a half times what a two-person shop can staff.

The partner-gap math follows from that, independent of the deadline. For a tender of this size, a partner would need to supply roughly 2 additional full-time positions over four years for the Java-backend and frontend roles, plus presumably financial-standing and public-sector reference proof. The contract value stays redacted; scaling the volume for reference against the only confirmed day rate on the board (€680/day at ProTIP) lands between roughly €435,000 and €1.74 million. That's an illustration using a third party's day rate, not a value stated by the contracting authority — but it still works as a calibration: at this scale, a partner doesn't just supplement, they carry weight.

Bosch cuts 950 software jobs in Germany

This profile's niche premium — IoT, smart home, automotive software, with researched €100–130/h* on the board and six entries today — has one main buyer, and it's shrinking. Bosch is cutting roughly 1,200 software-development jobs by end of 2026, 950 of them in Germany; affected is the Cross-Domain Computing Solutions division with about 20,000 employees. The reasoning is the actual news for this brief: progress toward fully automated driving is coming "significantly more slowly than expected."[4]

The date matters for context. The announcement is from 01/19/2026, so it's half a year old — not a fresh event, but an ongoing reduction with a year-end target. It's also not the same figure as the 22,000 jobs reported here yesterday: the 22,000 cover the entire German supplier industry over timelines through 2029–2032, the 1,200 is the software-specific slice through end of 2026. Both figures stand side by side without contradicting each other — only the smaller one is relevant here.

The practical consequence is a shift, not a shutdown. What's shrinking is software development for the vehicle itself; what's growing are AI-adjacent roles at the same companies — described in a market analysis reviewed today as "the most reliable source of new requisitions right now."[7] That shows up directly on the board: the six automotive and IoT entries sit at Fit 43 to 66, come exclusively from staffing agencies, and none of them reaches the hit band. The niche premium of €110–140/h* thus remains a target mark without a taker today.

The Bosch figure comes from a business-media report dated 01/19/2026, not a company statement; a more current status on the reduction's execution was not checked today. The market analysis on AI-adjacent roles is an industry blog and carries no number here, only framing.
