The shortage shrinks — and the price falls anyway [1][2][3][4]

Two figures from two surveys seem to contradict each other. Bitkom reports 109,000 unfilled IT positions in the German economy; 85% of the 855 companies surveyed cite a shortage, only 4% speak of an oversupply. On average, it takes nearly eight months to fill an open IT position, and roughly every fourth company gets practically no applications for its postings.[1] At the same time, the average freelancer hourly rate in the DACH region falls from €104 to €103/h.[3] Record demand and falling prices at once — that sounds like an error in one of the two figures.

It isn't. Two things resolve the contradiction. First, the direction: the 109,000 isn't a record high, it's a marked decline — two years ago the figure was 149,000, roughly 27% more. Second, the date: the Bitkom report is from 08/07/2025, so it's a year old and describes the state of 2025. It's the most recently published figure in this series, but it says nothing about August 2026.

Open positions ≠ price: "Unfilled positions" is a count of permanent-hire postings that nobody has accepted. That measures how hard it is for a company to find someone — not how much it's willing to pay. Like a restaurant that's been looking for a cook for months and still posts the same wage: the vacancy proves nobody will come at that price, not that the price is rising. For the freelance market, there's an added wrinkle: the two sides draw on different budgets — the open permanent role is paid from the headcount budget, the project mandate from the project budget. If one gets cut and the other stays unfilled, shortage and price pressure exist side by side.

And now the part that touches this profile directly. The Freelancer-Kompass 2026 breaks the average down by specialization, and the order is uncomfortable: SAP & ERP €120/h, data & analytics €100/h, IT infrastructure €95/h — and software & web development €90/h.[2] The IT median across all specializations sits at €95/h. Java-specifically, freelancermap's own Java directory cites €87/h (cited yesterday). The second Java anchor drops out today — and that's the more honest news: the GULP figure of €82/h, cited yesterday via the search index, couldn't be reproduced on a direct fetch of the same page. It now shows an average Java hourly rate of €97.73, explicitly dated October 2024.[4] A figure that diverges by roughly €16 from the same URL on the same day isn't a reliable anchor; the €82/h is therefore no longer carried here as a lower bound, and the October 2024 figure is too old for a price read today. What's left is a narrower but sturdier band of €87–90/h for exactly the technology that 40 of the 99 entries here require.

The board confirms this band from its own data. 71 of the 99 entries carry a researched euro hourly band. Of those, 39 start at €85/h, 23 at €90/h — and only eight at €95/h or higher, six of those at €100/h. By far the most common band is €85–110/h (34 entries). The only three entries with a confirmed hourly rate sit at €85, €75, and €70/h, still unchanged below everything listed here as a target mark.

Soberly summarized: the €100/h target mark for standard Java/Angular mandates sits above every median that can be documented today — above the IT median (95), above the specialization figure (90), and well above the Java band (87–90). That doesn't make it wrong; the upper ends of the board's bands reach up to €130/h, and niches like SAP show that specialization does carry a premium. But it means that €100/h in this market is a negotiating position above the average, not a floor the market hands out on its own.

The Bitkom figures come from the press release on the study "The Labor Market for IT Professionals" (855 companies with three or more employees), dated 08/07/2025 — they're carried here explicitly as the prior-year status, not as a 2026 figure. The Freelancer-Kompass 2026 is a platform survey by freelancermap, not an official statistic; the specialization breakdown is cited via the operator's blog, the €103 average via its press release from 03/17/2026 (the PDF itself couldn't be machine-read; the figure comes from the search-index rendering of the same release). The GULP figure reflects self-reported rates from registered freelancers, i.e., asking prices, not closed deals — and it's unstable: a repeat fetch of the same URL on 08/05/2026 returned €97.73 dated October 2024 instead of the €82 previously cited via the search index. Neither value is therefore carried here as an anchor.

UBS frees up 3,000 Swiss jobs — and IT goes first

The highest bands on this board have been Swiss for days: eight entries in Zürich, Bern, and St. Gallen — seven of them with hourly bands between CHF 120 and 180/h*, plus the Zürich employee position at ELCA at CHF 140,000–190,000 a year*. For this profile, they're unreachable, because every single one requires presence in Switzerland. Starting in the second half of 2026, they'll also face a supply problem.

UBS is cutting roughly 3,000 jobs in Switzerland as part of the Credit Suisse integration, announced with a target horizon of end of 2026; roughly half of that was completed by February 2026. The remaining part falls mainly into the second half of 2026, with some spillover into early 2027 — and, according to a Swiss IT job portal's market analysis, this time it hits primarily the area that has carried the integration so far: IT. As CS customers migrate onto UBS systems, that's exactly where the redundancies pile up.[5]

The more telling figure is a different one. The bank's external IT workforce has already fallen from 33,013 at the end of 2022 to roughly 16,000 — a halving that preceded the cuts to permanent staff.[5] That's the number that describes the freelance market in Zürich, not the 3,000.

External roles and their "internalization": Large banks cover peak load with external contractors — freelancers and consultants who don't show up in the corporate headcount statistics. Like temp labor in a warehouse before Christmas: staff up first, then cut first when volume drops. When an official cost-cutting round comes, some firms then move remaining external roles onto their own payroll (that's called internalization) — the corporate headcount can even rise while the external market shrinks. For a freelancer, that means: the cuts hit earlier and harder than the headline about eliminated jobs suggests.

What comes onto the market as a result is exactly this board's profile. The analysis lists the skills becoming available as: Java, C#/.NET, Kafka, MuleSoft, Oracle/SQL Server, Azure, and AWS.[5] That's no foreign niche — it's the ingredient list of the Zürich freelance mandates already on this board, plus two legacy stacks.

The point isn't that the premium disappears. It's that the people who make up this supply already bring the location advantage that makes the Swiss entries here unreachable in the first place. All eight CH entries require presence or residency in Switzerland — three purely on-site, two "remote only within CH," one at most three home-office days a week, plus two hybrid Zürich setups. Their location component in the fit score accordingly ranges between 3 and 12 out of 20 points. Whoever becomes available in Zürich starting this fall gets the full 20.

On top of that, a second salary source confirms yesterday's. SwissDevJobs puts Senior Software Engineer in Zürich, evaluated across more than 750 listings with salary data, at a range of CHF 95,000–140,000, with an average of CHF 117,100 and a median of CHF 115,000.[6] Yesterday, a different survey put it at CHF 115,000–145,000. Two independent sources, the same result: the classic Swiss senior band ends below this brief's own filter threshold of CHF 150,000. In Switzerland, the title "senior" alone no longer cuts it — the premium hinges on an AI-adjacent scope.

The IT-specific read on the UBS cuts (IT first, external headcount 33,013 → ~16,000, skill list) comes from a Swiss IT job portal's blog — a secondary source with its own stake in the topic, not a UBS statement; a primary fetch from the bank wasn't done today. The underlying figure of roughly 3,000 Swiss jobs and the roughly 1,500 completed by February 2026 are cross-checked via search snippets from several Swiss media outlets' coverage (including Tages-Anzeiger, Blick); a direct fetch of these articles failed today with HTTP 403 or a redirect loop.
