A tender with no revenue hurdle [1][2]

Public IT tenders trip up a two-person company at almost always the same spot: minimum annual revenue, three comparable reference projects, N staff for role X, ISO 27001. Notice 512867-2026, published 2026-07-24 in Official Journal S 141/2026, demands none of that. The contracting authority is the Oberfinanzdirektion Baden-Württemberg – Landeszentrum für Datenverarbeitung, places of performance are Karlsruhe (Moltkestraße 80) and Stuttgart (Jobstweg 7–9). Sought is "Automation von Testfällen im TCK — Dienstleistungen im Bereich der Testautomation," tendered in two identical lots.

KONSENS and the TCK: KONSENS is the joint IT program of Germany's tax administrations — instead of sixteen state tax offices running sixteen separate tax programs, it's built once and used everywhere. The TestCenter KONSENS is, per the notice, "the central quality gate in the release management process": before a new version goes out to the tax offices, it has to pass through here. Like the final check at the end of an assembly line — nothing leaves the building without going through it. What's tendered isn't the testing itself but the automation of the test cases: checks someone clicks through by hand today are meant to run as scripts going forward.

Partner gap: none on the formal criteria. The single eligibility requirement is "submission of a bank confirmation as a third-party declaration, or alternatively proof of existing professional/business liability insurance" — for a German GmbH with running business liability cover, that's one signature, not a consortium. The gap sits in capacity: 240 person-days per lot and renewal year exceed one person's annual output (roughly 220 available working days). A partner is needed for: a second lot (a second full-time hire with a test-automation profile) and for the peak load from 2027/28. Still open: (1) the technology stack, (2) whether the two places of performance require on-site presence. Both are answered in the tender documents.

Why the volume is the core question — and not the paperwork. 160 person-days over an eight-month base term is roughly 20 days a month: that's not a side gig, it's a full-time position for the length of the contract. From 2027/28 it climbs to 240 person-days a year per lot — more than one person can even deliver in a year, assuming roughly 220 available working days. The assessment follows fairly directly from that: one lot in the base term can be bid solo, and it then effectively replaces project work rather than supplementing it. Two lots, or the renewal years, need a second full-time hire — that's where the only real partner need sits, and it's a capacity problem, not a qualification one. The payoff: a plannable base load with options through 2030-06-30.

And the honest part. The notice text names no programming language and no testing tool — only the three CPV categories. Test automation in the tax-procedure world tends to run on a JVM stack, going by experience, but that's not documented here. Just as undocumented is whether the work may be delivered remotely; two concrete addresses in Karlsruhe and Stuttgart are listed. For a bidder based in Brazil, that's the one point that can sink the deal — and it can only be clarified through the tender documents, which require registration. Eleven business days remain for that.

What 40 years is worth: €16/h [3]

The Freelancer Compass 2026 breaks down the DACH hourly rate not just by specialty but also by age — and that row is the more interesting one for this profile. 20- to 30-year-olds charge an average of €80/h, the 40+ group reaches €96/h. The premium for roughly fifteen extra years in the profession is thus €16/h, a good fifth more. That's real, but it's not a jump into another league: €96/h is still below the €100/h target mark and only one euro above the IT median of €95/h. The Software & Web Development specialty sits below that at €90/h, while SAP & ERP reaches €120/h and Data & Analytics €100/h.

Median versus average: the median is the middle value — half charge less, half more. The average divides the sum by the count and gets pulled upward by a few very high rates. Like the income at a bar: a millionaire walks in, the average jumps, the median barely moves. Here both figures sit side by side — €95/h is a median across all IT freelancers, the €96/h for the 40+ group is an average. That they're nearly equal means: the 40+ age group is roughly the middle of the market, not its top.

The board confirms the order of magnitude once again. 81 of the 113 entries carry a researched euro hourly band. 38 start at €85/h, 35 at €90/h, six at €100/h, exactly one at €95/h and one at €70/h. Summed up: 7 of 81 bands start at €95/h or higher. But more telling than any researched band are the four entries with a confirmed hourly rate — and those read €95/h (Java/Spring Boot with a DevOps and API focus), roughly €85/h (Python/FastAPI/Go, capped at a €680 day rate), €75/h (AWS/AI platform), and €70/h (Angular, Munich, 100% remote). Three of the four sit below the IT median, two below the specialty figure.

The sober reading stays the one from Wednesday, now with an age dimension. €100/h for a standard Java/Angular mandate is a negotiating position above the market average — and age alone doesn't get you there, it gets you to roughly 96. What actually pushes the bands higher shows up in the board's outliers: the six entries starting at 100 are, without exception, specializations — AWS Bedrock and RAG in Frankfurt (€100–130/h*), an auth and security focus, one employer that explicitly advertises "> 15% above market average." Not seniority — niche.
