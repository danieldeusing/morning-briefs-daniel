Three listings for the same role [1][2][3]

Saturday's run brings six new entries, and two of them carry a title that barely existed in Europe two years ago. Camunda — Berlin roots, a fully remote company — is hiring an AI Forward Deployed Engineer, EMEA. At JetBrains, a Principal Forward Deployed Engineer – AI-Native Software Development with fit 82 has held rank two on the whole board for days. And Cognition, maker of Devin and Windsurf, still lists its Deployed Engineer – EMEA at fit 42. Three of nine Intriguing entries, three different employers, one role concept.

Forward Deployed Engineer: A salaried developer who doesn't build on the company's own product but sits with the customer and writes the integration that makes that customer's use case work. Like a kitchen installer who doesn't stand at the factory assembly line but drives to the apartment, measures, cuts, and fits it in — same company, same parts, but the last stretch happens on site. The difference from a classic consultant: they write real code and carry it back into the product. The difference from a classic developer: their success metric is a running customer system, not a merged pull request.

Why this is more than a vocabulary note for twiceD. That exact last stretch is what twiceD sells in its project business: stack knowledge plus fitting it into a specific customer process, billed by the hour. The three listings describe the same activity — just as an employment contract with an annual salary, a base workload, and no need to hunt for clients. This isn't career advice, it's a market observation with a price tag: the role type whose freelance variant is losing workload, per the survey, is being hired on staff by product makers. Notably: Camunda meets all four mandatory criteria of the employee track — EU employer, compensation well above threshold, established company, enterprise AI on the product — yet is still filed as intriguing, with a stack component of 14 out of 45. Camunda's core is BPMN on the JVM, i.e., Java enterprise; scoring 14 there measures the listing's wording, not the technology behind it.

Correction: the base year was wrong [4]

Since July 1, this brief has printed the same comparison in seven issues: the average monthly revenue of a DACH freelancer allegedly fell from €8,432 to €6,653, a drop of 21%. The source was a trade article dated 2026-03-17 citing the Freelancer Compass 2026. The survey operator's own blog states it differently — verbatim: "average monthly project revenue in 2026 falls from €8,022 to €6,653," and adds: "that's a drop of roughly 17 percent versus the prior year."

Why the base year matters more than the percentage: A percentage never describes a state — it always describes a distance from an earlier value. Get the starting point wrong, and the percentage is automatically wrong too, even if the current value is correct. Like a growth mark on a doorframe: if last year's line was drawn four centimeters too high, this year you're not measuring a real decline, you're measuring last year's drawing error along with it. That's exactly the case here: the 2026 value is identical in both sources, only the 2025 mark differs.

Which number applies from today — and why. This brief's source hierarchy ranks primary and operator sources above third-party trade articles. The blog belongs to the platform that runs the survey; the previously cited article only cites it. So from today, this brief uses €8,022 → €6,653, roughly −17%. What honestly stays open: neither side says why the values diverge. A different subsample would be plausible — say, all freelancers versus IT only — but that's not stated anywhere, and this brief won't guess.

The correction changes little about the story, more about the tone. Whether 17 or 21%, the direction holds: revenue is falling by double digits while the hourly rate, at €103 down from €104, is essentially flat. The operator frames that itself: since the survey began more than ten years ago, the rate had never fallen before. The related demand figure, last quoted in this brief in late July, stands unchanged: 43% without secured workload, 49% reporting a worse pipeline than the prior year. The bottleneck sits in billable hours, not the hourly price — and for the software & web development specialty, the same survey states €91/h at only 54% income satisfaction.

The operator slices the same compass differently across several blog pages: €103/h average across all respondents (this article), €95/h median across IT freelancers, and €90/h for software & web development in the Aug 7 IT breakdown, versus €91/h for the same specialty here. These are cuts of one survey, not independent confirmations. A platform survey, not official statistics: it captures requested rates and self-reported answers. The article is dated 2026-04-21 — the figures aren't new today, only the correction is.

Yet the score lands at 31, because two components read zero: location 0 out of 20 and track fit 0 out of 10. The reason is a hard gate — the careers page states a security clearance requirement for many engineering roles, and that collides with a Brazil residence.

Why a security clearance stalls on address alone: Before security-sensitive work, an authority vets the person — resume, places of residence, contacts, and at higher levels the surrounding circle too. It can only check what it has access to solid records for. Like a landlord who requires a German credit report: someone who's lived abroad for years doesn't have worse credit, their file just sits in the wrong place. So the gate here isn't blocking a qualification, it's blocking an address — which is why the entry stays visible on the board for transparency but is never pitched as an opportunity.
