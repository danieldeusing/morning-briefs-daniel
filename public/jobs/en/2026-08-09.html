Now the part that puts those numbers in perspective. This brief has cited for days the note the aggregator attaches to carried-over entries — "carried forward from <date>, not individually re-verified today." Today, 98 entries carry the date Aug 6, six carry Aug 7, five carry Aug 8; eleven pages were pulled live. But a check against this brief's own archive shows: four of those stamps can't be true. QUINCEY ID-237, BridgingIT (Kotlin/microservices), and the JetBrains role "Core Engine" first appear in the archive on Aug 7, Aleph Alpha first on Aug 8 — where it's explicitly logged as verified live that day. All four now claim to have been carried over unchanged since Aug 6. An entry can't date from a day on which it didn't exist.

What a carry-over stamp is supposed to mean: It's the answer to the only question that matters before you apply — "when did someone last check whether this listing still exists?" It's like the best-before date on a milk carton: it doesn't describe the contents, it describes how much trust you can still place in them. But when the same date ends up on cartons that only reached the shelf two days later, it stops being a date and becomes decoration. In practical terms: the direction of the claim still holds — the vast majority of the board hasn't been individually checked against the source page in days. What's worthless as of today is the precision of the date.

What this changes about how to use the board. Until now, the stamp let you read a rough ranking — freshly checked, checked yesterday, untouched since Wednesday. That no longer holds. The only cleanly documented split is binary: eleven entries were pulled today (the seven new ones plus the four highest-scoring carried-over entries: iSAX 83, JetBrains 82, ELCA 81, Keil 80), 109 weren't. Everything finer is self-reported, without proof. For a board whose sort order measures fit, not availability, that's the decisive limitation: before any cover letter, a click on the listing matters, not its rank on the list.

Except it isn't new. From the reader's point of view, it's a listing that's been running for at least 24 days.

Why a listing disappears and comes back: Project boards surface results depending on search term, sort order, and age — a listing can slip out of a queried results page without ever going offline. Like a book that got moved two shelves over in the library: someone searching the same spot won't find it and will report it as new the moment it turns up again. The consequence is inconvenient but simple: a listing dropping out of this board doesn't prove a project has been awarded — and one reappearing doesn't prove it's fresh.

Why there's no checkmark and no warning triangle here today. The notice names the categories of proof required — liability insurance, revenue figures, references, one certification each in IT security and quality management, plus qualified developers, data scientists, and IT project managers — but not a single threshold.[5] Without a number, no gap can be quantified, and claiming one would be a guess. The lots are cut differently: Lot 1 complete development projects, Lot 2 "skill-oriented" individual services in software engineering and data science/AI, Lot 3 brokering AI and data services. Only Lot 2 is even in the size class a two-person shop could reach — but that can only be confirmed once the tender documents are out.

Framework contract, lot, eligibility proof: A public authority rarely awards a single project — it awards a framework contract, essentially a season pass: whoever holds it can draw down work for years without a fresh tender each time. So that giant firms alone can't corner the bid, the contract gets split into lots, separately biddable packages. And because the authority doesn't want to gamble on whether a bidder can deliver, it demands eligibility proof up front: minimum revenue, headcount of developers, reference projects, certificates. For a very small shop, everything hinges on exactly those numbers — they're the line between "can bid alone" and "only with a partner." When they're not in the notice, the only honest answer is: still unknown.

What such a threshold looks like in numbers is shown by an already-expired tender of the same type: the state of Hesse, via HZD, sought .NET and Java application development in five lots, €45.8 million, deadline Jun 19, 2026 — long past, cited here purely for illustration. Lot 1 required €10 million minimum annual revenue in application development alone, at least 30 qualified developers, three references of 600+ person-days each, ISO 27001, and a Ü2 security clearance.[7] Against numbers like that, a solo shop isn't slightly short, it's in a different league — biddable only as a subcontractor. Whether Dataport sets its bar similarly high is open.
