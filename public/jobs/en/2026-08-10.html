Procurement: an automotive cluster seeks its AI matching platform [1][2][3]

For weeks, the procurement column has brought either enterprise-scale framework contracts or specialist systems with no discernible stack connection. Today it's something different: ACOD GmbH in Leipzig is seeking an "AI-powered web platform for matching events, training offers, and companies" — explicitly as an MVP. The acronym stands for Automotive Cluster Ostdeutschland: a network organization that brings together manufacturers, suppliers, research institutes, and associations, with BMW Leipzig, Volkswagen Sachsen, Porsche, Magna, Schaeffler, Fraunhofer institutes, and TU Dresden among its members.[3] The subject of the tender is thus exactly what this organization does by hand every day: sorting events, training offers, and companies to match one another.

Why there's no checkmark here despite the stack match. This brief's partnership-gap analysis runs on numbers: minimum annual revenue, required developer headcount, reference projects, certifications. The detail page on one mirror shows only a title and a countdown — nothing else; contracting-authority address, CPV code, value, procedure type, and every eligibility criterion are simply absent.[2] The second mirror supplies the client, region, and deadline, but likewise no value.[1] The substantive requirements sit in the specifications (attachments 1 and 2) behind the procurement portal. Exactly two things are demonstrable today: the subject matches the stack — applied AI, no foundation-model training, no research. And the deadline holds up: twelve business days.

MVP in a tender — and what it reveals about the size class: "MVP" stands for Minimum Viable Product, the smallest version that already delivers real value. Like a furniture store that builds one model room before fitting out the entire showroom floor — you see whether the idea holds up without paying for everything. When a contracting authority puts the word in the title, that's a signal about scope: what's being sought is a first working platform, not a five-year operation with on-call support. It's exactly at this size class that a two-person shop becomes conceivable at all. But scope doesn't replace the eligibility bar: even a small project can require a minimum revenue or three public-sector references. The title says how much gets built — not who's allowed to build it.

The second returning listing in two days — and this time it's about an exclusion boundary

The most eye-catching newcomer of the day is also the lowest-scored: Etengo AG is seeking an embedded software developer for IoT in Passau, 100% remote, four months, researched band €95–120/h* — the highest of today's newcomers — and still lands at only fit 58. The reason is in the score breakdown: stack component 7 of 45, because neither Java, Angular, nor cloud is in play. What's wanted is TCP/IP, Modbus, and TLS encryption on an ESP32.

And this is exactly where the entry runs along an exclusion boundary. This profile has explicitly opted out of embedded Linux, Linux kernel and driver development, and Yocto/OpenEmbedded — skills that are on the CV but that shouldn't become projects anymore. A listing with "embedded" in the title would, on that basis, be a mismatch. This time the aggregator explicitly justified the boundary: "general IoT/ESP32 application layer, no kernel driver work — therefore not covered by the embedded-Linux exclusion." That's the right distinction, and it isn't hairsplitting.

Why "embedded" doesn't just mean "embedded": a microcontroller like the ESP32 is a chip costing a few euros, on which your own program runs directly — you write the application, not the operating system. Embedded Linux, by contrast, means bringing a full-fledged Linux onto a device, writing drivers for unfamiliar hardware, and building the system from source. The difference is between furnishing a room and building the house the room sits in. Both get called "embedded" colloquially, but they demand different work — and only the second kind is opted out here. Protocol work at the application layer — Modbus, MQTT, KNX, Z-Wave — explicitly remains the premium niche.

The important part is in the rate column. It reads €84,000–183,000/year* with the note "Levels.fyi median ~€117,000." That's not an employer figure but an analysis of self-reported salaries on a third-party platform — and the range is so wide, at €99,000, that its low end sits below this track's €100k threshold while its high end nearly doubles it. The score treats it as "above threshold" anyway, because it computes on the median. Both can be true and still mislead.

Why a salary band isn't just a salary band: there are two fundamentally different kinds. The listing figure is stated by the employer itself — in some places, such as New York or California, even legally required — and the employer must honor it in the interview. The crowdsourced median, by contrast, comes from employees voluntarily reporting what they earn, with a platform averaging the results. It's like a restaurant review: it says something about the kitchen, but the waiter never promised you any of it. Only the first kind is a promise. Every asterisked band on the board is the second kind.

What the scoring misses: DeepL itself has just cut jobs. On May 7, 2026, the company announced it would cut roughly 250 positions from its total workforce — about one in four of its 1,000-plus employees worldwide.[7] The rationale wasn't a revenue slump but a rebuild into an "AI-native" organization, where, according to management, smaller teams now use AI to do what previously required entire departments.[8] Headquarters sits in Cologne — exactly this listing's location. DeepL hasn't published a breakdown of the cuts by location; how much of it landed in Cologne is unknown and isn't claimed here. That's background, not a disqualifier: reopening senior backend roles three months after a restructuring is normal, and this role sits on the product side — exactly where investment goes after a cut like this. But it does take the shine off the full track-fit point — "established and well-funded" holds for the funding, "stable" doesn't follow from that. And it corrects the score note itself: the "550 employees" figure cited there comes from a search-engine snippet and is contradicted by two independent reports putting the May 2026 figure at over 1,000 — the number stands unchanged and wrong in the JSON and isn't used in this brief.

The backdrop this track is currently hiring against remains uncomfortable — with the explicit note that both figures below are background, not today's signal. SICK AG is cutting roughly 500 jobs in southern Baden — with about 5,000 employees across Waldkirch, Reute, Freiburg, and Donaueschingen, that's one in ten jobs in its home region, announced in April 2026.[5] And BASF has raised its cost-savings target to €2.3 billion and is warning its IT division, with roughly 8,500 employees, of "significant" job cuts, while functions migrate to hubs in India and Malaysia. That report is from Feb 27, 2026 — six months old, not updated in this run, and therefore to be read strictly as backdrop, not news.[6] For the employee track, the sober takeaway is: Germany's industrial IT labor market is releasing candidates, not opening roles. Anyone job-hunting here is competing with the laid-off, not the merely restless.
