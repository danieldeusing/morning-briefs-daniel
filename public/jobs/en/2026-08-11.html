The tax administration wants two outside hires — and sets almost no hurdles [1]

For four runs, the EU procurement database wasn't directly readable; this brief had to rely on aggregator mirrors. Today it worked again: the PDF export of notice 512867-2026 delivered eight pages of full text, published in the Official Journal S 141/2026 on Jul 24, 2026. Everything that follows is stated verbatim in it.[1]

The contracting authority is the Baden-Württemberg Regional Finance Office – State Center for Data Processing (LZfD). The background is KONSENS, the jointly run software-development effort of the federal and sixteen state tax administrations, operating since 2005. The KONSENS TestCenter (TCK) is, per the notice, "the central quality gate in KONSENS's release management process," based in Stuttgart and Karlsruhe. What's sought is test-case automation — in a sentence that sets the size class: "For the tasks at hand, 2 external staff are required."

Why this is the cleanest procurement situation in weeks. This brief's partnership-gap analysis usually looks for minimum revenue, a required team size, public-sector references, ISO certifications, or security clearances — and regularly finds enough of them to rule out a two-person shop. Here, none of those thresholds appear in the notice. Even the personnel disclosure is waived: "The names and professional qualifications of the personnel deployed for contract performance must be stated: Not required." And the two lots are identical in substance, with a bidder able to win at most one — the contracting authority deliberately wants two different providers, which raises a small player's chances rather than lowering them.

Eligibility criterion ≠ award criterion: a public tender involves two completely different checks. The eligibility criterion asks: is this provider even allowed to take part? — revenue, insurance, references, certifications. The award criterion then asks: which of the admitted bids wins? — usually price and quality, weighted. Like a car race: first the license and the technical inspection, only then the lap time. For a two-person shop, it's almost always the first hurdle that decides — you fail on a required annual revenue of two million before your price is even read. That's why a tender with just one eligibility criterion is rare and valuable. And here the second half is missing: the scoring matrix sits as a document titled "A2_Angebotsprüfung_Wertung" behind the procurement portal.

The order of magnitude, explicitly my own math. The contracting authority states no contract value, only acceptance volumes: 160 person-days in the first contract year, then three times 240 person-days. 160 person-days over eight months works out to a full-time position — roughly 20 days a month. Whoever wins a lot is thus fully utilized, not moonlighting. Applying this board's bands (€90–115/h, eight hours per person-day), one lot works out to roughly €115,000 to €147,000 in the first contract phase, and €630,000 to €810,000 over all four years. These are my figures from volume × market band, not a statement from the contracting authority — the actual day rate comes from the tender submission.

The one real gap is location, not size. The notice lists Karlsruhe and Stuttgart as places of performance; it says nothing about working from home, a remote share, or a presence requirement. For a bidder resident in Brazil, that's the decisive open question, and it's still clarifiable: the deadline for additional information ends on Aug 13, 2026 at 23:59 CEST, with communication running exclusively through the project area of the ausschreibungen.landbw.de portal. Second open point: the notice names no technology — test automation in the KONSENS environment tends to be Java-adjacent by experience, but that isn't documented here; the specifications also sit behind the portal. The stack match is thus plausible, not proven.

The hourly rate holds — utilization falls [2][3]

This week's most interesting market figure is one that hasn't moved. The Freelancer Compass 2026 — a platform survey with roughly 5,412 respondents, conducted between late 2025 and early 2026 — reports an average hourly rate of €103 for the DACH region, down from €104 the prior year. A one-euro difference, practically flat. But the same dataset shows: average monthly income fell from €8,432 in 2025 to €6,653 — just over 21%.[2]

Together, the two figures allow for only one explanation: it isn't the price that fell, it's the number of paid days. The survey supplies the confirmation itself — 24% of respondents were tied up in projects for fewer than 50 days last year, so almost a quarter worked fewer than ten weeks in the entire year.[2] Dividing €6,653 by €103/h leaves roughly 65 working hours a month, just over eight days — my own math, not stated as such in the survey.

Utilization beats the hourly rate: a freelancer has two levers — what they charge per hour, and how many hours they sell. The first shows up in every statistic, the second decides what's in the bank account. Like an ice cream stand that hasn't touched the price per scoop but now only opens every other day: the price list looks unchanged, the till is half empty. For negotiations, that means something uncomfortable: in a market with idle capacity, people argue over the rate, even though the real scarcity is project length. A twelve-month assignment at €95 beats three months at €115 — and that's exactly why the term stated on a listing is the second-most important number after the rate.

Comparing the two anchors: €103 is an average, while the €95 from the same platform's analysis is a median across all IT disciplines; software and web development forms the lower end there at €90.[3] That the mean sits above the median isn't a contradiction, but the usual picture of a skewed distribution: a few very high rates pull the average up, while the middle stays below it.

The board keeps sitting below that middle. Of the 72 euro hourly bands, 24 start at €85 and 39 at €90; only eight start at €95 or above, one below. And where a client has actually stated the figure instead of a research asterisk, it becomes clear: the three confirmed German hourly figures on today's board are €95 (senior Java/Spring Boot, 100% remote), roughly €85 as a €680 day-rate ceiling (Python/FastAPI/Go, nearshore), and €75 (full-stack cloud, AWS migration). None of them sits above the median, two sit below. 83 of the 92 bands carry an asterisk, meaning researched benchmark; six are confirmed, three are missing entirely.
