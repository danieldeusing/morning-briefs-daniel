Sovereign Cloud / BSI C5: Government agencies and critical-infrastructure operators aren't allowed to put their data in just any data center. The BSI's C5 criteria catalog is the German audit standard for this — a provider has an independent auditor certify that access controls, encryption, location, and disaster-recovery plans follow specific rules. Think of it like a used-car inspection report: it's not the seller saying "it's safe," it's a third party that checked and signed off. For freelancers, that means in practice: on projects with a C5 or KRITIS angle, the client is slower to switch providers, terms run longer — and rates sit higher than in plain application development. That's exactly the profile carrying today's €100 position — the entry's own score rationale places it there (BSI C5, ISO 27001, KRITIS, sovereign cloud, public sector).

What does that mean for an opening conversation? Yesterday's argument still holds, but it now has evidence behind it. Anyone asking for €100/h can, as of today, point to a real, publicly listed position that pays exactly that — but then has to explain why their own work sits in the same price bracket even though the mandate is cut differently. The solid bridge isn't "Java is worth a lot too," it's the overlap: Kubernetes appears in 24 entries on this board today, AWS in ten, DevOps as a tag in twelve. Senior Java plus solid operations and cloud experience is the combination that actually lands at the target band on this board. Pure application backend isn't.

What a partner supplies: capacity. €3.5 million over four years works out to roughly €875,000 in delivery per year — five to seven full-time people depending on rate. OKSTRA: The "Objektkatalog für das Straßen- und Verkehrswesen" (object catalog for roads and traffic) — by its own definition, the first comprehensive German standard covering everything from road planning to as-built documentation to traffic-data capture. Think of it as a binding vocabulary for agencies, planning firms, and software: when a state agency and an engineering firm talk about "lane" or "structure," they mean the same data field. The tendered contract isn't a new build, it's maintenance and further development of this standard — for five years. Contracts like this are rare, long-lived, and almost always go to firms with prior experience in exactly this domain.
Minimum Revenue — What It Really Means The required annual revenue is a property of the bidder, not of the bid. Like a rental application where the landlord wants to see pay stubs: it's not about whether you could pay the rent, it's about proving you already have. That's why a low price never solves the problem — the check happens before bid evaluation. For a solo setup there are exactly two legal routes: as a member of a bidding consortium where a partner demonstrates the revenue, or as a named subcontractor. Both have to be declared in the bid, not afterward.

Honestly totaled up: three of the four tenders are only reachable for twiceD through a bidding consortium or as a named subcontractor. The exception is Lot 3 of the OKSTRA tender: €100,000 in comparable revenue over three years plus one reference is the only hurdle today where company size isn't the exclusion criterion. That's not a ✅ direct fit yet either — the required reference has to come from the QGIS/geodata space. But it's this track's first hit in weeks where the question isn't "which partner?" but "does our own reference hold up?" TU Munich is the best stack match, but €800,000 a year is a different company size. The day's real progress sits in the tender track. After three lean weeks, four TED notices were opened all the way to the full text — and for the first time on this track, the actual eligibility thresholds are on record instead of estimated.

The Fernstraßen-Bundesamt is tendering maintenance, upkeep, and further development of OKSTRA, Germany's object catalog for roads and traffic. Five-year term, deadline September ninth at ten in the morning. And this tender splits into three lots, with three different thresholds. Lot one, the OKSTRA software itself, requires five hundred thousand euros in comparable-service revenue over three years. Lot two, an OKSTRA-compliant web service, three hundred thousand. Lot three, an OKSTRA plugin for the geodata program QGIS, only one hundred thousand. All three lots accept a single reference from the last five years. That makes lot three the only eligibility bar all day that a solo operation can clear as a bidder in its own right — a hundred thousand euros over three years works out to roughly thirty-three thousand a year. The bottleneck there shifts from revenue to the reference: a documented project in the QGIS or geodata space, and that isn't on hand today. For lots one and two, it stays a named subcontract to an engineering or geodata firm.

The Technical University of Munich is tendering dashboard development over two years, deadline September tenth at nine in the morning. The cleanest stack match of the day — but economically it demands eight hundred thousand euros in minimum annual revenue and three comparable references. That's a different company size. And the Informationstechnikzentrum Bund requires two point three million euros in sector-specific net revenue per year for its Fachverfahren KESS system. That's not a negotiable figure, it's an exclusion criterion for the bidder itself. Here, twiceD can only ride along if a systems house specifically names a specialist.

Worth understanding: the required minimum revenue is a property of the bidder, not of the bid. Like a landlord who wants to see pay stubs — it isn't about whether you could pay, it's about proving you already have. A low price never solves that problem; the check happens before the bid is even evaluated. There are exactly two routes: joining a bidding consortium where a partner proves the revenue, or coming in as a named subcontractor. Either way has to be declared in the bid itself, not afterward.
