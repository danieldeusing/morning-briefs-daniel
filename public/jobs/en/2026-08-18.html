The Rate Held, the Days Didn't — the Other Half of the Freelancer-Kompass

The number that's been running through these editions for weeks is €103: the average fee across all disciplines, one euro below the year before, the first decline since the survey began. It's accurate, it's backed by the primary source — and it's the more benign half of the press release. The same two pages contain a figure that casts this rate debate in a different light.

The rate held almost steady, the income collapsed. A one-percent drop in the hourly fee can't explain a 21-percent drop in monthly income — and people worked more too, two hours a week more than the year before. That leaves exactly one explanation, and the survey names it itself: 24 percent of respondents billed fewer than 50 days of invoiced projects in the past year. A quarter of the sample, in other words, billed less than ten weeks' worth of work. As the main reason for holding rates steady, participants cite a lack of incoming work (61%) ahead of strong competition (31%); 62 percent plan no increase at all for 2026, nine percent want to lower rates.[1]

Utilization Rate: The share of a year's working days on which you can actually bill a client — the rest goes to business development, proposals, bookkeeping, and downtime. Like a taxi: the fare per kilometer says nothing about daily revenue unless you know how many of those kilometers had a passenger in the seat. For a freelancer, that's the real lever — €110/h for 100 days adds up to less than €90/h for 180 days. That's why a rate negotiation that ignores the contract term and the committed number of person-days is only half a negotiation.

What that means for this board. It consistently measures the wrong axis. 118 listings with bands from €85 to €130 say something about the price a client has in mind — nothing about how many of those days ever get billed. Two figures from the board point the same direction as the survey: only twelve of 118 entries carry the Long-Term tag, and 111 show no posting date, so it stays unknown how long a mandate has already been open. A mandate that's sat unfilled for months is either poorly paid or poorly scoped — the third possibility, that nobody's looking, doesn't exist against a reported 61-percent shortfall in incoming work.

One contradiction deserves naming. Bitkom's latest figure put 109,000 IT positions unfilled across the German economy.[8] How does that square with a quarter of freelancers going underutilized? Most likely like this: Bitkom counts open permanent positions, the Kompass measures project demand. Anyone cutting budgets trims external day rates first and leaves the permanent req open anyway. For context: the Bitkom figure is from 2025-08-07, so roughly a year old.

EU Threshold / UVgO: Above a certain contract value, a public body has to tender across the whole EU — full formal apparatus, publication on TED, and the strict eligibility proofs that small providers fail. Below it, national law applies — in Germany, the Unterschwellenvergabeordnung (UVgO, sub-threshold procurement regulation): the same duty to competition and equal treatment, but leaner and without an EU-wide notice. Like the difference between a tender with a notary's worth of paperwork and a clean but simple bid procedure. In practice that means: below the threshold, company size is rarely the exclusion criterion — and for a two-person shop, that's the more promising league.
