Why seven numbers here outweigh 3,300: Normally the rule is: bigger sample, more reliable result. But that's only true if both samples measure the same thing. Picture eBay. You could ask 3,300 sellers what they'd want for their used bike — or you could look at seven bikes that actually sold. The 3,300 asking prices are a good read on sellers' expectations and a poor one on the market price. The seven closed deals are statistically thin but measure the right quantity. The €102 survey figures are asking prices; the €85.29 is a posted price. Anyone who pits both numbers against each other as "the market rate" is comparing supply with demand — and then wondering about the €17 gap.

What's new about it today. Not the amount — €85 sits almost exactly on the previous average and pulled it down by four cents. What's new is where the number comes from. It's the first client-published rate on this board for a mandate explicitly advertised as an AI project. The common assumption that AI competence commands a price premium in the DACH freelance market finds no support here: the one confirmed AI rate sits at the same level as the confirmed non-AI rates, and today's three other AI mandates carry researched bands of €90–115/h* and €85–110/h* — the same bands as the standard Java mandates.

How solid this is n = 1 for AI, n = 7 overall. A single client doesn't make a market, and a five-month mandate with two onsite days a week isn't a full-price remote project. What holds up is the weaker claim: there's no evidence of an AI premium in this board's confirmed data — not "there is no premium." What carries the comparison is the stability of the series: seven rates, average between €85 and €86 for three editions running. Two rule changes, neither of which applies to this profile

Two numbers come up in every DACH recruiting discussion this year: the new EU Blue Card salary thresholds and Switzerland's quota for skilled workers from third countries. Both have applied since 2026-01-01, both get cited as a DACH labor-market bottleneck — and neither simply applies to a German citizen residing in Brazil.

EU Blue Card and quota: The Blue Card is a residence permit for skilled workers from non-EU countries. It's granted when there's an employment contract and the salary sits above a set line — a fast lane you can only enter with a minimum salary. A quota is different: a fixed annual number of permits, like seats in a concert hall. Once they're gone, that's it — no matter how good the candidate is. Switzerland manages access this way because it's not in the EU. Neither applies to EU citizens: freedom of movement exists within the EU, so no permit and no seat are needed.

First, the Blue Card. Since 2026-01-01, explicit minimum gross annual salaries apply: €50,700 in the regular case and €45,630 in shortage occupations, which include IT. Also new: remote work is now explicitly permitted for holders, provided they have an uncancelled employment contract[5] — that exact addition is what makes the rule look like an opening for remote work. It still doesn't apply to this profile, though: the permit requires employment with a German employer, and the threshold sits far below this brief's mandatory employee threshold of €100k.

Second, Switzerland. The ceilings for 2026 remain unchanged: a maximum of 8,500 qualified workers and specialists from third countries, split into 4,500 residence permits (B) and 4,000 short-term permits (L); the Federal Council decided this on 2025-11-19, and the corresponding amendment to the admission ordinance (VZAE) took effect on 2026-01-01.[6] A German citizen doesn't fall under this quota but under the EU/EFTA freedom-of-movement agreement instead. The bottleneck for the seven Swiss board entries is therefore not a residence-law one. It's stated verbatim in the listings: "onsite," "2–3 days a week in the office," "remote only within Switzerland." What rules out these mandates is the place of residence — not permit practice.

What actually constrains instead For a German GmbH setup with a managing director based in Brazil, the real hurdles are more mundane: the client's willingness to work with a five-hour time offset; onsite clauses that can't be bundled into block visits; and, for Swiss engagements, the 120-day limit above which a cross-border service also becomes notification- or permit-liable even for EU providers. That's exactly why this board scores presence requirement as its own component (location_remote, 0–20 points) and doesn't score residence law at all.

Source quality, honestly: both rule sets are documented today only via search-engine summaries of official sources, not via a direct pull of the primary pages: admin.ch answered three attempts with HTTP 403, make-it-in-germany.com with a bot-check page, bamf.de with HTTP 404. The figures come from official domains and are internally consistent, but weren't read here on the original page — anyone who needs them for a decision should verify with BAMF or SEM respectively. The 120-day limit for EU/EFTA service providers is general regulatory knowledge and isn't separately sourced in this edition.

One last item of housekeeping. Two rule changes keep coming up in every DACH recruiting conversation this year: the new EU Blue Card salary thresholds, now fifty thousand seven hundred euros in the regular case and forty-five thousand six hundred thirty in shortage occupations, and Switzerland's quota of eighty-five hundred permits for skilled workers from outside the EU. Neither applies to a German citizen. The Blue Card is a residence permit for people from non-EU countries; the quota is a fixed annual number of permits, also only for third countries. What actually rules out the seven Swiss entries on this board is stated plainly in the listings: onsite work, two to three office days a week, remote only within Switzerland. It's the place of residence, not residence law.
