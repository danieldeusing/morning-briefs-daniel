Bavaria Is Budgeting €125 — and Needs Twelve People per Lot for It

For four days running, track 3 of this brief has delivered nothing new because its main tool is down. Today a find comes in anyway, and it's the biggest one yet: the Bavarian State Ministry for Food, Agriculture and Forestry (StMELF) is putting application development out to tender in five identical lots. In substance, it's about agricultural and forestry subsidy programs plus the processing of EU payments — in other words, the software Bavaria uses to pay subsidies out to farmers. Technically, it's about two in-house frameworks: Racy10 (Java, Apache Wicket, Spring, JPA) for the legacy system, and Racy25 (Java/TypeScript, Angular, Spring, JPA) as the "standard for new development."[1]

That's Daniel's core stack, word for word. Java, Spring, JPA, TypeScript, Angular — the notice reads like the skills list off the CV, just in a government agency's order. And then there's a number in it that this brief has never had before.

The division comes out even, and that's not a coincidence. €9,375,000 ÷ 75,000 h = exactly €125.00. The framework ceiling works out just as exactly to €187.50 — precisely one and a half times as much. Anyone who sets a tender up like that has worked backward from an assumed hourly rate, not the other way around. So the Free State of Bavaria is budgeting Java/Angular development at €125 an hour, with headroom up to €187.50.

Why €125 isn't the same as €125 on your own invoice: Picture a construction site. The city pays a building company €125 per tradesman-hour. The bricklayer doesn't get €125 of that — he gets his wage; the rest is site management, bid preparation, warranty, insurance, downtime between jobs, and the company's profit. IT tenders work the same way. The €125 is the price the contracting authority pays the general contractor. The €85 this board carries as confirmed rates is the price an agency passes on to the freelancer — the end client behind it pays considerably more. These are two floors of the same building, not two prices for the same room. The 46.6% gap is therefore not evidence that Daniel is being brokered below his worth — it shows how large the margin between the floors is, and by extension, what a direct contract with a public contracting authority would be worth.

The capacity math is the sticking point. 75,000 hours spread over 45 months works out to exactly 3.75 years, or roughly 20,000 hours per year — per lot. At 1,600 productive hours per head per year (vacation, sick leave, internal time deducted), that's roughly 12.5 full-time employees, continuously, for four years. At a more generous 1,800 hours it's still a good eleven. twiceD is a two-person setup. The gap isn't a question of documentation — it's a question of arithmetic.

What a partner would need to supply: (1) Staffing. Roughly 12–13 full-time employees per lot over four years — twiceD can supply at most two of those. That's not a supplement, it's the main deliverable; realistically the only viable role here is subcontractor to a bidder who supplies the remaining eleven, not bidder itself. (2) Creditworthiness. At a lot volume of €9.375 million, a minimum-revenue requirement in the low-single-digit millions is likely — though the notice on the mirror states no revenue threshold, no minimum FTE count, and no certificate requirement. That doesn't mean there isn't one: eligibility criteria live in the tender documents, not the summary. (3) References. Evaluation runs 70% on performance (technical concepts plus personal references) and only 30% on price, with a minimum score of 400 of 700 on the technical concept — miss that, and you're disqualified. A lot here isn't won on the cheapest rate, but on proven headcount.
Framework agreement with lots: A normal contract is a purchase: fixed item, fixed price. A framework agreement is more like a subscription with a cap — the contracting authority secures the right to draw on it for years, but doesn't have to use up the full amount. Hence two figures: the estimated volume and the ceiling as a limit. Lots are sub-packages of the same tender; five identical lots means the contracting authority wants five contract partners side by side and splits the work among them later — like a restaurant with five framework suppliers. For a small provider, that's theoretically good (five chances instead of one), but practically only if a single lot is small enough. Here, it isn't.

The time horizon puts everything in perspective. The bid deadline is 2026-09-24, but the work doesn't start until 2027-04-01 — seven months between the bid and the first billable hour. Anyone who takes part here is committing staff they don't even have yet at submission. For a general contractor that's normal; for a two-person outfit, it's the real disqualifier.

The rule isn't a formality: it's the time buffer a one-person bid needs between reading the tender documents, checking eligibility, drafting the concept, and submitting electronically.

ANÜ (Arbeitnehmerüberlassung, temporary staffing) — and why that's different for a GmbH: External development work gets bought in two fundamentally different ways. Under a work or service contract, the client commissions a company to deliver a result; how and with whom it delivers is the company's business. Under Arbeitnehmerüberlassung, a person is loaned out: they work under the client's direction, inside the client's team, like agency labor. The difference is the one between "I order a cake" and "I borrow a baker." Germany's AÜG (Arbeitnehmerüberlassungsgesetz, the Temporary Employment Act) only permits the latter with the staffing agency's license, and generally caps the loan-out to the same client at 18 months; collective agreements can extend that. In practice: anyone delivering through their own GmbH is typically contracted, not loaned out. If a tender says "ANÜ" or "fixed-term employment," the agency is thinking of the mandate as staff supply — a different contract model, a different chain of instruction, a term limit. Background knowledge, not separately sourced in this edition.

That's exactly where the friction sits. The 34-month mandate at Randstad Digital is flagged on the board as an ANÜ framework, and ZiTOS explicitly advertises its four months as "fixed-term employment, ANÜ-like." A 34-month loan-out to the same client isn't straightforwardly workable without a collective-bargaining basis — the agency usually resolves that by switching contract models or inserting a break. For a DE GmbH setup with its managing director based in Brazil, both are more than a formality, because being bound by instructions and on-site presence requirements hang directly off the model. Before any conversation about the rate, the question of the contract model is therefore the more important one.

Why the date matters more than the number: Search engines and AI summaries rank reports by relevance, not by age — a frequently cited headline from eleven months ago can therefore sit at the top today and sound like it's from yesterday. For workforce reports, that distinction is decisive, because cut programs overlap: "13,000" and "22,000" can mean the same people, different divisions, or programs building on each other. Check the original article's date first, then the number.

SAP is still hiring, just elsewhere. A report from 2026-07-06 describes SAP limiting new hiring almost entirely to core AI roles and additionally cutting travel budgets to fund AI investment. The CEO is quoted expecting to operate not with a smaller, but with a "very, very different work force."[7] The "1–2% per year, indefinitely" addendum quoted yesterday is missing from this source and couldn't be re-confirmed today — it therefore no longer stands as a figure in this brief as of today. For this profile, the direction matters more than the percentage: a company narrowing its hiring to AI-adjacent roles tends to push standard development work outward — straight into the freelance and framework-contract channels this board is made of.

It's Friday, August twenty-first. Today brings the first number in weeks that clears a hundred euros and still doesn't come from a survey. It shows up in a new tender from the Free State of Bavaria — and it doesn't appear as a stated figure so much as fall out of a simple division.

The Bavarian State Ministry for Food, Agriculture and Forestry is putting application development out to tender, in five identical lots. In substance, it's about agricultural and forestry subsidy programs and the processing of EU payments — the software Bavaria uses to pay subsidies out to farmers. Technically, it's Java, Spring, JPA, TypeScript, and Angular. That's Daniel's core stack, word for word.

Each lot is budgeted at around nine million three hundred seventy-five thousand euros, for around seventy-five thousand work hours. Divide one by the other and you get exactly a hundred twenty-five euros. The framework ceiling of fourteen million and change works out just as evenly to a hundred eighty-seven euros fifty — exactly one and a half times as much. Anyone who sets a tender up that way has worked backward from an assumed hourly rate. So Bavaria is budgeting this work at a hundred twenty-five euros an hour.

For comparison: the seven client-published rates on this brief's board average eighty-five euros and twenty-nine cents. The freelance.de survey puts the IT average at a hundred one ninety-eight, and the Freelancer-Kompass gives a median of ninety-five. Bavaria's number sits forty-six percent above the board's confirmed rates.

But that gap isn't proof that Daniel is being brokered below his worth. Picture a construction site. The city pays a building company a hundred twenty-five euros per tradesman-hour. The bricklayer doesn't get a hundred twenty-five euros of that — he gets his wage. The rest is site management, bid preparation, warranty, insurance, and the company's profit. IT tenders work the same way. The hundred twenty-five euros is the price for the general contractor; the eighty-five euros is the price an agency passes on to the freelancer. They're two floors of the same building. What the number really shows is the size of the margin in between.

The second story is less comfortable. Seventy-five thousand hours spread over forty-five months comes to roughly twenty thousand hours a year, per lot. At sixteen hundred productive hours per head each year, that's roughly twelve and a half full-time employees, continuously, for four years. twiceD is a two-person setup. The gap here isn't a question of paperwork — it's a question of arithmetic. The verdict: a partner is needed, and realistically only as a subcontractor to a bidder who supplies the other eleven people, not as the bidder itself.

Then there's the timing. The bid deadline is September twenty-fourth, but the work doesn't start until April first, twenty twenty-seven. That's not work for next quarter — it's a capacity commitment for the year after next.
