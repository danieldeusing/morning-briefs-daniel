And then, on that same page, a word puts everything before it into perspective. The contract type is listed as Arbeitnehmerüberlassung — employee leasing.[1] For a German mandate, that would be a question of the model — for a Swiss host company, it's a legal question with a clear-cut answer.

Cross-border staff leasing — why "remote" doesn't solve the problem: There are two fundamentally different ways to buy in outside development work. With a works or services contract, the client orders a result from a company — like ordering a cake from a bakery: who bakes it and with which oven is the bakery's business, and if it fails, it has to be redone. With staff leasing (in Germany: Arbeitnehmerüberlassung, employee leasing), by contrast, a person is hired out — you borrow the baker and tell them yourself what to bake. SECO names exactly this boundary: leasing exists when "the essential right to give instructions" is handed over to the host company, and an important counter-criterion is whether the provider "also guarantees a certain outcome" and redoes the work if it falls short.[2] And here's the point where Switzerland is stricter than Germany: Germany's AÜG allows leasing with a permit and a time limit. Swiss law grants no permit for this case at all — "staff leasing from abroad into Switzerland by a foreign staffing agency is not permitted" (Art. 12 para. 2 AVG).[2] A German GmbH therefore can't obtain this authorization; for this scenario, it simply doesn't exist. What matters isn't where the developer sits, but who has the right to instruct. A 100%-remote engagement where the Bern client assigns work daily and bills by the hour is leasing. The same engagement structured as a works contract over defined deliverables is not. The location doesn't change — the legal situation does.

In practice that means: the first question for Valumize isn't the rate. It's whether the mandate can also be awarded as a services or works contract with twiceD GmbH instead of as staff leasing. If the answer is yes, it's the best Swiss mandate this board has shown so far — stack coverage nearly complete, no location requirement, Swiss band. If the answer is no, it's not a close call or a matter for negotiation — it's simply closed.

Two Auto-Supplier Figures That Aren't From Today

Research for this edition surfaced two reports on job cuts in German automotive software, both with round numbers and both carrying the qualifier "by the end of 2026." That qualifier is exactly the trap: it makes an announcement sound current, even though it only describes its target horizon, not its date.

Bosch: 1,200 jobs in software development, 950 of them in Germany, in the Cross-Domain Computing Solutions division — justified by fully automated driving development progressing "significantly slower than expected." The number is correct. It's just that the primary report is from 2024-01-18, 13:18[7] — two and a half years old, with a target horizon that happens to fall in our present.

Aumovio: up to 4,000 jobs worldwide — here the number and its recency check out, but the headline framing doesn't. The source dates to 2026-02-12 and is narrower than the headline in two ways: it's explicitly about jobs in research and development, spread across India, Singapore, Romania, Serbia, Germany, and Mexico — and for Germany, the company expects "cuts in the high three-digit range," not 4,000.[8] The purpose is a target metric: the R&D cost ratio is meant to fall below 10% of revenue by 2027, down from 11.9% in Q3 2025.

R&D cost ratio — and why it's the real story here: The research-and-development cost ratio says what share of revenue flows into development. Like a household budget: if you spend 12% of your income on tools and want to get to 10%, you can either earn more or buy fewer tools. A company that lowers this ratio while revenue isn't growing is cutting development. For an outside provider, the direction of this metric matters more than the headcount: it tells you whether less gets developed after the cuts — in which case outside demand disappears too — or whether the same work is meant to get cheaper, which typically pushes work outward. With a target of under 10% over two years, more points to the former.

Second day in a row that a circulating layoff figure fails a date check. On 08-21 it was the Bosch mark of "a further 13,000 jobs" with a primary article from 2025-09-25; today it's 1,200/950 from January 2024. The mechanism is mundane: search engines and summaries rank by relevance, not age, so often-cited headlines stay permanently at the top. For this board, that has a practical consequence — the automotive niche stays premium here, and the data backs that up: 12 IoT entries, exactly as many as yesterday and one more than on 08-20; top unchanged at Amoria Bond with fit 78 in the €100–130/h* band, plus an Akkodis automotive mandate at fit 64 in the same band. None of the cited OEM cuts has removed an entry from this cluster to date. That's not a contradiction, it's the usual order of things: large corporations cut internal development capacity while specialized connectivity and machine-data work stays bought in from outside.

The distinction here is between two ways of buying in work. In a services or works contract, the client orders a result from a company — like ordering a cake from a bakery. Who bakes it and with what oven is the bakery's business, and if it fails, it gets redone. In staff leasing, you borrow the baker instead and tell them yourself what to bake. The fact sheet draws exactly that line: leasing exists when the essential right to give instructions passes to the host company, and an important counter-test is whether the provider also guarantees a certain outcome. So what matters isn't where the developer sits — it's who gives the instructions. The location doesn't change. The legal footing does.

In practice, that means the first question for this client isn't the rate. It's whether the mandate can be awarded as a services contract with the GmbH instead of as staff leasing. If yes, it's the best Swiss mandate this board has ever shown. If no, it's not a close call and not something to negotiate — it's simply closed. And this isn't an isolated case: twelve of the hundred forty-one entries already carry the employee-leasing note in the dataset itself, two of them Swiss mandates.
