Ninety Euros, Not One Hundred Three

On 08-21, this brief compared the board's eight client-published hourly rates against an external figure for the first time and calculated an 18% gap. On 08-22, the same sentence ran again, with an honest addition: this brief couldn't produce a field-specific figure, because the same provider page listed €87/h and €92/h side by side. Same eight numbers, seven different stories. The board rates' mean hasn't moved since yesterday — €677 ÷ 8 = €84.625, median €85. What's moved is only what it's measured against. Against the all-discipline figure, this board looks like a discount market. Against the field Daniel actually works in, it sits six percent below — well within what eight data points can even resolve.

Median vs. average — and why that's not the real trap here: Picture ten people in a room: nine earn €30,000 a year, one earns three million. The average — add it all up, divide by ten — comes to roughly €327,000 and describes nobody in the room. The median — line everyone up in order, take the middle one — comes to €30,000 and describes nine out of ten. For hourly rates, the median is usually the more honest number. Here, though, the two are only two euros apart (€95 vs. €97), and that itself is informative: the distribution is symmetric, there's no small cluster of extreme outliers. The real trap was the population, not the math. €103 is a cleanly calculated average — just over a different set of people. Compare yourself against the wrong group, and the arithmetic is correct while the lesson is wrong.

Within the eight rates, it gets concrete. Three of the eight mandates carry Angular in the title: €70/h for a frontend developer in Munich, €80/h for Angular with data visualization in Frankfurt, €87/h for a web developer with Angular 20.x. Average: €79, 12% below the field median. Exactly one mandate carries Java and Spring Boot in the title — €95/h at SOMI Experts, exactly the IT median and five euros above the field. The remaining four are Python (€85, ~€85) and cloud/DevOps (€75, €100).

Three against one isn't a sample, and this brief doesn't claim otherwise. What's notable is the direction: it matches what the survey says about the fields — the closer to infrastructure, data, and systems, the higher the rate; pure frontend work sits at the lower end. Practical consequence for positioning: on this market, the Java/Spring backend is the more expensive half of the profile, Angular the cheaper one. Anyone who can do both should lead with the backend side in outreach — not because Angular is worth less, but because the market prices it lower.

One figure stays high regardless: the target. The profile specification names ~€100/h+ for the standard Java/Angular mandate. That's 11% above the field median and 5% above the IT median — explicitly a premium ask, not a market price. Of the board's eight published rates, exactly one reaches it (€100/h, GCP DevOps). That was already true before; what's new is knowing how far above market the ask sits.

What's solid here — and what isn't Solid: the four IT figures come from a page opened live today with a stated publication date (2026-07-02) and a stated sample (620 IT freelancers).[1] The eight board rates are pulled field by field from today's embedded dataset and carry no asterisk, so they're client-stated. Not solid: the IT page doesn't state a survey period; the window 2025-11-17–2026-02-08 comes from the Kompass landing page and was carried over from the 08-22 edition.[2] That both breakdowns refer to the same survey is plausible, but unverified. And the survey remains self-reported: it asks what freelancers charge, not what gets paid. Eight client-stated rates against 620 self-reports are two different quantities — the gap between them also contains the difference between asking and getting.

Forward Deployed Engineer — what it actually is: A regular product developer builds a tool and hands it across the counter; whatever the customer does with it, they see at most in a support ticket. A Forward Deployed Engineer takes the tool to the customer and keeps building there — like a kitchen fitter who doesn't just deliver the kitchen, but cooks alongside the client for a week and moves the countertop because it was placed wrong. The term comes from Palantir. Why it's showing up everywhere now: AI products are almost never useful out of the box. A language model has to be adapted to a specific operation's data, terminology, and workflows, or the demo stays a demo — and that work can't be built into the product or handed off to classic consulting. For a two-person setup, that's interesting because it describes exactly what twiceD already does: fitting other people's building blocks into a specific client process. Just as an employee instead of a contract — and with travel, which is the real obstacle from Brazil.

The market numbers behind this are unusually steep. TechCrunch reported on 2026-07-30 that the share of companies planning to hire FDEs stood at 5–10% at the start of the year and jumped to 70% in the second quarter; the recruiting firm Christian & Timbers expects a further 2,100% surge in demand by year's end.[8] Named employers: OpenAI, Anthropic, Palantir as the originator and largest single buyer, plus insurers, fintechs, health and gaming companies, and corporations building internal teams.

The fact that the IT median and IT average here sit only two euros apart says something on its own: the distribution is symmetric, there's no small cluster of extreme outliers. So the real trap wasn't the arithmetic — it was the population. A hundred three euros is a cleanly calculated average over the wrong group of people.

Inside those eight rates, it gets specific. Three of the eight mandates carry Angular in the title: seventy euros for a frontend developer in Munich, eighty for Angular with data visualization in Frankfurt, eighty-seven for a web developer on Angular version twenty. That's seventy-nine euros on average, twelve percent below the field median. Exactly one mandate carries Java and Spring Boot in the title, and it pays ninety-five euros — exactly the IT median. Three against one isn't a sample, and this brief says so too. But the direction matches what the survey says about the fields, and the practical takeaway is simple: on this market, the Java and Spring backend is the pricier half of the profile. Anyone who can do both should lead with the backend side when reaching out.

On the tender side, there's an answer for the first time in five silent days — though only half of one. The European procurement database's search interface is returning results again, a hundred twelve notices in the window from August sixteenth onward. The pages meant for humans — the notice detail page and the PDF export — still return the same error they have for the five days before. That was checked again today. The consequence is awkward: title, buyer, value, deadline, and classification show up in the response; eligibility criteria don't. Minimum turnover, references, team size, certifications all live in the full text, and that stays out of reach. Every partner note in this edition is therefore an estimate from the published volume, not a quoted requirement.

Of four tenders reviewed, exactly one is a genuine hit, and it's the same Bavarian tender already reported via a mirror on August twenty-first. What's new is that it now stands in the primary source — with a number worth doing the division on. For the first lot, the notice states both figures: an estimated value of nine million three hundred seventy-five thousand euros and seventy-five thousand work hours over four years. Divide one by the other and you get exactly a hundred twenty-five euros an hour. That's what a German state ministry is budgeting for exactly this stack — Angular, Spring, JPA — thirty-nine percent above the field median and forty-eight percent above what this board's private clients publish on average.

The wrong conclusion to draw here is false hope: a calculation rate isn't a bid price, competition pushes bids below it, and a margin sticks to whoever sits between the general contractor and the subcontractor. By the numbers, a single lot needs roughly twelve and a half full-time employees, and twiceD fields two — realistically, only a subcontract is on the table. But as a reference point, the number holds up. It's the strongest argument yet against the idea that Java and Angular are commodity work. The deadline is September twenty-fourth, twenty-three full business days out. The three other tenders reviewed were rejected and listed with their reasons: a security-vulnerability information service out of North Rhine-Westphalia, a product procurement for BIM projects, and an extension of a Vienna sterile-goods documentation system. None of them a development contract in the profile's stack.

A third finding comes from the board itself. Seven of the hundred forty-three entries carry the title Forward Deployed Engineer, across six different companies — JetBrains, Camunda, Cohere twice over, Anthropic, Cognition, and supermodular. The JetBrains entry, at fit eighty-two, ranks third on this board, with no classic stack match at all. A Forward Deployed Engineer is someone who doesn't just deliver the tool but takes it to the customer and keeps building there — like a kitchen fitter who cooks alongside the client for a week while moving the countertop. The reason for the cluster is that AI products are almost never useful right out of the box: a language model has to be adapted to a specific operation's data and workflows, or the demo stays a demo. TechCrunch reported in late July that the share of companies planning to hire for this role stood at five to ten percent at the start of the year and jumped to seventy percent in the second quarter. This edition deliberately says nothing about compensation — the figures circulating out there only appear in secondary aggregators.

Zero departures against a hundred forty-one carried-forward entries is therefore not a statement about the mandates, but a statement that nobody has looked at a hundred thirteen of them in five days.
