Eight, not one — and the difference between criterion and evidence

Track 1 of this brief has four mandatory criteria that must all be met: location in Germany or Switzerland, pay above the threshold (DE senior ≥ 100 k€/year, CH senior ≥ CHF 150k), an established company, and either a product-facing AI subject area or an excellent non-AI setup. The fit score is independent of this — it measures fit with the résumé, not eligibility. It is exactly these two axes that got mixed up in the last few editions.

The aggregator writes the result of the criteria check into its own field: track 10 means "all four met," a lower value means "capped, because one is missing." And the score simply missed one criterion today. Both new Employee entries from The Quality Group GmbH (ESN & More) — Staff Engineer AI Enablement, fit 68, and AI Engineer, fit 65 — carry the same qualifier in the location field: "100 % remote, aktuellen Erstwohnsitz in Deutschland erforderlich" ("100% remote, current primary residence in Germany required"). Both nonetheless received loc 20, the full score for "100% remote with an EU employer." For someone living in Ribeirão Claro, that's not a deduction — it's a disqualification.

Why "100% remote" and "primary residence in Germany" show up in the same sentence: An employment contract isn't just an agreement about work and money — it's tied to a social security and tax system. Think of a company as a house that pays trash-collection fees to exactly one city for every resident: if someone moves to another country, the company suddenly has to register with a foreign authority there, pay contributions there, and risks that country saying "you have an office here" (legally: a permanent establishment) and demanding corporate tax. For a single employee, that effort almost never pays off. That's why "100% remote" at German employers almost always means "remote within Germany" — home can be as far from the office as you like, but not across the border. Practical consequence: for salaried roles, the location line isn't fully read until you've read the parenthetical. For freelance engagements the problem largely disappears — twiceD is a German GmbH and bills as a contractor, not as an employee.

What the profile's hard-gate rule makes of this. The specification knows exactly this conflict and prescribes, for the named case — security clearance requiring German residence — location_remote 0 + track 0. A plain primary-residence clause isn't a security clearance, but it creates the same Brazil conflict. Under this reading, 68 (20 stack + 20 loc + 10 rate + 10 intrigue + 8 track) drops to 40, and 65 (20 + 20 + 10 + 9 + 6) drops to 39. Instead of ranking 5th and 7th in the Employee track, both land in the bottom third — behind wealthAPI, whose time-zone clause ("±2 h Berlin") was correctly penalized with loc 3. The same clause type, two different scores in the same dataset.

Framework contract, estimated value, ceiling value — three numbers that don't mean the same thing: A framework contract isn't an order, it's an admission ticket. Like a maintenance-on-call plan for an apartment building: the landlord doesn't promise that something will break — they just fix who gets called when something breaks, and at what rate. Individual orders get drawn down later, and nobody knows at award time how many there will be. The estimated value (here 252,000 €) is the contracting authority's expectation, the ceiling value (300,000 €) is the contractual upper limit beyond which nothing may be drawn down. Why this matters for a small team: the estimated value is not a revenue guarantee — worst case, the authority draws down little and the admission ticket was unpaid bid work. The ceiling value, conversely, is the figure procurement offices size their eligibility requirements against: whoever has to demonstrate the minimum-turnover proof has to measure it against the ceiling, not the estimate.
