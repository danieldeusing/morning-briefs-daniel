125 € per hour — why that isn't Daniel's hourly rate: Divide a lot's estimated value by the hours it buys, and you get the budgeted gross hourly price: 9,375,000 € ÷ 75,000 h = 125 €, or 187.50 € at the contract's ceiling value. That's the price on the menu, not the cook's wage. Out of these 125 €, the prime contractor pays for the developer hour and everything else the tender demands: project management, quality assurance, the ISO 27001 apparatus, the security clearances, bid costs for a 700-point technical concept, the risk of a four-year fixed-price commitment — and its own margin. Practical consequence: what ends up with a subcontractor at the end of the chain is a fraction of that, and the realistic order of magnitude sits on the same board — 70 to 100 €/h, where a contracting authority printed the rate itself. The 125 € works as a ceiling for the chain and as a negotiating argument, not as an expectation.

One detail of this tender is unusually valuable for this profile. The contracting authority explicitly regulates the use of AI tools: "use of AI tools is generally permitted" — paired with a disclosure requirement for the planned tools and a veto right for the contracting authority.[1] This is the first tender in this brief that neither ignores nor bans agentic coding, but regulates it. For a provider whose productivity edge lies exactly there, that's the cheapest possible clause form: the advantage may be used, it just has to be named. Conversely, a veto right means that a bid quietly built on Claude Code or Copilot loses its margin the moment an agency says no. Anyone calculating that way is calculating the rejection into the price.

Dynamic purchasing system or negotiated procedure with prior notice — the difference decides whether Sep 8 is a hard deadline: A dynamic purchasing system is a permanently open supplier pool. Think of a taxi stand: whoever holds the license can join the back of the line at any point during the term, and each individual ride is awarded among whoever's standing there. The contracting authority is not allowed to cap how many qualified providers can join. A negotiated procedure with prior notice is the opposite: a one-time bouncer appointment. You apply by a cutoff date, the contracting authority picks a shortlist, and only that shortlist may bid afterward. Why it matters here: the source for this process describes both at once — a dynamic system and "a maximum of five applicants." Those are mutually exclusive. If it's a dynamic system, Sep 8 is a convenient but not a final date, and entry in 2027 remains possible. If it's a capped competitive process, Sep 8 is the door to a contract running through the end of 2029. Until that's clarified, this brief treats the deadline as hard — the more expensive assumption is the safer one here.

A second process shows up today in the tender lane, and it's a case study in honest ambiguity. Bavaria's state office for digitization, broadband, and surveying is setting up a procurement process for programming services, with an application deadline of Tuesday, September eighth, at ten a.m. — seven full business days. But the source describes the process simultaneously as a dynamic purchasing system and as a negotiated procedure capped at five approved applicants. Those are mutually exclusive. In the first case, September eighth is a convenient date; in the second, it's the only door to a contract running through the end of twenty twenty-nine. Until that's resolved, this brief treats the deadline as hard.
