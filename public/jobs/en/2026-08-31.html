Niche premium — why a weaker stack match lands on top: This board's fit score isn't a similarity measure between a listing and a résumé. It's a sum across five buckets, and two of them explicitly reward scarcity over overlap. Think of it like a used-car market: the tenth silver wagon sells at the going rate; the one classic car with matching papers sells for more — not because it's more of a car, but because almost nobody else can offer it. Almost any senior in DACH can offer Java and Angular — today 46 of 61 entries carry the Java tag. Almost nobody can offer Java plus the automotive domain or plus IoT protocols: 2 and 4 entries today, respectively. That's exactly why the model has a dedicated 7-point niche bucket and a higher target-rate band (€110–140/h instead of €90–120/h). In practice: the 85 isn't a better Java mandate than the 83 — it's a Java mandate with a differentiator Daniel can actually document: OEM references in one case, Z-Wave/EnOcean/KNX certifications in the other. That's the difference between "applying alongside forty others" and "getting the call."

No estimated contract value appears in this brief today. The Aug 30 brief carried the figure from the full-text PDF opened at the time; it isn't repeated here unverified.

Exclusion criterion vs. eligibility criterion — two very different doors: A public procurement office screens bidders in two stages, and the terms for them sound more alike than they are. An exclusion criterion is a yes/no gate: fail it and the bid never even gets read — like a driver's license at a car-rental counter. At StMELF, that's professional or business liability insurance with an insurer licensed in the EU or EEA; the notice explicitly flags it as an "exclusion criterion."[5] An eligibility criterion, by contrast, asks about capacity and experience — revenue, staff, reference projects — and here bidders are compared and weighted. Required are a self-declaration of net total revenue for the last three completed fiscal years plus one to five reference projects, each as prime contractor and each with a contract value of at least €1 million, explicitly from "digitalization of state subsidy procedures," within the last five years at most.[5] For twiceD, the hurdle is clearly the second type. A liability policy can be bought; revenue can be declared as it stands. But a million-euro reference as prime contractor on state subsidy procedures can neither be bought nor made up after the fact — a partner has to bring it. That's why this listing carries ⚠️ Partner required instead of "directly biddable."

The anchor drops — and two agencies contradict each other

The Freelancer-Kompass 2026 states €103/h as the average hourly rate on its own study page, surveyed from Nov 17, 2025 to Feb 8, 2026 among roughly 5,400 freelancers and self-employed professionals.[1] An independent analysis of the same study puts it in context: "The average hourly rate has fallen for the first time – to 103 euros."[2] So for the first time since the survey began, the rate is declining.

That contradicts the figure that ran in this spot yesterday, and the contradiction belongs named, not quietly swapped out. The Aug 30 brief cited a DACH median of €95/h and an average of €97/h from a blog post by the same provider. Median and average are different measures and are allowed to diverge — 95 versus 103 isn't a conflict. 97 versus 103 is. This is only partly resolvable: the study page opened today cites the larger, later sample (5,400 participants through February 2026, versus 1,300 in the first wave through December 2025), and it's the study's primary page. This brief therefore carries €103/h as the anchor from today onward and treats the older average as superseded — without being able to claim both figures describe the same population. For a Java-specific rate, the only figure available remains GULP's often-cited €97.73/h, which carries an "as of 10/2024" stamp and is therefore nearly two years old.[7]

On the demand side, Germany's IT labor market keeps cooling measurably. The Federal Employment Agency counted "roughly 13,000 registered ICT positions on the books" for 2025 — "22 percent fewer than 2024"; newly registered were "roughly 39,000 openings, eleven percent fewer than the prior year."[3] And the agency draws a notable conclusion from it: for 2025, it found no general skills shortage in software development anymore.[3] Bitkom, meanwhile, maintains: "Germany continues to lack more than 100,000 IT specialists."[4] The decline isn't a one-year blip: IW Köln had already measured a 26.2 percent drop in open IT positions for 2024, with highly qualified experts falling from 40,369 to 26,753 — down 33.7 percent.[8] Two straight years of double-digit declines.

"Registered positions on the books" — why both can be right: The Federal Employment Agency doesn't count how many positions exist in Germany — it counts how many employers reported one to it. That's voluntary, and in IT especially, hiring usually runs through recruiters, a company's own careers page, or LinkedIn — the agency only sees a slice of that. "On the books" also means a snapshot, not an annual total. Think of it like a supermarket bulletin board: fewer flyers up there can mean fewer people are hiring — or that everyone has since moved their postings elsewhere. Bitkom measures something different: the association asks companies how many positions they themselves report as unfilled, regardless of where those are advertised. Both figures can therefore be true at once — and together, for Daniel, they show exactly what this board shows: there's demand, but access runs through intermediaries and platforms, not the official channel. 54 of today's 61 entries come from a single freelance platform; not one comes from the employment agency.

A side finding on Switzerland that explains the employee track. For a senior software engineer in Switzerland, Experteer.ch names an average salary of CHF 148,000 a year including bonus.[6] Other aggregators come in lower, in a band of roughly CHF 118,000 to 141,000 — though those figures come from search-result summaries rather than directly opened pages, so they only serve as a rough indication. The threshold at which this profile even considers a Swiss full-time role is CHF 150,000. That sits above the role's average in the country. So the employee track staying mostly empty isn't a market weakness — it's a deliberately high bar: this isn't about any Swiss position, but a clearly above-average one. Today's only candidate, ELCA in Zurich, carries a researched band of CHF 140–190k — the threshold sits right in the middle of it.
