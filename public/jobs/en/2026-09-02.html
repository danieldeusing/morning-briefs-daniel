Track 3: Four new tenders, three different kinds of no

Today's TED pull searches for notices published since Aug 28, with place of performance Germany or Austria and an IT-services CPV code. Result: 71 hits, four of them a stack match.[6] The rest are license purchases (VMware, SonarQube, Qlik Sense, IBM), pure consulting or project-management lots, or CPV-adjacent false hits — hardware, medical-device maintenance. They're logged as skip class, explicitly without individual review.

The four survivors snag on three different points. At ITZBund and the Federal Environment Agency, it's headcount. At Chemnitz, the eligibility bar is the lowest this track has ever seen — and it still doesn't fit, because what's being bought there is a product, not labor. At Wels, the subject matter is a bullseye, and of all places, that's the one where the requirements couldn't be read today.

Revenue hurdle vs. headcount hurdle — the difference decides everything: Public contracting authorities protect themselves in two very different ways. One kind asks for evidence: minimum annual revenue, insurance coverage, an ISO certificate, a reference list. The other kind asks for heads: five named individuals, each with a role, a CV, and their own evidence of experience. The distinction sounds like a formality, but it decides whether a two-person shop can even take part at all. Evidence can be bought in. Like building a house, where the owner doesn't calculate the structural engineering themselves but hires a structural engineer and submits their stamp: a partner brings in revenue, certificate, and reference, and the work still stays with the small bidder. Heads can't be bought in. Whoever fills three of five key roles does most of the work — and by that fact becomes, almost inevitably, the lead partner, while the small bidder becomes a subcontractor in its own bid. In practice: a revenue or certificate hurdle is worth chasing a partner for, because twiceD keeps the work; a minimum-headcount hurdle is only worth it if the role of supplier is acceptable. The deadlines on September 14 and 15 belong to the second group — which is why, despite a perfect stack match, they don't get listed here as an opportunity.

42,300 fewer jobs in the auto industry — and 109,000 unfilled IT positions

Germany's automotive industry employed 691,500 people in the first half of 2026 — 42,300 fewer than a year earlier (−5.8%) and the lowest count since the comparison series began in 2005. The decline runs 7.6% among suppliers and 6.1% among manufacturers.[4] Behind it stand named programs: BMW around 8,000 positions worldwide, the VW Group including Porsche and Audi on the order of 100,000 positions, Bosch up to 22,000 globally, ZF around 14,000 in Germany.[4]

At the same time, Bitkom reports 109,000 unfilled IT positions in Germany, with an average time-to-fill of 7.7 months; for roughly 70% of advertised expert roles, there is, by the numbers, no matching unemployed person in the market. That figure sits well below the 2023/24 peak of 149,000 — and the cited explanation for the drop is remarkably candid: not better recruiting, but the weaker economy.[5]

How both can be true at once: "The industry is cutting jobs" and "positions stay open for months" sound like a contradiction, but they're not — they're two different markets in the same country. Like a city where apartments sit empty and apartments are missing at the same time: the empty ones are in the wrong place and the wrong size. It's the same with people. Someone laid off from a gearbox plant isn't a Java cloud developer; the 7.7-month time-to-fill therefore doesn't measure a shortage of people, but a shortage of fit. For a freelancer, that's the distinction that matters: a shrinking sector doesn't automatically lower demand for external development work — it often rises, because work is cut internally and bought back in project by project. What the cuts lower is negotiating position. Today's board shows both sides: five automotive mandates remain on it, but not a single new one was added.

Two figures that explicitly do not appear in this brief today. First, the widely cited "1,600 positions" at VW's software subsidiary Cariad: the report resurfaces under 2026-dated aggregator pages, but the underlying heise article is clearly dated Mar 12, 2025, and describes cuts "by year-end" — meaning by the end of 2025. A year and a half old, not news from this week. Second, the 55,000 unfilled IT positions from Bitkom's own market-data overview: no visible date, not attributable to any reporting year — the datable 109,000 figure is used instead.

The first two fail on headcount. The Federal IT Center — ITZBund — is tendering a six-year framework contract with a maximum call-off volume of ten thousand one hundred ninety-five person-days. It requires five named specialists at expert or senior level: one systems architect, two developers, one systems integrator, one tester — each with their own evidence of experience. The bid deadline is Monday, September fourteenth. The Federal Environment Agency is looking for operation and further development of its DIVID platform, split into two lots, deadline Tuesday the fifteenth. The difference between these two kinds of hurdles is the heart of the matter. A revenue threshold, an insurance sum, an ISO certificate — those are pieces of evidence, and evidence can be bought in. A partner brings them along, and the work still stays with the small bidder. Heads can't be bought in. Whoever fills three of five key roles does most of the work and becomes, almost by definition, the lead partner — while the small bidder turns into a subcontractor on its own bid. That's why, despite a perfect stack match, neither of these two deadlines counts as an opportunity here.

The third fails on format. The city of Chemnitz has the lowest eligibility bar this track has ever seen: a single criterion, three comparable engagements, no revenue threshold, no headcount, no certificate. The catch is that this isn't a development contract at all — it's the rollout of finished software, roughly eighteen hundred licenses for about five thousand users. Whoever bids here is selling a product. That's not a staffing problem and not a financial problem — it's a business-model problem.

The new additions are also weaker than the existing board. Not one of the seven cracks the top tier; the highest sits at sixty-eight, while the board's ceiling is eighty-five. On rates, yesterday's figure holds up under a second reading. The freelancermap analysis puts software and web development at ninety euros an hour and all of IT at a median of ninety-five. More telling is the age breakdown: ninety-five euros for freelancers in their thirties, ninety-six for freelancers in their forties — one single euro of difference across an entire decade. Experience alone no longer moves the needle in this statistic. The same study's overall average has fallen for the first time, to one hundred three euros. The only figure pointing up is roughly one hundred twenty-one euros for freelancers with a recognized AI certification, nearly twenty percent above the average — though that number is thinly sourced, from a secondary summary, and counts here as a signal, not a price.

One detail from today's additions fits right alongside that. A Java backend mandate in a government setting describes itself, in its own ad, as very price-sensitive. So it carries the day's only band of eighty to one hundred euros an hour instead of the usual ninety to one hundred fifteen — and that lands exactly on the rate the study names for this segment. Whoever pitches there isn't negotiating for one hundred twenty.

On the demand side, two figures that look like they contradict each other. Germany's automotive industry employed six hundred ninety-one thousand five hundred people in the first half of the year, forty-two thousand three hundred fewer than a year earlier — the lowest count since two thousand five. At the same time, the industry association Bitkom reports one hundred nine thousand unfilled IT positions, with an average time-to-fill of seven point seven months. Both are true, because they're two different markets. Someone laid off from a gearbox plant isn't a Java cloud developer. The long time-to-fill doesn't measure a shortage of people — it measures a shortage of fit. For a freelancer, that means a shrinking sector doesn't lower demand for outside development work — it lowers your negotiating position.
