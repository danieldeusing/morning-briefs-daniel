The rate held, the days didn't: €6,653 at 42 hours

The Freelancer Compass 2026 survey polled over 5,400 freelancers across DACH between late 2025 and early 2026. Four figures from this survey need to be read together, because read alone they mislead:[1]

MetricPrior yearCurrentChange
Monthly income€8,432€6,653−€1,779 · −21%
Average hourly rate€104/h€103/h−€1 · −1%
Weekly working hours40h42h+2h · +5%
Share with < 50 project days a year24% (almost one in four)—

The hourly rate doesn't carry the collapse. One euro less doesn't explain a fifth less income, and more hours worked explains it even less — that points the other way. What's left is utilization: the share of worked time someone can actually invoice for.

Utilization — the number no freelancer puts on their website: An hourly rate is a price tag. Utilization is how often someone actually gets to the register. Like a taxi: the per-mile price is on the window and almost never changes — what decides the month is the share of miles driven with a paying passenger. Nobody pays for empty runs. Doing the math for this study: €6,653 at €103/h works out to roughly 65 billable hours a month — against 42 weekly hours, or a good 180 hours worked. (Our own calculation from the two study figures; the study doesn't say whether "income" means revenue or profit after costs — in the latter case there'd be more billable hours, but that wouldn't change the order of magnitude of the gap.) What follows: a discount on the hourly rate is the most expensive lever — it costs linearly and buys no extra days. The dial that actually moves this statistic is the number of weeks under contract, i.e. contract length and follow-on continuity. Practically, for this board: a 17-month framework at €95/h beats a six-week mandate at €115/h by a wide margin once the gap in between is realistically priced in.

The demand side confirms this from a second angle. An analysis of the IT freelancer landscape puts 43% without secured project utilization, order volume down 23% versus 2025, and 67% of companies cutting their contractor budgets. The automotive niche sits well below the IT average at −32%; the same analysis expects a stabilization at the earliest by late 2026.[4] Context: that's a consultancy secondary source dated March 2026, not a study report — the direction matches the Federal Employment Agency's primary figures (right column), and the percentages are simply sourced.

And the market size behind it: around 90,000 IT freelance projects were posted in Germany in 2025 — back to 2020 levels, after peaking at roughly 132,000 in 2022. Median project duration is six months, and the most sought-after skills remain SAP, Java, and DevOps.[3] For this profile, that's the single most useful line in the whole research: the core stack still sits high on the demand list. What's missing isn't fit — it's volume.

Official: shortages have "noticeably declined"

Today's strongest source isn't a portal survey but a report from the Federal Employment Agency ("The labor market for ICT occupations in the context of transformation"), published in June 2026, based on 2025 annual averages. It says four things that belong together:[5]

  • Demand has collapsed. The stock of registered ICT vacancies averaged just under 13,000 in 2025 — 22% less than the prior year. New postings, which say more about momentum, came in at 39,000, down 11%.
  • Unemployment is rising sharply. Just under 54,000 registered unemployed ICT professionals, +27% year over year, "the highest figure in recent years." The occupation-specific unemployment rate rose to 4.5% — for the expert subgroup it's markedly lower at 3.5%.
  • The skills shortage in the core segment is gone. Verbatim: "The long-standing shortages in software development have recently declined." In 2025 only unspecialized IT occupations and technical informatics at expert level still showed signs of a shortage.
  • Employment keeps growing anyway. 1.61 million employed ICT professionals, of whom over 1.15 million are in jobs subject to social insurance contributions — +2%. So fewer IT people aren't being employed; fewer are being sought.

And then there's a line an agency rarely writes this way. On the cause of the unemployment rise since 2023, it states: "driven by the weak economy and the growing use of artificial intelligence."[5] That's not a consultancy's forecast — it's the Federal Employment Agency's own labor market statistics, naming AI as a co-cause of a concrete rise in the numbers.

13,000 versus 109,000 — why two official-sounding numbers differ eightfold: The brief from 09/02 cited 109,000 unfilled IT positions per Bitkom; today's figure here is 13,000. Both are correct, because they count different things. The Federal Employment Agency only counts what's reported to it — a vacancy shows up there when an employer registers it with the agency, and many IT jobs run entirely through job boards, recruiters, and company career pages. Bitkom asks companies how many positions they'd like to fill. Like a restaurant: one number is the reserved tables in the book, the other is the owner's answer to "how full would you like to be?" Why the Federal Employment Agency's number leads here anyway: it's collected the same way year after year, has a defined reporting year, and so delivers a reliable trend — and the trend is minus 22%. You can't compare a survey's wish-number against last year; a registration statistic, you can.

The corporate proof point is SAP. The Walldorf-based group froze most hiring and internal travel in early July 2026; per an internal memo, the only exceptions are AI-focused hires plus customer-facing travel and travel with a direct AI connection. SAP cites its own rising AI costs — more production AI scenarios running means higher token usage.[7][6] For this board, that explains a pattern: 17 of the 87 listings carry an AI-integration tag, and today's two best-paid employee roles are exactly that.

Two figures deliberately not in this brief today. First, the circulating "−33% year-over-year drop in computer-science job ads": the Federal Employment Agency report gives −22% in stock and −11% in new postings for a defined period; the 33% figure couldn't be matched to any reporting period or population, and the article carrying it returned HTTP 403 on retrieval. Second, "highest level since records began in 2011": the agency itself writes only "the highest figure in recent years" and shows its series starting in 2015. This brief uses the dated primary figures.
