Two-thirds plan no price increase — and 95 listings almost never name a price

This week the Freelancer Compass 2026 survey delivers a number that outweighs the raw fee figures: 62% of respondents plan no price increase for 2026, and nine percent even intend to actively cut their rates.[1] That's a statement about behavior, not about outcomes — and it explains why the price side of this statistic has barely moved in a year: €103/h on average, versus €104/h the prior year. I cross-checked both figures directly on the study's own page today.[2]

One detail about the sample base worth knowing. The income section of the Freelancer Compass cites 1,310 respondents as its analysis base, while the study is reported elsewhere with over 5,400 participants.[2][1] That's not a contradiction — an overall study and the subset that answers an income question are different things. But the €6,653 figure rests on the smaller number, and anyone citing it should cite that too.

The asterisk behind the rate — why 86 of 95 bands on this board look made up and aren't: A band marked with * doesn't appear in the listing. It's a researched estimate drawn from industry benchmarks for role, stack, and region. Like a real-estate listing site that builds a range from neighboring sales for a house without a listed price: useful for context, but not the seller's offer. Why this is the rule rather than the exception: in the DACH freelance market, the rate is a matter for negotiation, and printing it gives away your negotiating position — that's why only 7 of 95 listings print a figure today, two name none at all, and the remaining 86 carry the asterisk. What follows in practice: the number on the board is an entry-point expectation, not a commitment. The only reliable price is the one you have in writing after the first conversation.

The researched bands sit close together: 48 of the 95 entries are at €90–115/h*, eleven at €100–130/h* — consistently the IoT and automotive niche, which this profile carries at a premium —, seven at €85–110/h*. The printed rates, by contrast, spread from €75 to €95/h.

The sober reading of the 62%: if two-thirds of the supply side freeze their prices and a tenth cuts them, then 2026 will be decided not by the rate but by the number of weeks booked. The board reflects this one to one — what's growing are long-duration mandates (iSAX's 28-month mandate, teclift's 12-month framework), not high-rate mandates.

Gap analysis on a real example Volume: minimum call-off 640 person-days, estimated 2,560, maximum 3,200 over at least two years — on its own that would be borderline but conceivable for a two-person setup. The hard gates sit elsewhere: it requires an average annual net revenue of at least €3.2 million over the last three fiscal years, plus at least one equivalent reference with a term ≥ 2 years, budget ≥ €3.2 million net, bidder share ≥ 80%, ≥ 3,200 person-days total volume, and ≥ 4 distinct specialist roles.[5] Verdict for a mandate of this shape: ⚠️ Partner needed. The partner would have to supply: the revenue and creditworthiness proof above €3.2 million net, the public-sector reference of comparable size — and the staffing depth for four named specialist roles. twiceD would bring the stack (Java, Spring, Angular, IAM) and the delivery, not the balance sheet. That's the realistic shape for this track: ride along, don't lead.

And the exception that proves it: of all the carried-over deadlines in the week ahead, the "Justus" technical procedure at the Stuttgart Higher Regional Court, at roughly 130 person-days a year, is the only shape whose volume a solo setup could carry without a partner.
