Verdict⚠️ Partner needed
Framework agreement with five lots — what's actually being awarded: A framework agreement isn't a contract — it's an entitlement to receive contracts: the authority locks in prices and terms for four years, then calls off work hours as needed. Like a tradesperson's contract with a property management company: the hourly rate is fixed, but nobody knows in advance how many hours will actually come up in a year. The "five lots" are five separate such contracts for the same service — the authority deliberately binds five providers at once and may award each bidder only one contract. In practice: you're competing for one of five slots, not for a single contract — structurally, the odds are better than in a single-award tender. And the €9.375 million is a four-year estimate, not guaranteed revenue.
Partnership gap — what's missing, specifically Revenue: a self-declaration is required covering net total revenue for the last three fiscal years, and separately revenue from IT services/software development. At a framework of €9.375 million per lot, the expected order of magnitude sits well above a two-person setup. Staffing: one technical point of contact plus two more key personnel, each with at least three years of experience in Racy10/Racy25; remaining staff with a relevant degree and at least five years of IT professional experience. References (lots 3–5 only): one to five company-level references each with a contract value of at least €1 million, plus two to five person-level project references with at least two years of deployment duration. Certificate: ISO 27001 based on BSI IT-Grundschutz — mandatory, not a nice-to-have. Plus professional liability insurance. Language: German at least C1 for everyone deployed, an exclusion criterion — the one point where twiceD isn't just meeting the bar but ahead of it.

Verdict: ⚠️ Partner needed. The partner would have to supply: the ISO 27001 certificate, revenue and creditworthiness proof at framework scale, at least one public-sector reference ≥ €1 million (for lots 3–5), and two more key personnel with Racy experience. twiceD would bring the stack, the C1 German, and the delivery — not the balance sheet and not the staffing depth. Wolfsburg freezes up — and the rate anchors contradict each other [2][3][4]

Volkswagen's supervisory board approved the "Future Plan 2030" on 09/03/2026. The core: roughly 50,000 more jobs are being cut group-wide — on top of the cuts already running since 2024, bringing the cumulative total to roughly 100,000 group-wide —, including management positions, the model lineup is being halved, and production is being throttled to around 9 million vehicles a year. For four plants — Hannover, Emden, Neckarsulm, Zwickau — "no competitive follow-on utilization" is planned between 2031 and 2034. The smaller story right next to it is immediately relevant for the labor market: a hiring freeze and a freeze on promotions into the top pay grade are already in effect.

Pay grade — why a "promotion freeze" hurts even without layoffs: In German industrial companies bound by collective agreements, pay doesn't depend on negotiating skill but on a pay grade: every job is assigned a numbered level, and the level determines the money. Like grade levels in school — you don't move up because you negotiated well, but because the job gets reassessed. A freeze on promotions into the top group therefore means: whoever is already doing the more demanding work still doesn't get the upgrade. Why this matters here: a company that neither hires nor promotes doesn't buy in external senior work either — and that's exactly what the automotive share of the freelance market lives on. Five entries on today's board carry the automotive tag, including the top scorer.

On the sourcing, importantly: both pieces of evidence are secondary sources — a financial portal from 09/04 and an independent confirmation of the same figure and the same decision date via a second report.[2][3] No VW primary document was fetched for this brief. The 50,000 figure and the 09/03 date are double-covered; the plant list and the 9-million figure hang on a single source.

Five identical lots, each estimated at roughly nine point three seven five million euros, around seventy-five thousand work hours over four years. The bid deadline is September twenty-fourth, ten a.m., electronic submission only. That's thirteen business days of lead time. I verified this tender myself, in full text, today.

Even so, it's not biddable solo. It requires a revenue declaration covering three fiscal years, a technical contact plus two more key people with at least three years of experience in exactly these architectures, company references worth at least one million euros for three of the five lots, and an ISO twenty-seven-thousand-one certificate based on Germany's BSI baseline security standard. So the verdict is: partner needed. A partner would have to bring the certificate, the balance sheet, the reference, and two more heads. twiceD would bring the stack, the delivery — and the required German at C-one level, the one spot where the small setup is actually ahead. Ride along, don't lead.

In the background sits the biggest German labor-market story of the week. Volkswagen's supervisory board approved a future plan on September third: roughly fifty thousand jobs disappear group-wide, the model lineup gets cut in half, and production is throttled to around nine million vehicles. The smaller story right next to it is the one that matters immediately for the job market: a hiring freeze is already in effect, along with a freeze on promotions into the top pay grade. A company that neither hires nor promotes doesn't buy in outside senior talent either — and that's exactly what the automotive slice of this market lives on. Both sources here are secondary; I didn't pull a document from Volkswagen itself.

And finally, a numbers conflict that calls for a judgment call. A recruiting firm published day rates for Java freelancers across German-speaking Europe on September third: nine hundred to twelve hundred euros a day, up to thirteen hundred with a documented microservices background. Worked out over an eight-hour day, that's a hundred twelve to a hundred fifty euros an hour. The board's dominant band, by contrast, sits at ninety to a hundred fifteen, and the highest printed rate remains ninety-five euros. By source rank, the study figure beats the blog of a placement agency that profits from a high published rate. The defensible reading: the day-rate figure is a best-case scenario for a narrow specialist profile, not the market average. It's still useful as an anchor for the top end, though — if the stack genuinely covers Spring Boot plus Kubernetes plus Amazon's cloud.
