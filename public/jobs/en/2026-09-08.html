What changed is the listing's title: "starting immediately, 5 months" became "starting immediately–12/31/2026 + option." And because the dedup key id is, per spec, a slug built from source + company + title, the key moved along with the title: …-api-loesungen-cloud-automatisierung-bonn became …-api-cloud-automatisierung-bonn. For the day-over-day comparison, those are two different entries. The real net-new count on the board today is four listings, not five — the other four carry URLs that appeared neither on 09/07 nor on 09/06.

Why a dedup key built from the title doesn't work: For a board to stay comparable day over day, every listing needs a stable identifier — otherwise there's no way to tell tomorrow whether a row is the same one as yesterday. Here, that identifier is derived from the listing's title. It's like recognizing neighbors not by their house number but by the nameplate on the door: if someone puts up a new plate, the system suddenly treats them as a different person — even though they never moved out. And staffing agencies change listing titles constantly, because the term, the start date, or a project ID lives right there in the title. In practice, that means: every "addition" has to be checked against the URL before it counts as a new opportunity — and every "departure" the same way, or a title change gets booked as a listing ending. The house number that actually works reliably here is the URL. A related finding of the same kind: the Munich Angular entry from Progressive (SThree GmbH) carries the key freelancermap-sinc-gmbh-angular-developer-95-remote-muenchen — a slug naming a company that appears nowhere in this entry's own data.

Finally, a stack-specific rate figure — and its catch [4]

The 09/07 issue ended with an open ask: what's missing is a stack-specific figure from a study rather than a placement-agency blog. That figure is here today. freelancermap's DACH hourly-rate analysis puts the median for the "software and web development" category at €90/h, and at €95/h across all IT disciplines. By age bracket: €80/h at 20–30 years, €95/h at 31–40, €96/h at 41–50.[4]

Median versus average — why the difference matters here: The median is the value in the middle: one half charges less, the other more. The average adds everything up and divides by the count — a single top earner pulls it upward, the median doesn't. Like income in a bar: if a billionaire walks in, the average jumps into the millions, while the person in the middle still earns exactly the same. That's exactly what explains the apparent contradiction with yesterday's figure: €103/h average across all freelance sectors versus €90/h median in the software category. Both can be true. For a negotiation, the median is the more honest number — it describes what a typical contract actually pays, not what a handful of outliers manage.

The skills shortage is cut in half — two numbers, one direction

On 09/03/2026, Bitkom released its annual survey on the IT skills shortage: 79,000 open IT positions in Germany. For comparison: 109,000 in 2025 and 149,000 in 2023 — the gap has practically halved in three years.[2] 17% of the open roles fall under software development and architecture, exactly the field this brief covers.[2]

A second survey measures something different and points the same way. An analysis of 37 IT professions (IW Köln, a 2024-vs-2023 comparison resurfaced in August 2026) found an average of 46,431 posted positions — 26.2% fewer than 2023; for the IT expert roles specifically, it was −33.7% year over year.[3] So the figures describe 2024, not 2026 — still useful as evidence of a continuing downward trend. The two figures must not be added together or converted into one another, and they don't contradict each other either: they simply count different things.

Open positions vs. posted positions — stock versus flow: "Open positions" is a stock: how many chairs are empty right now. "Posted positions" is a flow: how many chairs got advertised at all over a given period. Like a restaurant: the stock is how many tables are unoccupied right now; the flow is how many reservations came in today. Both can fall at the same time — and here, they do. Why that matters for a rate negotiation: a shrinking stock has two possible causes, and both lead to the same outcome. Either the gap got filled — in which case the pressure that once made clients pay any price is gone. Or demand fell — in which case that pressure is gone too. The shrinking flow makes the second reading more likely: if positions were simply being filled, more, not fewer, would have had to be posted beforehand. For freelance rates, that simply means less negotiating power than in 2023 — which fits a board whose top end has been stuck printing €95/h for days.

That's the one market figure this board can't produce on its own. 92 of the 108 entries come from a single platform family; a sample like that can't say how many positions exist nationwide. The Bitkom survey can — and its finding cuts against the narrative that IT talent is a scarce good the market is chasing after. It has been chasing after it less and less for three years. For this profile, that doesn't mean "bad market," it means a shift in the argument: away from availability, toward specificity. The 34 entries that require Java and Spring Boot and Angular together, and the five with an automotive angle, are the part of the board where a shrinking overall demand is least noticeable — because the pool of matching providers is already small there.
