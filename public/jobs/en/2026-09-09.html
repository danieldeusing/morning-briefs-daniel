VW cuts 50,000 jobs — and creates 9,000 in software

This week's biggest story for this profile doesn't live in the board, it lives right next to it. Volkswagen's supervisory board approved the "Future Plan 2030": roughly 50,000 jobs worldwide are disappearing — while, of all things, roughly 9,000 new ones are meant to appear in digitalization and software development.[1][2] The Bosch software cuts often set up as the counterpoint, though, do not hold up as a fresh counter-signal: the "1,200 jobs, 950 in Germany" figure comes from a report dated 01/19/2024, and a works-council agreement from October 2025 has since lowered the plan to roughly 1,500 instead of 1,850 jobs in Germany by end of 2027 — with operational layoffs ruled out through end of 2029.[3] The structural direction still holds — the OEM is pulling software capacity toward itself while its Tier 1 has been shrinking its own for two years — but the simultaneity isn't real. For an automotive-software profile, the VW news is therefore not a catastrophe headline, it's a shift headline — and it lands right in the niche this board carries as a premium. Alongside it, the first figure that confronts this board's rate optimism head-on: €103/h DACH average, down from €104/h last year — the first year without an increase since the survey began, and just €91/h for "development."[4] Meanwhile the board grows cleanly to 112 entries.

The OEM builds up software — at the supplier, the cuts have been running since 2024 [1][2][3]

Volkswagen's supervisory board unanimously approved the "Future Plan 2030." The key figure is in the company statement itself: "a Group-wide workforce adjustment of approximately 50,000 positions – including management roles – will be necessary."[1] Roughly 50,000 jobs worldwide, management roles included. The same statement names four plants — Emden, Zwickau, Hanover, and Neckarsulm — for which a concept for a competitive production structure must be in place by June 2027.[1]

But the figure that actually matters for this profile only shows up in the trade press. Alongside the cuts, Personalwirtschaft reports, "rund 9.000 neue Stellen in Zukunftsfeldern, wie der Digitalisierung, Software-Entwicklung" are meant to appear.[2] The reduction itself runs without layoffs: "Betriebsbedingte Kündigungen wurden ausgeschlossen", using partial retirement, early retirement, and voluntary severance agreements instead.[2] Production at the four plants reportedly ends in 2031 (Emden, Zwickau), 2032 (Hanover), and 2034 (Neckarsulm).[2]

"Socially responsible downsizing" — why that matters for freelancers: A company can get rid of jobs two ways. It can lay people off — then people are suddenly on the market from one month to the next. Or it can shrink through natural attrition: whoever retires isn't replaced; whoever leaves voluntarily gets paid to do so. Like draining a swimming pool: you can pull the plug — or just stop refilling it. The second route takes years. In practice, that means: 50,000 cut VW jobs don't turn into a sudden oversupply of experienced developers that pushes day rates down. But it does mean internal capacity disappears while software demand stays — and that gap is traditionally bridged with external contracts. So the effect on the rate side is more supportive than depressive, at least as long as the 9,000 new software jobs aren't filled faster than the old ones disappear.

And then there's the counter-move from the same industry — but, unlike what this brief's earlier research assumed, it isn't an event of this week. Bosch, Germany's largest automotive supplier, is indeed shrinking its software and vehicle-architecture unit "Cross-Domain Computing Solutions." The figure often cited for that — roughly 1,200 jobs, up to 950 of them in Germany — actually comes from a Handelsblatt article dated 01/19/2024, cross-checked against a same-dated report from automotiveit.eu. Verbatim there: "Die mögliche Personalreduzierung bewege sich nach aktuellem Stand im Bereich von ungefähr 1.200 Stellen weltweit – davon bis zu 950 in Deutschland."[3]

Since then, that plan has been superseded — downward. In October 2025, management and the works council reached an agreement after nearly a year of negotiation: "Bis Ende 2027 sollen nun rund 1500 Jobs wegfallen – und nicht 1850", implemented via early retirement, severance offers, and transfers to other Bosch divisions. And: "Betriebsbedingte Kündigungen sind bis Ende 2029 ausgeschlossen".[3] Bosch is therefore not a same-day counter-signal to the VW news, but a restructuring that's been running for two years, has since been softened, and is contractually capped through end of 2029. The structural direction — the OEM pulling software capacity toward itself, the Tier 1 shrinking its own — still holds. The drama of simultaneity was a dating error.

What honestly stays unclear here First, the date. The company statement on volkswagen-group.com carries the date 09/03/2026, while several German trade outlets date the supervisory-board decision to September 4, 2026. The one-day discrepancy couldn't be resolved; it doesn't change the figures, but it belongs in the record. Second, the Germany share. The VW statement names none. The trade press names, in a single text, both "rund 25.000 Stellen in Deutschland" and "konzernweit … rund 50.000 Stellen in Deutschland", and the aggregators from the earlier research round speak of "etwa der Hälfte".[2] These three figures rule each other out, which is why this brief states no Germany number. Third, Bosch — this is where the brief had to correct itself. The 1,200/950 figures had been logged during earlier research as a current story because they only ran through search summaries. Following up against the primary article showed: the Handelsblatt piece is dated 01/19/2024, and so is the cross-checked automotiveit.eu article. It isn't news from September 2026, and the phrase "almost simultaneous" was simply wrong. What's authoritative instead is the October 2025 works-council agreement (roughly 1,500 instead of 1,850 jobs in Germany by end of 2027, layoff exclusion through end of 2029) — those figures now appear above. Still not adopted: the Bosch totals of ~13,000 (September 2025) or ~22,000 (Handelsblatt report on the works-council agreement from 03/12/2026). They cover the entire Mobility division, not the software area, and are likewise not a story from this week.

What that means for the niche. This category carries automotive software as a premium niche with a €110–140/h target band. This week delivers exactly one fresh signal for that — the VW decision — everything else is multi-year background: demand for automotive software isn't disappearing, it's migrating. Away from the supplier that has been shrinking its own software capacity since 2024, toward the OEM building it up — and, historically true of any such shift, temporarily toward external contracts, because internal build-up cycles run slower than demand. This migration is a year-scale effect, not a week-scale one — for today's rate negotiation, it's a tailwind, not a trigger. On the board, that's five entries today, led by the Frank Recruitment tender at fit 85, reconfirmed live again today. Five out of 112 isn't a boom. But it's the part of the board where the number of matching providers is smallest — and therefore the part where a rate above €100/h even stays negotiable.

First year without an increase — and the board doesn't know it yet [4]

The Freelancer Compass, the largest DACH survey among IT freelancers, reports an average hourly rate of €103/h for 2026 — down from €104/h in 2025. Verbatim: "The average hourly rate for IT freelancers in the DACH region… in 2026 is €103, representing a slight decline from 2025's €104."[4] A one-euro difference is statistically nothing. The direction isn't: it's the first year the curve doesn't point up.

The breakdown by discipline is the more important half for this profile: management & IT consulting €121/h, SAP €113/h, IT infrastructure €100/h — and "development" just €91/h.[4] So the €103/h is a blended figure pulled upward by consulting and SAP mandates. Pure development work sits twelve euros below that. That lines up almost exactly with the figure this brief carried on 09/08: €90/h median for "software and web development" from a separate analysis on the same platform. Two different survey products, the same order of magnitude — that's the most solid statement this brief has made about rates so far.

Track 3: the only document ever read at the primary source has expired [5][6]

The tender from the Free State of Bavaria, via the State Ministry of Food, Agriculture, Forestry and Tourism (StMELF), stands unchanged on the calendar: five identical lots, each estimated at €9.375 million, with a maximum value of €14.0625 million, roughly 75,000 work hours per lot over four years, deadline 09/24/2026. Two independent aggregator mirrors agree on shape, value, and deadline.[5] The stack is a direct hit: Racy10 (Java, Apache Wicket, Spring, JPA) and Racy25 (Java/TypeScript, Angular, Spring, JPA — "the standard for new development work"). An ISO 27001 certification based on BSI IT-Grundschutz is mandatory, justified by the tie-in to the EU paying agency.[5] The notice wasn't re-fetched today; the last self-fetch of its status is 09/08.

What's structurally missing on this track The canonical TED number for this tender again couldn't be resolved. The detail pages on ted.europa.eu are built client-side and returned an empty response on every fetch attempt. This brief's entire Track 3 entry therefore rests on mirrors, never on the primary document — it's solid because two independent mirrors carry the same figures, not because the source was verified. Anyone who wants to look up the case themselves should search TED for "Rahmenvereinbarungen Programmier- und Beratungsleistungen zur Anwendungsentwicklung"; bid submission runs only via eVergabe Bayern behind the bidder login anyway.

A real primary document does exist today, though — it just belongs to a different, expired procedure. TED notice 86435-2026 ("IT-Dienstleistungen für die Java-Softwareentwicklung", Bavarian State Tax Office, Munich) could be read in full as a PDF. Its deadline for requests to participate ran out on 02/23/2026 — it's not an opportunity. But as a benchmark for what a Bavarian Java procedure demands financially, it's the most precise thing this brief has so far. Verbatim: "Bestätigen Sie, dass der durchschnittliche Jahresumsatz Ihres Unternehmens in den vergangenen drei Jahren mindestens 3.200.000 Euro netto betragen hat." And on staffing: "Projektleitung, Java Entwickler (Front- und Backend), Java Architekt, Test Engineer".[6]

Minimum annual revenue — why a public authority asks for it: Contracting authorities are allowed to require bidders to be financially stable enough to actually see the contract through. The usual yardstick is average annual revenue over the last three years. Like a bank wanting to see a pay stub before issuing a loan — not because the salary repays the loan, but because it shows the borrower can carry it. The threshold is often roughly double the annual contract value. In practice for twiceD: a €3.2 million minimum revenue is an order of magnitude a two-person setup doesn't reach — and it can't be negotiated away either. It's not an argument, it's a filter. That's exactly why the partner question isn't optional for this contract class, it's the entry ticket: the partner brings the balance sheet, twiceD brings the stack and the language.
Partnership gap — status unchanged For the active StMELF tender, only ISO 27001 based on BSI IT-Grundschutz is documented beyond doubt as a mandatory criterion so far.[5] The extended criteria list captured on 09/06–09/07 (professional liability insurance, revenue self-declaration over three fiscal years, one technical point of contact plus two key personnel each with three years of Racy experience, references from €1 million for lots 3–5, German C1) hasn't been re-read since and stands here as a carryover. The €3.2 million minimum revenue explicitly does NOT apply to this procedure — it comes from a different, expired Bavarian Java tender and serves only as an order-of-magnitude reference.[6] Verdict remains: ⚠️ Partner needed. The partner supplies the certificate, the balance sheet, and the million-euro reference; the realistic shape stays "ride along, don't lead." The one point where twiceD has the edge remains the language.

Honest about coverage: this track's volume target is 3–10 tenders per day. Today, for the fourth day running, it's one. The reason remains the access route, not the market: the TED search API isn't reachable via the available fetch tools, the detail pages are JS shells, and the mirrors stepping in supply shape and deadline but rarely the eligibility criteria. As long as that stays true, this brief's Track 3 coverage is incomplete — and that's a statement about the tool, not about the number of open tenders.

It's Wednesday, September ninth. Today, for once, the most important story in this brief doesn't live in the jobs board itself — it's the story sitting right next to it, and it lands squarely in the niche this profile treats as premium.

Volkswagen's supervisory board has approved its Future Plan 2030. The company's own statement calls for a group-wide adjustment of roughly fifty thousand positions, management roles included. Four plants — Emden, Zwickau, Hanover, and Neckarsulm — need a plan for a competitive production setup by June of twenty twenty-seven; trade press reports say production there winds down sometime between twenty thirty-one and twenty thirty-four.

But the number that actually matters for this profile shows up in the trade press, not the company statement. Alongside the cuts, roughly nine thousand new positions are meant to appear — explicitly in future fields like digitalization and software development. And the reduction runs without layoffs: no forced terminations, just partial retirement, early retirement, and voluntary buyouts. Think of it like draining a swimming pool: you can pull the plug, or you can simply stop refilling it. Volkswagen isn't refilling it. Fifty thousand cut jobs, handled that way, don't turn into a sudden glut of developers that drags day rates down.

One correction belongs here. The move often cited as the counterpoint is Bosch's software-sector cuts: roughly twelve hundred jobs, nine hundred fifty of them in Germany. But that figure isn't news from this week — it dates back to January nineteenth, twenty twenty-four. The current, authoritative status is a works-council agreement from October twenty twenty-five: in the software and vehicle-architecture division, roughly fifteen hundred jobs are now set to go by the end of twenty twenty-seven, down from a planned eighteen hundred fifty, handled through early retirement, severance, and transfers — with forced terminations ruled out there through the end of twenty twenty-nine. Bosch, in other words, is background, not a fresh counter-signal. The structural direction still holds: the automaker is pulling software capacity toward itself while its supplier has been shrinking its own for two years — and shifts like that traditionally get bridged with outside contracts, because internal hiring moves slower than demand. That said, this is a year-scale effect, not a week-scale one.
