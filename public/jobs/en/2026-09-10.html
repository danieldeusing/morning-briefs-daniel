Same survey, two published results — and only one figure holds [1][2]

The 09/09 issue carried the Freelancer Compass 2026 with a DACH average of €103/h and €91/h for "Development", drawn from the publisher's English study page (as of 08/05/2026, a survey of more than 5,000 freelancers).[2] Today the German version of the same study was read at the primary source — freelancermap.de, published on 07/02/2026, author named, stated IT sample n = 620. It reads: „IT-Freelancer im DACH-Raum verlangen einen Stundensatz von 95 Euro", and further: „Software- und Webentwicklung bildet mit 90 Euro das untere Ende".[1]

Both versions carry the same study name. Four of five lines diverge. And they don't diverge in one direction, but every which way: SAP runs higher in the German version (120 instead of 113), IT infrastructure lower (95 instead of 100), the overall figure eight euros below. A pure median-versus-average effect doesn't explain that, because it would have to pull every line the same direction. More likely are different survey dates and a markedly different sample cut (620 versus more than 5,000). The contradiction couldn't be resolved, and neither side references the other.

Median versus average — why that isn't the same thing in a negotiation: The average adds up all the values and divides by the count. The median is the value exactly in the middle once you line everyone up by size. Picture ten kids in a class, each with five euros of allowance — and one with a thousand. The average is over a hundred euros, the median is five. The average describes no one in that case. For hourly rates, the "thousand euros" are the consulting and SAP mandates. In practice: if someone in a conversation says "the market average is 103," that doesn't describe the price of development work — it describes a blended figure pulled upward by other disciplines. The number that counts is the one for your own discipline — and in both versions, that's roughly 90.

VW: 50,000 or 100,000 — and why this brief sticks with 50,000 [3][4][5]

On 09/04/2026, Euronews published a report under a headline stating Volkswagen would cut 100,000 jobs. It states verbatim that management and unions had agreed to "a further 50,000 jobs by the end of the decade", "bringing total losses to 100,000". And further: "The total 100,000 cuts will be the largest restructuring ever carried out in the global automotive industry, amounting to about 15% of the carmaker's staff worldwide." The piece explicitly places that in a lineage: General Motors in 1991 with 74,000 jobs and 21 plants, GM in 2009 with a further 47,000, Ford in 2002 with 35,000.[3]

Volkswagen's own company statement doesn't know this figure. It was read in the original for this brief on 09/09, and at the key point it reads: "a Group-wide workforce adjustment of approximately 50,000 positions – including management roles – will be necessary."[4] Roughly 50,000, group-wide, management roles included. No mention of 100,000, no mention of 15 percent, no comparison with GM or Ford.

Both figures can be true at once — but only once you know what's being added together. Euronews adds today's decision on top of previously announced programs to arrive at the sum. The 100,000 is therefore the outlet's own calculation, not a published VW figure. The report also doesn't name which individual programs make up the "50,000 already planned" — without that derivation, the sum can't be verified. That's why this brief keeps the primary figure: roughly 50,000 from today's decision. The 100,000 is carried as what it is: a plausible but unsubstantiated aggregation by a reputable outlet.

What the Euronews report doesn't contain — and what that means for this profile The one figure that actually matters for a software profile is missing entirely. The report doesn't mention, in a single word, that new jobs are appearing in parallel. That detail comes exclusively from the German trade press: „rund 9.000 neue Stellen in Zukunftsfeldern, wie der Digitalisierung, Software-Entwicklung", plus the explicit exclusion of operational layoffs.[5] Anyone reading only the 100,000 story sees a wipeout; anyone reading the company statement plus the trade press sees a shift. For this board's automotive niche, that difference matters: a socially responsible reduction via partial retirement and early retirement doesn't flood the freelance market with experienced developers and doesn't push rates down — it creates an internal capacity gap while software demand stays. Once again not adopted is the circulating Bosch figure of roughly 22,000 jobs (works-council agreement, reported 03/13/2026): it covers the entire supplier division in Germany through 2029–2032, not the software organization, and isn't a story from this week.[9]

On the board, none of that shows up anywhere today. Automotive stays at five entries — but with a swapped roster: the Akkodis tender in Aachen (€100–130/h*) departed, and Computer Futures moved in with an e-mobility backend in Düsseldorf (fit 70, 100% remote). What's notable about this listing is what it doesn't say: the client references a "market-standard nearshore range," and the actual figure is redacted on the page. That's the most honest rate signal on the entire board — a reference to nearshore pricing is an early warning, not a negotiating offer.

Track 3: nothing active today — but the figure that's the real filter [7][8]

No matching TED tender in the filter today. For this issue, no active procurement with a submission deadline on or after 09/17 — the five-business-day lead time the spec requires — could be independently verified. The reason remains the access route, not the market: the TED search interface accepts only POST requests, and the available fetch tools can only do GET, which gets HTTP 405 back; the detail pages on ted.europa.eu are client-side rendered shells. A framework agreement with lots — and why "staff provisioning" is different from a project: A public authority often doesn't know exactly how much work will come up next year. Instead of tendering every order separately, it signs a framework contract: a supplier is preselected and then called on as needed. A lot is a portion of that, awarded separately — here, one per programming language. Like a catering contract for a school: you're not ordering one menu, you're securing a promise that someone can deliver two hundred meals every business day. That's exactly the difference from project procurement: the question isn't "can you build this," it's "can you provide N people starting day one and hold that for four years." In practice for twiceD: €300,000 in annual revenue per lot is a range a small GmbH can talk about. Being able to provide four senior Java developers at the same time is not — and that hurdle can't be closed with more work, only with a partner who supplies the headcount.

That's the real takeaway of the day for this track, and it corrects an assumption from earlier issues. Until now, this brief's partner question always ran through the balance sheet: the €3.2 million threshold from the Bavarian procedure is a filter a two-person setup never reaches.[8] The Baden-Württemberg procedure shows an entirely different class: €300,000 minimum revenue per lot, references from €200,000 — figures that, in principle, are within reach for a small GmbH. And yet the verdict stays ⚠️ Partner needed, just for a different reason: the binding filter here is the required headcount. Anyone who can't demonstrate four senior Java developers doesn't get into the procedure, no matter how healthy the balance sheet looks.

Partnership gap — what a partner would actually need to supply in this contract class For a staff-provisioning framework agreement on the pattern of 268718-2026: four named senior Java developers with an availability commitment over 48 months (twiceD realistically supplies one of them), two reference projects with ≥ €200,000 contract volume each in the public sector, plus the revenue self-declaration above €300,000/year net for the respective lot. What twiceD brings to this setup is the stack and the language — not the capacity. The realistic shape remains "ride along, don't lead": as a named key person in a larger bidder's proposal, not as the bidder itself. Honest about coverage: this track's volume target is 3–10 tenders per day. Today it's zero active, the fifth day running below target. That's a statement about the tool, not about the number of open tenders — as long as the TED search is only reachable via POST, this track stays structurally incomplete.

It's Thursday, September tenth. This brief's most important find today isn't a news story, it's a contradiction — and that contradiction happens to deliver the most reliable rate figure this brief has ever had.

The largest freelancer survey in the German-speaking world, the Freelancer Compass twenty twenty-six, exists in two published versions from the same publisher. The German version, from July second, puts the DACH average at ninety-five euros an hour. The English study page, as of August fifth, puts it at a hundred three. SAP is off by seven euros, infrastructure by five, and the disciplines don't even rank in the same order. The two sides couldn't be reconciled; their samples differ by a factor of eight — six hundred twenty respondents against more than five thousand.

Exactly one line holds across both versions. Software and web development comes in at ninety euros in one, ninety-one in the other. Two different cuts of the same survey, published five weeks apart, and for development work both arrive at the same number. That's the most solid rate statement this brief has ever made — and it also says something about where that work sits: ninety euros is the bottom of the IT scale, not the middle. Consulting, SAP, and data work all sit above it.

There's a distinction worth knowing for any conversation about this. The average adds everything up and divides by the count; the median is the value sitting right in the middle. Picture ten kids, each with five euros of allowance — and one with a thousand. The average comes out above a hundred, the median stays at five, and the average describes nobody. So when someone tells you the market average is a hundred three, that's not the price of development work — it's a blended figure pulled upward by other disciplines entirely.
