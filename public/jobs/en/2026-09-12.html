The rate falls for the first time — and Daniel's segment is at the bottom

The Freelancer Compass 2026 is the largest recurring survey of the German freelance market; 5,400 self-employed professionals were surveyed. The finding, verbatim: "The average hourly rate has fallen for the first time – to 103 euros."[1] That's the more interesting news than the figure itself — this series had only ever moved in one direction before.

The cross-check holds. freelance.de surveyed independently between January 27 and March 2 and arrives at "around 102 euros."[3] Two separate samples, one euro apart: the €100/h level is now documented as the market midpoint rather than estimated — exactly what this brief had been missing all week.

Average vs. median: €103/h is the average across all IT segments — every rate added up, divided by the number of respondents. A single SAP consultant at €200/h pulls it upward. The €90/h for software & web, by contrast, is a median: the middle value when every rate in the segment is lined up by size. The median is the more honest yardstick, because individual top earners can't skew it. So comparing the two numbers isn't quite comparing like with like — but they point in the same direction.

The uncomfortable line is in the segment breakdown. Verbatim: "Software and web development forms the lower end at 90 euros. With 356 respondents, though, it's also by far the largest and most heterogeneous group, from frontend developer to cloud architect."[2] Above it sit IT infrastructure at 95, data & analytics at 100, SAP & ERP at 120, and consulting at €121/h.[1][2]

What that means for the target band. Daniel's baseline starts at €100/h for standard Java/Angular work — ten euros above the median of his own segment. That's not a contradiction, it's positioning: the €90/h figure averages the entry-level frontend developer with the cloud architect. The consequence is still concrete: the six printed hourly rates on today's board, with a median of €82.5/h, sit not just below the target band but below the market median. Anyone negotiating there is negotiating against the market, not just against a personal baseline.

On the demand side that explains the price: the same survey reports the number of published projects well below the 2022 peak and around 70% of freelancers expecting a difficult market in 2026.[3] Falling rates alongside a falling project count is the expected combination — the opposite would be the surprise.

Two layoff headlines, and neither is new

This brief's DACH backdrop remains negative, but today it isn't news either — both headline numbers are cumulative totals that have simply been added up again.

Volkswagen. The company's own statement on "Future Plan 2030" states verbatim "a Group-wide workforce adjustment of approximately 50,000 positions – including management roles" and frames it as going beyond existing programs.[4] The reading circulating in secondary sources — that total cuts would thereby double to 100,000 positions by 2030 — does not appear in VW's own statement, and neither does a Germany-specific share. This brief therefore continues to report only the 50,000 figure, with no German subtotal.

Bosch. Here the opposite mistake is the tempting one. The report states verbatim: "In the Mobility division, around 22,000 jobs in Germany are set to be cut, according to the figures communicated so far" — "according to the figures communicated so far," i.e. cumulative. The widely cited 13,000 figure is part of that total and comes from an announcement in September 2025.[5] Reading both numbers side by side as fresh news counts the same cuts twice.

What does get concrete is a single site instead: Nuremberg, around 1,800 employees, high-pressure pumps for gasoline injection plus components for hydrogen and fuel-cell technology. The site-security agreement expires at the end of 2026; labor representatives cite "significant staff cuts" as an option under review and do not "rule out a long-term closure" if no new products materialize. No figure has been published for the site itself.[5]

Site-security agreement: a fixed-term contract between employer and works council that rules out layoffs and plant closures for a set period — usually in exchange for concessions from the workforce. Like a lease with eviction protection: as long as it runs, nobody can be shown the door; once it expires, the normal rules apply again. That's why "expires at the end of 2026" is the real story — not because something happens then, but because nothing stands in the way anymore.

SAP, for context: on July 2, 2026, the company "tightened its policies on new hires and business travel" to free up funds for AI investment. That's SAP's own wording — "hiring freeze" was Bloomberg's characterization, and an explicit carve-out for AI roles isn't documented in SAP's own account.[6] For the employee track, that means: no freeze, but a longer path through approval.

It's Saturday, September twelfth. For weeks, this brief couldn't cite a solid outside number for hourly rates, because every source we checked was either outdated or contradicted itself. Today, one holds up — and it points down.

The Freelancer Compass two thousand twenty-six, the largest recurring survey of Germany's freelance market, polled five thousand four hundred self-employed professionals. It reports the first decline in the average hourly rate since the survey began. The new figure: one hundred three euros an hour. The notable part isn't the amount — it's the direction. This number had never fallen before.

A second platform backs it up. Freelance dot de ran its own independent survey between late January and early March and landed at roughly one hundred two euros. Two separate surveys, one euro apart — that's about as close to confirmation as it gets.

The less comfortable number sits one line down. Broken out by specialty, software and web development comes in lowest of all I-T segments, at ninety euros. Above it: I-T infrastructure at ninety-five, data and analytics at one hundred, S-A-P and E-R-P at one hundred twenty, and consulting at one hundred twenty-one. Daniel's own target starts at one hundred euros an hour — ten euros above the median for his segment. That's not a mismatch, it's positioning: the ninety-euro figure averages an entry-level frontend developer with a senior cloud architect.

Worth keeping straight: the one-hundred-three figure is an average across every I-T segment, which a single high earner can pull upward. The ninety-euro figure is a median — the actual midpoint of the segment — and it's the more honest number, because top earners can't skew it.

That split shows up concretely on today's board. The six listings that actually print an hourly rate come out to a median of eighty-two fifty — below Daniel's target and below the overall market median. On the demand side, that lines up with the rest of the survey: published project volume remains well under its twenty twenty-two peak, and about seventy percent of freelancers expect a difficult market this year.

The listings board itself grew from one hundred seventeen entries to one hundred twenty-five — eight additions, zero removals, for the second day running. That zero isn't a market signal; it's a method artifact. A total of eighteen listings were checked today: eight brand-new, plus ten already-known listings reconfirmed. The listing states no rate at all — at a target band of one hundred ten to one hundred forty euros an hour for I-o-T work, that's not a gap, it's room to negotiate.
