Rank 1 has been advertising since October 2024 — and the board can't tell [1][6]

An apply button is active; there's no posting or update date.

Same story at rank 4, teclift, Fit 82: "Start: October 1, 2024", "Term: 12 months ++". The listing title only says "from Oct 1" with no year — and that's exactly what turned into a project start of Oct 1, 2026 in the last three issues' week-ahead. That line was wrong and is corrected today.

Not everything old is dead. Of the five listings opened, three are plausibly current: iSAX (Fit 83) states "Start: 9/2025" with "Duration: 28 months", so it nominally runs to early 2028; Keil Business Solutions (Fit 80) states a start of August 1, 2026; and, of all things, the oldest entry on the entire board — mund consulting, on the board for 103 days — states a start of November 1, 2026, making it the opposite of a zombie. Board age and listing age are two different things. What matters is the start date on the page, and that's exactly what the aggregation doesn't read today.

Recruiter pool listing: Staffing agencies don't post projects only to fill an open role — they also post them to collect résumés for their candidate database. Like a restaurant that leaves a dish on the menu in the window even though it's been off the menu for a year — the menu still pulls people in, and something else turns up instead. In practice that means: "listing still online" is no proof the project still exists. Loading the page can't tell a dead listing from a live one — only the start and duration field can, and even that's only a clue.

And the board preferentially collects the old good ones. Three hourly rates that aren't measuring the same thing

The Daily Rate Benchmark 2026 puts IT/software development at €110–130/h, equivalent to €880–1,040 per day.[2] The freelancer directory puts the IT|Software category at €95/h and, across all segments, at €103/h.[3] The board listings that print a number at all sit at a median of €82.5/h.[1] Three figures with over €45 of spread — and none of them is wrong.

Survey rate versus listing rate: The €110–130/h figure is self-reported by freelancers: what they end up billing, usually direct to the end client, usually as an experienced specialist. The €82.5/h on the board, by contrast, is an agency's offer: what a staffing firm pays after deducting its own margin. Like the difference between what a book costs in the store and what the publisher pays the author — same product, two stops along the way. The €95/h category average sits in between, because it blends both paths. Read only the board figures and you'll think the market is worse than it is; read only the survey and you'll think it's better.

In practice, for the €100/h+ target: going through an agency, it's out of reach on the current board's six printed examples — the highest printed rate is €95/h. It's reachable within the researched band of €90–115/h*, but only at the top edge, and in the niche IoT/automotive segment, where this brief pegs €110–140/h. The premium comes from the niche, not from negotiation.

Why less new supply is arriving: the project base is shrinking [4]

The standstill at the top of the board has a counterpart in the market. On freelance.de, the number of published projects fell from 97,400 (2022) to 56,000 (2025) — the lowest level since 2015. In the IT sector the decline is sharper: 34,000 (2023) → 27,000 (2024) → roughly 17,000 (2025). IT & Development's share of all the platform's project sources fell from 29% (2025) to 20% (2026).

The effect shows up in search time, not in price. 26% of freelancers needed over three months to land their next contract in 2026, up from 20% before; only 3% found something within a week, down from 5%. Hourly rates, meanwhile, stay roughly stable — what's being negotiated isn't the price, it's the wait.

For this board, that's the real explanation. Two new entries on a Monday isn't an aggregation error; there simply isn't much new supply coming in. And the less that comes in, the more it matters that old entries never get removed — a board that only grows looks healthiest in a shrinking market.
