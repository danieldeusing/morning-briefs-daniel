Why there's no verdict here: the volume is the first in weeks that a two-person shop could actually carry — 205 person-days a year is pretty much exactly one person, year-round. But (1) the deadline breaks this brief's five-business-day rule at three business days, and (2) the decisive point — whether the stack is even Java/Angular — sits in documents that, per the contracting authority, only become available "after submission of a fully completed and signed non-disclosure agreement." A ✅ or ⚠️ here would be a guess. The role description names a Puppet developer, which points more toward operations than application development.
Framework agreement with lots: an authority knows it will need development work for years, just not which kind. Instead of tendering every project separately, it awards a framework once and draws on it later. Like a tradesperson's contract with a property management company: the job isn't "paint apartment 4," it's "be our painter for three years." A lot is a standalone portion of that framework, awarded separately — Bavaria has five of them, identical in content, to have several suppliers under contract in parallel. In practice, that means the tendered value isn't a revenue promise, it's a ceiling. Whoever wins still has to hold the capacity ready as if everything got called off.

The truly usable figure sits in the Bavarian tender, and it isn't the volume. €9.375 million over 75,000 hours works out to exactly €125 per hour; the framework ceiling of €14.0625 million equals €187.50. That's the price at which a German ministry calculates Java development in 2026 — 51% above the median of the six hourly rates that are printed on the board at all (€82.50), and a third above the Compass median of €95. The gap isn't a math error, it's the chain: between ministry and developer, the procurement model has a general contractor and its margin; the freelance market has a broker and its margin. Anyone who wants to bill €125 an hour has to get closer to the client — not negotiate harder.

The barometer is rising — the layoff figure cited alongside it doesn't check out [2][3]

The only market figure primary-sourced today is a good one: the ifo Employment Barometer rose to 94.8 points in August, up from 93.0 in July — in ifo's own words, "the highest level since May 2025." In April of this year, the same index still stood at 91.3, the lowest level since May 2020. Over five months, that's a clear reversal.

Employment barometer: every month, the ifo Institute asks thousands of companies whether they plan to employ more, the same number, or fewer people over the coming three months, and converts the answers into a single figure. Like polling farmers on whether they expect rain: what's measured isn't the rainfall, it's the expectation. In practice, that means a rise from 93.0 to 94.8 means fewer companies are planning cuts than in July — not that anyone is hiring. For a freelancer, that's a leading indicator for fourth-quarter project budgets, not a signal for this week.

The counter-figure to the barometer is real — this corrects my own caution from yesterday. Volkswagen's supervisory board unanimously approved a transformation program on Sep 3, 2026: another 50,000 jobs worldwide are being cut — on top of the 50,000-job reduction that's been underway since late 2024. Why the "board median of €102.50/h" isn't a market figure [1][4]

87 of the 132 entries carry an hourly-rate band in euros; the median of their midpoints is €102.50/h, the range €90–115/h. This figure looks like a finding about the market — but it's essentially a finding about this brief. Of the 132 bands, 104 are researched (marked with * on the board), meaning: not stated by the listing, but derived from this profile's own benchmark bands (senior Java remote DE €90–120/h, IoT/automotive €110–140/h). A median over predominantly self-assigned values can't confirm the benchmark it was derived from.

Circular reasoning in a self-estimated figure: anyone who estimates a room's temperature, writes it down, and then treats that note the next day as proof the estimate was right hasn't measured anything. That's exactly how a median over researched rate bands behaves relative to the benchmark they were derived from. Only figures that appear in the listing itself are independent observations.

Six hourly rates are independent today: €95 · 87 · 85 · 80 · 80 · 75/h, median €82.50/h — the same six as on prior days, all posted through brokers. Alongside them stand 13 printed figures in total, including three annual salaries and one CH figure (Bank Julius Bär, CHF 121,500–206,500). 15 listings state nothing at all.

So the honest triad looks like this: €82.50/h is what brokers actually print on this board. €95/h is the median freelancers self-report across all IT disciplines in the 2026 Compass survey — for "Software & Web Development" specifically, just €90/h, for SAP & ERP, €120/h.[4] €125/h is what a ministry calculates for the same work. The €100/h+ target sits between the second and third figure — that is, outside what this board yields via brokers. The premium comes from the niche or from a direct relationship, not from negotiating with a staffing agency.

It's Tuesday, September fifteenth. The tender track is back after two empty days, and it's brought two tenders that are unbiddable for exactly opposite reasons. One's too big, the other's too late. Together, they make a better lesson than either one would have on its own.
