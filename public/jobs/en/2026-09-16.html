Two readings, and neither is decidable today: either there really is no technical hurdle (then this would be this track's first ✅ in weeks), or the technical criteria sit in a part of the eForm the interface doesn't surface. The full text wasn't readable today — hence a flag instead of a verdict. Recommendation: open this one notice by hand. It's the only one of the three where the effort could pay off.[4]
Eligibility vs. award criteria: A public tender runs in two rounds. In the first, it's checked whether a bidder is even allowed to compete — revenue, headcount, references, insurance, certificates. Those are the eligibility criteria, and they work like a driver's license: without it you can't enter the race, no matter how fast you drive. Only in the second round do the award criteria — price and concept — decide who wins. In practice that means: a perfect stack match is an argument for round two, and completely worthless if you fail round one. That's exactly what happens with the Federal Highway Research Institute lot: the tech list is the racetrack, the road-construction reference is the license.

The day's real insight sits in the comparison between the two hurdles, not in either tender alone. GIZ demands €1.5 million in revenue and ten heads. That's a hard number, but it's a purchasable number: in a bidding consortium, the larger partner brings the balance sheet, twiceD brings the development work, and formally the threshold is met. The Federal Highway Research Institute demands three of five years of project work with road-construction authorities. That threshold cannot be bought and cannot be borrowed either — it only exists because someone has already worked on such a project once. That leads to an uncomfortable but clear order of operations: for a two-person shop, the path into public contracts doesn't run through the first solo bid, but through the first subcontract under someone who already holds the sector reference. The stack match isn't worthless in that scenario — it's exactly what you'd apply with to that someone.

The shortage has halved, the difficulty hasn't [8][9]

The day's most solid market figure is one that sounds like an all-clear at first glance and isn't one on the second. Bitkom reports on September 3, 2026: 79,000 IT professionals are currently missing in Germany — down from 109,000 the prior year and 149,000 in 2023. The shortage has nearly halved in three years. In the same breath comes the number that reopens the whole picture: 97% of companies with open IT positions have trouble filling them, and 78% observe a shortage at all.

Why both numbers are true at once: picture a game of musical chairs. In 2023 there were 149,000 chairs in the room, today only 79,000 — so the game is played less often. But the chairs that remain have an unusual shape: not everyone fits them. The first number measures the volume of open positions, the second measures how well the requirements fit. Less demand overall, but the demand that's left has become more specialized — more senior, more niche stacks, less "anyone who can code." For a profile with thirteen years of experience and a narrow niche, that's the better half of the statistic; for entry-level candidates, the worse one.

The rate side confirms the finding by not moving: €101.70 average across all disciplines in 2026, up from €100.55 the prior year — nominally 1.1% higher, practically a standstill.[6] IT sits at €101.98, almost exactly on the overall average — so no IT premium. A second platform survey puts it at €103, along with the number that explains the flat rate: 49% of respondents report a worse pipeline than the prior year.[7] A market where nearly one in two sees fewer inquiries doesn't raise prices.

The supply side shows the opposite pole. SAP, Germany's largest software employer, imposed a broad hiring freeze in August per an internal memo; explicitly exempted are only core roles in artificial intelligence. That followed the 2024 restructuring program's cuts of roughly 10,000 positions, 3,500 of them in Germany; the group currently employs about 110,600 people worldwide, roughly 25,000 of them in Germany.[9] For this brief's employee track, that unchanged means: the largest DACH employer in the stack is closed as a target — except for AI roles. That's not a special case, it's the same pattern as in the Bitkom numbers: the door is narrower, but it stands open exactly where the need is specialized.

It's Wednesday, September 16th. Today's tender track delivers three hits, and what makes them interesting is that they show three different kinds of hurdle. One you can buy. One you can't buy. And one you can't even see from the outside.

This is the closest stack match this brief has ever printed. No security clearance is needed, the submission language is German, and there's no revenue or headcount threshold in the notice.

And yet it's not biddable. What's required is three of the last five years of IT project work with public road-construction authorities. Plus implementation experience with the bridge-inspection codes A-S-B-Ing, R-I-E-B-W-Prüf, and D-I-N ten seventy-six. twiceD has references in automotive, in defense and avionics electronics, and in industrial work. But no road-construction authority. German at C-two level for project management and technical contacts is the only one of these criteria that's fully met.

The second tender comes from G-I-Z, the German development agency, and it's the opposite case. Here the hurdle is pure arithmetic, and all four numbers are spelled out in the text. At least three reference projects in content management with OpenText, each at least five hundred thousand euros net, all within the last thirty-six months. Average annual revenue over the last three fiscal years of at least one point five million euros. An average of at least ten employees and managers. And professional liability insurance with at least five hundred thousand euros of coverage, doubled. All four exceed twiceD, and on top of that the stack is product operations rather than application development. This line stands in the brief as a benchmark, not an opportunity.

And it's exactly the comparison between the two that's today's real insight. G-I-Z demands money and headcount. That's a hard number, but it's a purchasable one: in a bidding consortium, the larger partner brings the balance sheet, twiceD brings the development work, and formally the threshold is met. The Federal Highway Research Institute demands history. Nobody can bring that along — it only exists because someone has already done the work once. That leads to an uncomfortable order of operations: for a two-person shop, the path into public contracts doesn't run through the first solo bid, but through the first subcontract under someone who already holds the sector reference. The stack match isn't worthless there. It's exactly what you'd apply with to that someone.

The third tender is the City of Bamberg with an app for the Smart City project. The procurement code is the best match of the day, application software programming. And the only retrievable eligibility criterion contains no revenue, headcount, or reference threshold. Instead it requires registration in the list of the Association for Prequalification of Construction Companies, or alternatively a self-declaration or the Uniform European Self-Declaration. That's a construction clause in an app tender, so very likely a boilerplate paragraph a construction-oriented contracting office copied in. Two readings are possible: either there really is no technical hurdle, in which case this would be this track's first clear hit in weeks. Or the technical criteria sit in a part of the documents the interface doesn't surface. That's why there's deliberately no verdict here today, just a flag. This one notice is worth opening by hand.

All three share a clear limitation in their sourcing. The human-readable page of the European procurement platform responds to automated fetches with a CAPTCHA check. All of today's figures therefore come from the public search interface, and there mostly from the field with the eligibility criteria. That field gives the wording of the criteria, but not the scope of work and not the estimated contract value. So no value is retrievable for any of the three tenders, and the value column stays blank today rather than guessing a number. All three deadlines fall in mid-October, that's twenty and twenty-one business days of lead time. The most comfortable lead time this track has had in weeks.

On the market side there's a number that sounds like an all-clear and isn't one. Bitkom reported on September third: seventy-nine thousand I-T professionals are currently missing in Germany, down from one hundred nine thousand the prior year and one hundred forty-nine thousand in the year two thousand twenty-three. The shortage has nearly halved in three years. But the same report also says: ninety-seven percent of companies with open I-T positions have trouble filling them. Both are true at the same time. Picture a game of musical chairs: there are fewer chairs in the room than three years ago, but the ones that remain have an unusual shape, and not everyone fits them. Less demand overall, but the demand that's left has become more specialized. For a profile with thirteen years of experience and a narrow niche, that's the better half of the statistic.

The hourly rates confirm that by not moving. The average across all disciplines in two thousand twenty-six stands at one hundred one euros seventy, up from one hundred euros fifty-five the prior year. That's nominally one point one percent, so practically a standstill. I-T sits at one hundred one euros ninety-eight, almost exactly on the overall average, so no more I-T premium. A second platform survey puts it at one hundred three euros, along with the number that explains the flat rate: forty-nine percent report a worse pipeline than the prior year. The reliable range is therefore roughly one hundred two to one hundred three euros, not a single value. For context: the six listings on the board that print an hourly rate at all sit at a median of eighty-two euros fifty.

On the supply side, S-A-P remains the opposite pole. The company imposed a broad hiring freeze in August according to an internal memo, with only core roles in artificial intelligence explicitly exempted. Before that, roughly ten thousand positions had been cut, three thousand five hundred of them in Germany. Worldwide the company still employs about one hundred ten thousand six hundred people, roughly twenty-five thousand of them in Germany. Same pattern as with Bitkom: the door is narrower, but it stands open exactly where the need is specialized.
