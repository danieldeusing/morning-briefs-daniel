Three hourly rates, three ways of measuring [1][2][3]

This board's rate column is 82% estimate: 113 of 137 bands are researched, only 8 listings print a number at all, and 16 say nothing.[1] That's known. What's new is that today, for the first time, the estimate can be checked against a survey that names exactly Daniel's discipline — and it doesn't look good.

The 2026 Freelancer Compass breaks out four IT disciplines: SAP/ERP €120, Data & Analytics €100, IT infrastructure €95, software & web development €90 — all medians, €95/h across all disciplines.[2] Daniel's bread-and-butter discipline is thus the worst-paid of the four. Set against that are the board's 89 euro hourly bands (midpoint median €102.50/h, dominant €90–115/h* in 59 cases) and the six listings with a printed rate: 95 · 87 · 85 · 80 · 80 · 75 €/h, median €82.50/h.

Median vs. average: Picture ten people in a room: nine earn €80 an hour, one earns €300. The average is €102 — even though no one in the room earns €102. The median is the middle value, here €80: five earn more, five earn less. For rates, the median is almost always the more honest number, because a handful of top rates pull the average upward. The Compass says so itself — it works with the median, "because individual top fees pull the mean strongly upward or distort it."[2] Practical consequence: an average of around €102 and a median of €95 aren't a contradiction between two sources — they're two views of the same skewed distribution. Anyone who uses the average as a negotiating anchor is arguing with the top earners' number.

The order is the real finding — and it comes out against this brief's own numbers: €82.50 printed < €90 discipline median < €102.50 researched. The starred bands, then, aren't a neutral picture of the market but the upper half of the plausible range. That isn't necessarily wrong — the bands apply to senior remote roles, while the Compass median mixes every seniority and contract form. But it implies two things. First: an offer at €85–90/h isn't "below value" in this discipline — it's the market — and 6 of the 8 listings with a printed number sit exactly there. Second, and pricier: anyone who wants €110 has to justify it with something other than Java and Angular.

The board itself backs this up. The entries with a band above €100 are, almost without exception, not generic fullstack positions but niches: automotive and IoT carry €100–130/h* — the fit-85 automotive framework, the fit-81 IoT contract with gRPC/Protocol Buffers, the AI-developer mandate with AWS Bedrock and RAG. The dominant band €90–115/h*, by contrast, sticks to the interchangeable line "Java, Spring Boot, Angular, 100% remote," which appears 56 times on the board.[1] In a market where only one in four has made their rate increase stick and 70% expect a difficult year[3], differentiation is the only pricing argument left.

The five additions show the day's spread. TI briefly explained: KIM is German healthcare's secured email system, VSDM matches insured-persons' master data, ePA is the electronic patient record — a second fit-80 with the same theme is already on the board; healthcare TI is thus the second identifiable niche after automotive/IoT. Then fit 68 (Java/JEE/Quarkus, banking environment Frankfurt, 27 months, remote — no Angular, hence the gap), fit 55 (GitLab AI Engineer, remote worldwide, but more AI-ops than core development), fit 49 (Zürcher Kantonalbank, see above), and at the bottom fit 40: Java Swing maintenance in Hamburg, 60% remote, via employee leasing.

Employee leasing (Arbeitnehmerüberlassung, ANÜ): In a work or service contract, twiceD sells a result — the company delivers however it likes and is liable for it. Under employee leasing, by contrast, a person is loaned out: you work at the client's premises, under the client's direction and on the client's team, except the money runs through a leasing agency. Like a temp-staffing model for office jobs. Practical consequence for this profile: that fits poorly with a GmbH managing director based abroad — employee leasing presumes presence and being bound by instructions, and that's precisely not the model in which twiceD operates as a contractor. That's why this addition sits at the bottom with fit 40 despite "Java" on the label: here, 60% remote mainly means 40% is meant to be Hamburg.

A quick detour on the math, because it carries the whole point. Picture ten people in a room: nine earn eighty euros an hour, one earns three hundred. The average then works out to a hundred two euros, even though nobody in the room earns a hundred two euros. The median is the middle value, here eighty. That's exactly why the Compass explicitly works with the median, and it says why: because a handful of top fees pull the average up and distort it. So the often-quoted average of around a hundred two euros from the second survey isn't a counterargument — it's the same skewed distribution seen from the other side. That second survey, thirty-three hundred respondents surveyed in January and February, also names two more numbers: only twenty-five percent have raised their rate in the past year, and roughly seventy percent expect a difficult pipeline this year.
