The 21% AI share measures its own filter [1]

29 of 138 entries carry the AI Integration tag today. That's 21%, and as a headline it sounds like a market that has tipped. The breakdown says something else: 10 of the 116 freelance mandates are AI-tagged (8.6%), 10 of the 13 employee entries (77%), and all nine Intriguing entries.

The two high shares are self-made. The intake rule for the employee track explicitly names AI as a preferred topic, and the Intriguing track exists mainly for AI-harness builders. That 77% and 100% come out is not an observation about the labor market — it's the filter reporting back on itself. Only the freelance share carries any signal about actual supply — just under nine percent.

Even that nine percent rarely means "an AI project"; it's almost always an ordinary Java or Python mandate with an AI component bolted on. The top-rated one is a senior AI developer mandate via Allegis/TEKsystems for an investment bank (Frankfurt, 80% remote, fit 79, €100–130/h*): wanted is Java with Spring Boot WebFlux, Angular, PostgreSQL — and AWS Bedrock plus RAG. At the other end sits an Angular mandate where "AI tools" appears only as a nice-to-have and still gets counted.

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation): Instead of letting the language model invent an answer from memory, the system first searches a document store for the relevant passages and hands them to the model as context. Like an exam-taker who doesn't have to answer from memory but is allowed to look things up in permitted materials first. For project work, that means: the work being asked for is data connectivity, indexing, access rights, and delivery — backend craftsmanship. That's why these mandates are Java positions rather than research positions, and why they're in scope for this profile at all.

The premium you'd expect isn't there. Of the ten AI-tagged freelance mandates, exactly two carry a band whose upper bound exceeds €120/h — the Bedrock/RAG mandate (€100–130/h*) and an MLOps/AI-platform mandate in Bonn (€95–125/h*). And the only two AI mandates that print a figure at all instead of having it researched print €75/h and €85/h — both below the board's median band.[1]

Reading On the freelance side of this market, "AI" is currently a keyword, not a pricing argument. Anyone who takes the 21% as evidence of a trend is measuring this brief's intake rules. Anyone who takes the 8.6% is measuring a supply that's smaller than the Kubernetes share (26 entries) and only barely bigger than the Python share (22). The number that matters for a rate negotiation is the second one.

The board's top entry sits in a shrinking industry [1][4]

The same entry has held the top spot for weeks: Senior Java Developer (Automotive) via Frank Recruitment Group — Java and Spring Boot, AWS with Lambda and CodePipeline, React, MongoDB Atlas, Germany, 100% remote with one onsite day a month, twelve months, €100–130/h*, fit 85. The point breakdown is unusually clean: stack 38, location 19, rate 12, intrigue 6, track 10. This row carries the board — and it's a carry-over, last re-confirmed live on 09/19, not reopened today.[1]

The industry backdrop has been a different one for two and a half weeks now. On September 3, 2026, Volkswagen's supervisory board unanimously approved the "Future Plan 2030": roughly 50,000 additional jobs worldwide by the end of the decade, spanning VW, Audi, Porsche, and CARIAD, with restructuring costs of €16 billion. Around 37,000 termination agreements are already signed, overwhelmingly through voluntary programs and partial retirement. The four long-debated German plants — Emden, Zwickau, Hanover, Neckarsulm — survived the vote, but only contingent on a viable European production strategy due by June 2027.[4]

Termination agreement and partial retirement: A termination agreement isn't a dismissal — it's an exit both sides sign, where the employer pays severance and the employee gives up protection against unfair dismissal. Partial retirement is the variant for the final working years: on paper you work half-time, in practice often full-time first and then not at all, at a reduced, state-topped-up salary until retirement. Why this matters for reading the market: 37,000 signed terminations mean a large share of the cuts is already agreed, not still to be negotiated. The cuts are further along than the headline suggests — and they're hitting manufacturing and administration, not external software procurement.

The two fit together, and that's the uncomfortable part. A company cutting its core workforce through severance isn't building software capacity — it's buying it in, project by project, externally, cancelable. That's exactly what this mandate looks like: twelve months, one onsite day a month, a staffing agency in between. The automotive premium is real, but it's not a scarcity premium — it's the price of flexibility on the buyer's side. The counterweight belongs here too: €16 billion in restructuring spend is money not going into new initiatives, and a plant decision that won't land until mid-2027 isn't an environment for multi-year commitments.

And then there's the breadth of the thesis: four of 138 entries carry the automotive tag — 2.9%. Alongside the Frank Recruitment mandate (85), those are a Java/Spring Boot mandate for rail passenger-information systems via teamative with a 60-month term (78), an embedded-and-Java mandate via Optimus Search (73), and an e-mobility backend via Computer Futures (70).[1]

Self-critique The teamative entry gets 14 of 15 rate points for an automotive premium band — and its rate field is empty. The posting names no figure, and this board doesn't print a researched one either. So the points stand for a price assumption that appears nowhere in the entry. Same pattern on a smaller scale: of the four automotive rows, two carry a band above €120/h, both researched, none printed. This board's automotive-premium thesis is, so far, an assumption of this board's, not an observation from postings.
