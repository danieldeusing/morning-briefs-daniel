103 Instead of 104: the First Decline Is One Euro [2][3][4][5]

freelancermap's press release on the Freelancer-Kompass 2026 headlines that hourly rates fell "for the first time."[4] That's true — the DACH-wide average sits at 103 €/h, down from 104 €/h the prior year.[2] One euro is barely one percent. As a price signal, that's noise. It matters for a different reason: until now the figure rose every year, and now it doesn't.

Average vs. median — why 103 and 95 are both correct: The average (103 €) is all rates added up and divided by the number of people. The median (95 € for IT) is the rate of the person exactly in the middle once everyone lines up by size. If a few SAP consultants at 150 € walk into the room, the average jumps; the median barely moves. For your own pricing question, the median is the more honest number — and for software and web development it sits at 90 €/h, the lower end of all IT fields.[3]

The number that actually matters is a different one: −17 % project revenue, which freelancermap calls "the steepest income decline since the survey began."[3] If the price falls one percent and revenue falls seventeen, the remaining sixteen points come from fewer billable hours sold. The consultancy Hilker Consulting reads the study this way: 43 % of freelancers have no secured pipeline, and for half of them it's worse than the prior year. Hilker calls this "not a temporary dip," but rather "a structural shift."[5] That's an interpretation, not a study finding. The 43 % and 50 % figures weren't found on freelancermap's own pages today.

What that means for the board: the researched bands in the board have a median of 100 €/h. The Kompass median for software development sits at 90 €/h. The Kompass measures all experience levels, the board looks for senior roles. A premium is plausible, then, but the *-bands are closer to the ceiling of what's realistic in a negotiation than to the middle. The only printed euro rates today — 95 €/h (SOMI) and 87 €/h (Randstad) — both sit below it.

My read: in a market where the price holds and utilization is breaking, contract length matters more than the rate. A nine-month mandate at 100 € beats three six-week engagements at 115 € with gaps in between. A discount doesn't fix that, it just further lowers the one lever that's still holding. I'd rather negotiate over extension options than over five euros.

Bitkom: the IT Gap Has Nearly Halved [6]

According to Bitkom, Germany is currently short 79,000 IT specialists. In 2025 it was 109,000, in 2023 still 149,000.[6] The release is dated 2026-09-03 and is based on two surveys of 853 and 602 companies respectively (calendar weeks 18–25/2026). It's a month old, but it belongs here because it explains the Kompass numbers from the demand side.

Skills gap: The number of IT positions companies want to fill but can't. Like a game of musical chairs, only reversed: there are more chairs than players, and the gap counts the empty chairs. Fewer empty chairs means companies have to fight less for people, and outside freelancers become dispensable more often.

Bitkom itself gives no reason for the decline in the release. But two findings from the same survey argue against the convenient explanation "AI is replacing developers": 65 % of companies expect no job cuts from AI, and 76 % expect the shortage to worsen again.[6] So the gap is shrinking in a weak economy, but companies aren't betting on that being permanent. For a freelancer, that means: bargaining power is lower right now, but not gone for good. Locking in long mandates now is cheaper than waiting for a quick turnaround.

It's Friday, October second, and this is the first Jobs edition since September twenty-second. The week's headline is that freelance hourly rates are falling for the first time. That's true, but the drop is exactly one euro.

According to freelancermap's Freelancer Kompass survey, the average hourly rate across German-speaking Europe is now a hundred three euros, down from a hundred four last year. That's barely one percent, more noise than signal. IT freelancers charge a median of ninety-five euros an hour. Software and web development sits at ninety euros, the lowest of any IT specialty.

The number that actually matters is a seventeen percent drop in project revenue, the steepest income decline since the survey began. If the price falls one percent and revenue falls seventeen, billable hours are the real story. Rates are holding, workloads aren't. That's why how long a contract runs matters more right now than squeezing out a few extra euros an hour.

The trade group Bitkom tells the other side of the story. Germany is currently short seventy-nine thousand IT workers. Three years ago that number was a hundred forty-nine thousand, so the gap has nearly cut in half. Companies don't have to fight as hard for talent. But seventy-six percent of the companies surveyed still expect the shortage to get worse again, and two-thirds don't think artificial intelligence will cost jobs. So freelancers have less leverage right now, but it isn't gone for good.

The job board matches that picture. It lists thirty-one openings, but only one scores above seventy on our fit scale. Twenty-one listings vanished since the last edition, eleven of them among the strongest. The twelve that stayed carry the exact same score as ten days ago, so this drop is real, not a scoring glitch. Good mandates disappeared, and somewhat weaker ones took their place.

The top listing is a Java and Angular contract from One Day Ahead, fully remote. But its own web address lists a start date back on June first. A fresher listing from the same company, also Angular, starts October twelfth. The practical move: email One Day Ahead about that fresh posting, and ask in the same breath whether the Java mandate is still open.

In the smart-home and IoT niche, three listings stand out: a cloud and MQTT project in Hamburg, a KNX programming contract, and a building-automation consulting role. Only the building-automation role clears the niche target rate of over a hundred ten euros an hour, but it runs through a staffing agency rather than a direct contract — a structure that doesn't fit well with a Brazil-based GmbH. Worth asking upfront whether a services contract is possible instead.

One notable trend: artificial intelligence is now showing up in ordinary Java job postings. Two Spring Boot mandates name Amazon's coding assistant as a daily tool. One anonymous listing explicitly wants someone who can orchestrate Claude Code-style agents — exactly Daniel's day-to-day work, though it's a permanent position paying seventy-five to eighty-five thousand euros a year, below his own threshold. No permanent role today clears every one of the four required criteria.
