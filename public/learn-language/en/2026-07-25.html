Vocabulary Feira Livre: feilschen, ernten, o hortifrúti

Saturday, July 25 — weekly theme Food, Cooking, Restaurant, and specifically where the food begins: at the open-air market. Not street food (that was July 18), not the home kitchen (July 4), but the feira livre on Saturday morning: haggling, weighing, paying, and above all judging whether the produce is ripe or already past it. One word carries the whole day: a feira. It means both the produce market and the trade fair — today's family brief sends the family to the Feira de Arte Impressa in Londrina, and that's lexically the exact same word as the mango stall. 11 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, plus 3 refreshers (good value / affordable from June 27, Pix from June 29, the kitchen scale from July 4). Plus today's most instructive find: a Brazilian everyday verb that, in exactly this meaning, doesn't appear in the Portuguese dictionary at all.

  • FALSE FRIEND a receita ≠ EN receipt — receita is the cooking recipe, the doctor's prescription, and revenue in the accounting sense (which is why Brazil's tax authority is called Receita Federal, literally "Federal Revenue"). The store receipt, by contrast, is o recibo. Ask for "a receita" in a shop and you'll get a recipe at best — the same trap runs through Spanish via la receta ↔ el recibo.
  • BR-PT pechinchar — the word the dictionary forgot — in Brazil, to haggle is pechinchar. The Priberam entry for pechinchar (Portuguese lexicography) doesn't list this meaning at all, only "to win unexpectedly," "to profit," "to get unforeseen advantages." Backup comes from the same publisher elsewhere: under barganhar, meaning 3 reads "Pedir para baixar o preço," with the synonyms PECHINCHAR, REGATEAR. priberam.org · pechinchar
  • BR-PT o hortifrúti — a portmanteau of horticultura + fruticultura that German can only translate with a tapeworm compound (Obst- und Gemüsestand). Priberam: „Cada um dos produtos provenientes da horticultura, da fruticultura ou das granjas, isto é, frutas, hortaliças e legumes." priberam.org
  • BR-PT el tianguis (ES-MX) ↔ a feira livre (PT-BR) — Mexico has a Nahuatl word for the same pop-up street market: tianguis, from tianquiztli. In northern Mexico it's also called mercado sobre ruedas, "market on wheels." Two countries, two words, one Saturday morning. hispanopedia.com
  • CONJUGATION eu amadureço, que ela amadureça — amadurecer (to ripen) writes a ç before o and a, otherwise the soft /s/ would turn into a hard /k/. Same family as conhecer → conheço. And in Spanish, regatear does something similar with vowels: yo regateé in the preterite and que yo regatee in the subjunctive — both with a double e.
  • GENDER a colher (spoon) vs. colher (to harvest) — same spelling, different part of speech, different pronunciation: the noun a colher has an open e, the verb colher a closed one. A homograph with a different sound — in Portuguese, homógrafo heterofônico. At the market you colhe; at the table you use a colher.
  • IDIOM trocar seis por meia dúzia — literally "trading six for half a dozen," meaning: makes no difference. English has the exact same image (six of one, half a dozen of the other), German a completely different one: gehupft wie gesprungen ("hopped like jumped"). Origin per Dicionário inFormal: old-time trade, where different units of measure denoted the same quantity. dicionarioinformal.com.br

The Three Verbs of Market Morning

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br, and conjugator.reverso.net

Why eu amadureço gets a ç: Portuguese spells sounds, not letter sequences. A c sounds soft like /s/ before e and i (amadurece), but hard like /k/ before o and a. To keep that soft /s/ before o and a too, it gets a hook: ç. Hence eu amadureço and que ela amadureça. Think of a traffic light that changes color the moment traffic starts coming from a different direction — the letter adapts so the sound stays the same. The same mechanism is behind eu busquei (c→qu) and eu paguei (g→gu), just running the other way. Homograph: two words spelled the same but meaning different things. Some also sound alike (German die Bank, which means both "bench" and "bank"), others don't — and it's the latter Brazilians mean when they say homógrafo heterofônico: same spelling, different pronunciation. A colher (the spoon) and colher (to harvest) are one such pair. Perfect tense with sein: German needs an auxiliary verb for the past, and the choice isn't free. Whoever moves or changes state takes sein — a fruit that ripens is a different fruit afterward, so die Papaya ist gereift. Whoever, by contrast, does something to an object takes haben: ich habe geerntet, er hat gefeilscht. Portuguese and Spanish don't have this fork at all; there everything runs through ter or haber, respectively.

Dictionary Warning pechinchar is Brazilian everyday usage, but not a safe dictionary hit. The Priberam entry — Portuguese lexicography — lists three meanings for pechinchar, and none of them is "to haggle": „1. Ganhar inesperadamente ou imerecidamente (uso popular). 2. Lucrar. 3. Receber vantagens imprevistas." Look the verb up in Portugal, then, and you land on unexpected gain, not bargaining over price. Two practical consequences here: first, in Brazil pechinchar is still the word for what you do at the feira on Saturdays. Second, if you want to stay dictionary-safe, say barganhar — Priberam's own entry for it lists as meaning 3 „Pedir para baixar o preço" and names, of all things, PECHINCHAR and REGATEAR as synonyms there. Just watch out for barganhar's meaning 1, „Trocar, vender com dolo" — trading with intent to deceive; when in doubt, negociar o preço stays the most neutral phrasing. priberam.org/pechinchar · priberam.org/barganhar

One Word, Two Words colher is a homograph with two pronunciations. As a noun, a colher, it's the spoon — feminine, with an open e. As a verb it means "to harvest, to pick" — with a closed e. The spelling makes no distinction at all, the mouth makes all of it; Brazilian grammars call this a homógrafo heterofônico. In practice that means context carries the whole load. Vou colher ("I'm going to harvest") and vou pegar a colher ("I'm going to get the spoon") are only one little word apart. Incidentally, colher is, per conjugacao.com.br, a clean regular -er verb — the irregularity lives in the pronunciation, not the conjugation. conjugacao.com.br

One Word for Two Worlds Feira is the produce market and the trade fair, both at once. The mango stall on Saturday morning is a feira — and the Feira de Arte Impressa that today's family brief sends the family to in Londrina is lexically the exact same word. German cleanly splits this into market and fair; Portuguese keeps both under one roof and tacks on an adjective as needed: feira livre (the open-air street market), feira de arte, feira do livro. Mexico has its own word for the street market, from Nahuatl: el tianguis, from tianquiztli — described as „un mercadillo público mexicano ambulante (o no fijo) que se instala en las calles de una ciudad." In northern Mexico the same traveling market is also called mercado sobre ruedas. hispanopedia.com

the produce stall, the greengrocer's

o hortifrúti

Portmanteau Hortifrúti squeezes two branches of agriculture into one word. Horti- comes from horticultura (vegetable growing), -frúti from fruticultura (fruit growing). Priberam defines it as „nome masculino — Cada um dos produtos provenientes da horticultura, da fruticultura ou das granjas, isto é, frutas, hortaliças e legumes." — that is, the goods themselves. In Brazilian everyday life it has also come to mean the place: the sign over the shop door just says HORTIFRÚTI, and people say vou no hortifrúti. German can only manage this with a hyphenated construction, Spanish needs two shops side by side: frutería for fruit, verdulería for vegetables. priberam.org

Refresher from July 4 German builds a brand-new word for every kind of scale; Portuguese doesn't. Küchenwaage, Marktwaage, Personenwaage, Briefwaage — four compounds. Portuguese has a balança and tacks on a de cozinha when needed; in Spanish, la báscula covers the larger devices. That's exactly why German words look so long to Brazilians: the compounding does the work that other languages leave to a preposition.

Refresher from June 29 Two payment words that make the difference at the feira: dinheiro vivo is cash — literally "living money" — and o troco is the change. The standard line at the stall is „não tem troco?" ("don't you have exact change?"), and whoever's only carrying a 100-real bill will hear it, guaranteed. That's why the QR code is now taped to so many crates: Pix solves the small-change problem, not the price problem — pechinchar is still in charge of that.

el recibo

False Friend a receita looks like receipt, but it's never the receipt. Receita covers three meanings: the cooking recipe, the doctor's prescription, and revenue in the accounting sense — which is why Brazil's tax authority is called Receita Federal, literally "Federal Revenue." The scrap of paper you get after paying, by contrast, is o recibo (in shops usually printed as nota fiscal). Spanish builds the exact same trap: la receta is the recipe, el recibo the receipt. Memory hook for the market: a receita tells you what to cook; o recibo proves you paid.

Herb Pitfall Cilantro and parsley are called different things in Brazil and Mexico. Brazilian: o coentro (cilantro) and a salsinha (flat-leaf parsley) — the market vendors sell the mix of both as cheiro-verde. Mexican: el cilantro and el perejil. Ask for coentro in Mexico and you'll get a puzzled look; say cilantro in Brazil and you'll be understood, but it'll sound translated. And the example sentence also happens to contain this morning's verb: colhido is the participle of colher.

Refresher from June 27 German distinguishes günstig (good value) from billig (cheap); Portuguese doesn't automatically. Günstig praises the ratio between price and goods; billig insults the goods. Barato covers both — you have to add the positive spin yourself, and the idiomatic tool for that is em conta: „um preço em conta" is a fair, workable price, never a bargain-bin price. In Mexican Spanish, accesible takes on that role, while corriente carries the dismissive side.

Comprar na primeira banca ou na terceira é trocar seis por meia dúzia.

Dois reais de desconto e meia hora de fila: é trocar seis por meia dúzia.

da lo mismo

Comprar en el primer puesto o en el tercero da lo mismo.

Dos reales de descuento y media hora de fila: da lo mismo.

Origin Portuguese and English independently arrived at the same image. The Dicionário inFormal explains the Brazilian idiom this way: „No Brasil a expressão significa ficar tudo na mesma… A origem remonta ao comércio antigo, quando as medidas eram utilizadas para quantificar produtos. Trocar seis unidades de uma medida por meia dúzia de outra… não fazia diferença no resultado final." That exact same image sits inside the English six of one, half a dozen of the other — two trading languages, one arithmetic joke. German takes a completely different route and compares two motions instead of two numbers: gehupft wie gesprungen ("hopped like jumped"). It's Saturday, July twenty-fifth. This week's theme is food, cooking, and restaurants, and today we're heading to where food actually starts: the open-air market. Street food had its turn a week ago, and the home kitchen got its moment earlier in the month. Today belongs to the feira. Eleven entries in all, three of them verbs with full conjugation, plus three refreshers.

One word carries the whole day: a feira. It means both the produce market and the trade fair. The mango stall on Saturday morning is a feira, and the Feira de Arte Impressa in Londrina, which today's family brief sends everyone off to, is lexically the exact same word. German splits this cleanly into market and fair. Portuguese keeps both under one roof and adds an adjective when it needs to: feira livre, feira de arte, feira do livro. In Mexico, the same pop-up street market is called a tianguis, a word from Nahuatl, from tianquiztli. In northern Mexico people also call it mercado sobre ruedas, market on wheels.

Today's most instructive find is a verb the dictionary forgot. In Brazil, to haggle is pechinchar — that's the everyday word. The Priberam entry for pechinchar, Portuguese lexicography, doesn't recognize that meaning at all. It lists: to win unexpectedly, to profit, to get unforeseen advantages. Not a word about bargaining over price.

Backup turns up in the same dictionary, in a different entry. Under barganhar, meaning three reads, literally, "ask for the price to come down." And that very entry lists pechinchar and regatear as synonyms. So if you want to stay dictionary-safe, say barganhar. Just watch out for its first meaning, which is trading or selling in bad faith. When in doubt, the most neutral phrase is simply negociar o preço, negotiating the price.

Today's three verbs are to haggle, to harvest, and to ripen. Haggling takes haben: ich habe gefeilscht. And notice the form du feilschst, where the sch and the st really do sit side by side. Ripening, on the other hand, takes sein: die Papaya ist gereift, because afterward the fruit really is a different fruit. Verbformen dot de also lists a haben variant for the transitive reading: die Sonne hat die Früchte gereift.

Portuguese has two spelling rules at work today. Amadurecer, to ripen, gets a cedilla under the c before o and a: eu amadureço, que ela amadureça, que nós amadureçamos. The reason is pure sound logic — a c is soft before e and i, hard before o and a, and the cedilla protects the soft sound. Spanish does something related with vowels in regatear: yo regateé in the preterite and que yo regatee in the subjunctive, both with a doubled e.

The nicest detail hides inside the verb colher. As a noun, a colher, it's the spoon, feminine, with an open e. As a verb it means to harvest, with a closed e. Same spelling, different pronunciation, different part of speech. Brazilian grammars call this a homógrafo heterofônico. At the market you colher; at the table you use a colher.

Today's false friend is a receita. It looks like the English word receipt, but it's never the receipt. Receita covers three things: the cooking recipe, the doctor's prescription, and revenue in the accounting sense. That's why Brazil's tax authority is called Receita Federal, literally Federal Revenue. The slip you get after paying is o recibo. In Spanish the same trap runs through la receta and el recibo.

Two Brazilian specialties round things out. O hortifrúti squeezes horticultura and fruticultura into one word; Priberam defines it as the goods themselves — fruit, vegetables, and greens — and in everyday speech it's also become the name for the shop. And today's idiom: trocar seis por meia dúzia, to trade six for half a dozen, meaning it makes no difference. English has the exact same image, six of one, half a dozen of the other. German picks a completely different one: gehupft wie gesprungen, hopped like jumped.
