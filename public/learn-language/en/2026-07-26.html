Vocab: Candidate Selection — nominieren, a chapa, o vice

Sunday, July 26 — rotation theme Politics, Law & Health, and this time it's the calendar that's writing the vocabulary. Brazil is right in the middle of its convenções partidárias window: on Saturday, the Partido Liberal declared Flávio Bolsonaro its presidential candidate — no running mate yet, no backing from the centrist parties — and today the PSD in São Paulo is nominating the former governor of Goiás, Ronaldo Caiado. Voting takes place, per the TSE, on October 4, with the runoff set for October 25. On July 12 this brief spelled out the election itself; today it steps back one level: how a candidate even gets on the ballot in the first place. 11 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, plus 3 refreshers from July 12. Throughline: German puts a candidate up, Portuguese and Spanish throw him — and the idiom at the end even throws a hat.

  • FALSE FRIEND Parteitag ≠ convention — convenção (PT) and convención (ES) mean, for a party convention, exactly what they say. German's die Konvention can't do that: Duden lists three meanings — „1. Abkommen, [völkerrechtlicher] Vertrag … 2. Regel des Umgangs, des sozialen Verhaltens … 3. Regel (beim Fechten mit Florett oder Säbel)". No party assembly in sight. The word is der Parteitag. duden.de
  • BR-PT a chapa — in Brazil, president and vice president don't run side by side; they run as one single thing. The Código Eleitoral: „O registro de candidatos a presidente e vice-presidente … far-se-á sempre em chapa única e indivisível." German needs a whole subordinate clause for that, English says ticket, Latin American Spanish la fórmula. politize.com.br
  • CONJUGATION eu indiquei · eu lancei · yo lancé — three spellings, one job: rescue the sound when e shows up. indicar → indiquei (c→qu), lançar → lancei (ç→c), lanzar → lancé (z→c). The mirror image of yesterday, where amadurecer picked up a cedilla before o.
  • CONJUGATION eu me alio, but yo me alío — the same verb, a different spelling rule. Portuguese already stresses it correctly on its own; Spanish would read a-LIO and has to break the diphthong with an accent mark: a-LÍ-o. In the nosotros form it disappears again: nos aliamos. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net
  • PHRASE Wahlgang · turno · vuelta — three images for the same event: German walks to the vote, Brazil works a shift, Mexico turns a lap. Dates per the TSE: 1º turno on October 4, 2026, 2º turno on October 25, 2026. tse.jus.br
  • PHRASE running mate — no single shared word — German makes do with der Vizekandidat, Brazil shortens it to o vice, Latin American election coverage says el compañero de fórmula. Flávio Bolsonaro doesn't have one yet. clickorlando.com
  • IDIOM „seinen Hut in den Ring werfen" — the German idiom is itself already a translation, a loan translation from English (to throw one's hat in the ring, popularized by Theodore Roosevelt in 1912). Portuguese and Spanish skip the hat and simply say entrar na disputa and lanzarse a la contienda. deutschlernerblog.de

The Three Verbs of Candidate Selection

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br, and conjugator.reverso.net

Why lançar becomes lancei: Portuguese and Spanish spell sounds, not letter sequences. A c sounds hard, like /k/, before a, o, u, and soft, like /s/, before e, i. The ç is the emergency exit for a soft /s/ before a, o, u — exactly what happened yesterday (eu amadureço). Today it runs the other way: before an e the soft /s/ is guaranteed anyway, so the cedilla can go — lançar → eu lancei. Conversely, indicar needs its hard /k/ before e too and inserts a u (eu indiquei), and Spanish lanzar swaps the z for a c (yo lancé). Think of a plug adapter: the appliance stays the same, only the socket changes. Why -ieren verbs don't get a ge-: The German participle is normally built with ge- (gestellt, gemacht). Verbs ending in -ieren are loanwords and are stressed on the last syllable — there's no room for an unstressed ge- in front of that. That's why it's hat nominiert, not *hat genominiert, and likewise hat studiert and hat kandidiert. No exceptions. Reflexive: Some verbs need a little word that points back at the person doing the action — sich verbünden, not just verbünden. All three languages inflect this little word along with the verb (ich verbünde mich, eu me alio, yo me alío). In everyday Brazilian usage it almost always sits before the verb — eu me alio, not alio-me: the most noticeable difference from European Portuguese.

Word Choice In Brazilian political usage, "to nominate" is almost always indicar. Priberam lists, under indicar, meaning 4, exactly this usage: „Designar para cargo ou função", with the example „o conselho de administração vai indicar o próximo director financeiro"; meaning 3 is the softer recommendation, „Fazer a recomendação ou a prescrição de". Indicar covers both, then — the formal appointment and the friendly tip ("can you indicar me a lawyer?") — replacing the German trio nominieren / benennen / empfehlen with a single verb. Spanish nominar is narrower and more formal; in everyday Mexican Spanish you also hear postular. priberam.org/indicar

Two Images, One Action German stands the candidate up, Portuguese and Spanish throw him. verbformen.de glosses aufstellen as „Etwas aufrichten; etwas an einen Platz stellen" — a monument, a tent, a candidate: everything gets stood upright. Lançar comes from the opposite direction; Priberam's first meaning is „Atirar com força; arrojar", that is, to throw with force. When someone in Brazil lança a candidacy, they're hurling a name into the arena — the same image as the hat in the ring, and Spanish's lanzarse a la contienda. One caution about the dictionary: Priberam lists eleven meanings for lançar (to throw, to emit, to post an entry, to bid), and the candidacy reading isn't its own separate entry — it's the metaphor, fixed in Brazilian press usage, built on meaning 1. verbformen.de · priberam.org/lançar

One Accent Mark of Difference eu me alio with no accent mark, yo me alío with one. Both languages stress the form the same way, on the i: a-LI-o. Only the spelling rules differ. Portuguese already stresses a word ending in -o on the second-to-last syllable anyway, so it needs no mark; conjugacao.com.br simply lists eu alio. Spanish would read io as a diphthong and stress a-LIO. To break the diphthong, an accent mark lands on the i: alío, alías, alía, alían. In the nosotros form the stress already falls in the right place on its own, so it disappears: nos aliamos. Same mechanism as enviar → yo envío. Anyone deriving the Spanish form from the Portuguese one is guaranteed to spell it wrong. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net

Party Conventions, the Chapa, and the Missing Running Mate

4 entries · today's false friend, a Brazilian legal term, the missing German word, and the two election dates

Noun FALSE FRIEND DE m, pl. -e · PT-BR a convenção (partidária), f · ES-MX la convención, f
der Parteitag

Der Parteitag der Liberalen fand am Samstag in São Paulo statt.

Auf einem Parteitag wird abgestimmt, nicht mehr diskutiert — diskutiert wurde vorher.

the party convention

The Liberal Party's convention took place in São Paulo on Saturday.

At a convention people vote; they don't debate any more — the debating happened earlier.

a convenção (partidária)

A convenção do Partido Liberal aconteceu no sábado, em São Paulo.

Na convenção se vota, não se discute mais — a discussão foi antes.

la convención (del partido)

La convención del Partido Liberal se realizó el sábado en São Paulo.

En la convención se vota, ya no se discute — la discusión fue antes.

False Friend German die Konvention is never a party assembly. Duden lists exactly three meanings: „1. Abkommen, [völkerrechtlicher] Vertrag … 2. Regel des Umgangs, des sozialen Verhaltens … 3. Regel (beim Fechten mit Florett oder Säbel)". A treaty, a rule of etiquette, a fencing rule — no congress, no coronation. Write "die Konvention der Partei" and you've produced a sentence no native speaker understands. The right word is der Parteitag, and the -tag in it has nothing to do with the day of the week: it belongs to the verb tagen, "to hold a session" — the same formation as in Bundestag and Landtag. The trap runs the other way too: uma convenção can very well be the international treaty (a Convenção de Genebra) — just as an addition to the party convention, not instead of it. duden.de/Konvention
Noun BR-PT Brazilian legal term with no one-word German equivalent · PT-BR a chapa, f · ES-MX la fórmula, f · EN ticket
die Chapa (das Kandidaten-Duo)

Präsident und Vize treten immer als eine Chapa an — getrennt wählen kann man sie nicht.

Seine Chapa ist noch nicht komplett: der Vize fehlt.

the ticket (presidential ticket)

President and vice-president always run as one ticket — you can't split your vote.

His ticket isn't complete yet: the running mate is missing.

a chapa

Presidente e vice concorrem sempre em uma chapa — não dá para votar separado.

A chapa dele ainda não está completa: falta o vice.

la fórmula

Presidente y vicepresidente compiten siempre en una fórmula — no se pueden votar por separado.

Su fórmula todavía no está completa: falta el compañero.

A Word the Law Itself Invented In election law, chapa isn't a figure of speech — it's a requirement. The Código Eleitoral demands: „O registro de candidatos a presidente e vice-presidente, governador e vice-governador, ou prefeito e vice-prefeito, far-se-á sempre em chapa única e indivisível." In other words: registration always happens as a single, indivisible chapa. That's why nobody in Brazil can vote for one side's president and the other side's vice — the pair is, quite literally, a single piece, and that's exactly why Brazilian news outlets take the missing running mate so seriously. The word itself is remarkably broad; Priberam lists more than twenty meanings, including „Peça plana e relativamente delgada de qualquer material duro e resistente" (sheet metal), „Matriz, forma, cunho … que, por meio da impressão ou compressão, reproduz o seu desenho" (a printing plate), the license plate, the grill plate, in Brazilian slang „Conjunto de dentes postiços" (a set of dentures), and „Forma de tratamento amigável" — meu chapa, "my buddy." For the election, the printing plate is the easiest image to remember: a set of names, printed as one piece. politize.com.br · priberam.org/chapa

The News Angle & Word Choice The missing running mate is itself the news right now, not just the vocabulary. Flávio Bolsonaro was confirmed as the Partido Liberal's presidential candidate at its convention in São Paulo on July 25, 2026 — with two open flanks that the report spells out explicitly: „the younger Bolsonaro still seeks a running mate and does not enjoy the support of centrist parties as his father did". Linguistically, this office is a catch-all for makeshift solutions. German has no word of its own and builds one: Vizekandidat, or alternatively Kandidat für die Vizepräsidentschaft — both bureaucratic German, not everyday speech. Brazil shortens it radically to o vice. In Latin American Spanish, the role takes its name from the chapa itself: fórmula vicepresidencial, and the person is compañero de fórmula — documented here through current election coverage, not a dictionary entry; journalistic standard usage, not a legal term. clickorlando.com · infobae.com

Three Images, One Event The same event, three completely different metaphors. German thinks of the voter's movement: der Wahlgang is the walk to the ballot box. Portuguese thinks of a shift — o turno is, in everyday use, a work shift (turno da noite). Spanish thinks of a lap: la vuelta is the cycling lap, the return, the second time around. The TSE gives the dates as „As Eleições de 2026 ocorrerão no dia 4 de outubro de 2026 (1º turno)" and „Nos casos em que houver necessidade de 2º turno, a votação ocorrerá no dia 25 de outubro de 2026." Memory aid for German: the second round isn't called "zweiter Wahlgang" in the newspaper, it's die Stichwahl — a word that already came up on July 12. tse.jus.br

Refresher from July 12 · Bonus Word Portuguese has a word for "someone who's in the running to be a presidential candidate" — German doesn't. It's presidenciável, and Priberam defines it as „Que ou quem está apto para ser candidato a ou nomeado presidente", usable as an adjective and as a noun of either gender; the dictionary itself gives the example „todos os presidenciáveis aceitam participar no debate". Here's the fun part: presidenciável applies even before the candidacy — whoever it is gets talked about in the press but hasn't been through a convention yet. German needs a whole descriptive phrase for that ("touted as a possible presidential candidate"). Gender & Double Meaning Feminine in German, masculine in Portuguese and Spanish — and in Spanish the same word also means "the match." Die Partei is feminine in German; o partido and el partido are masculine — never *a partido. The real trap sits in Spanish: el partido is both the political party and the soccer match; el partido del domingo can be either one, and only context decides. Portuguese keeps them cleanly apart: the party is o partido, the match is a partida or o jogo. And German has a third relative lurking nearby: die Partie (a game of chess, a shipment of goods) sounds almost the same but is a different word entirely.

Refresher from July 12 German has one word; Brazilian Portuguese has two. Die Koalition covers both for us: the alliance you enter an election with, and the alliance you govern with afterward. Brazilian usage keeps two words side by side — a coligação for the electoral alliance formed to contest the vote, and a coalizão for the governing alliance that organizes majorities in Congress. The split is press usage, not a dictionary definition this brief has verified — but it's consistent enough that it jumps out the moment you notice it: convention weeks are full of coligação, government crises full of coalizão.

Origin The German idiom is itself a translation. It's a loan translation of the English to throw one's hat in the ring, and it comes from boxing: anyone who wanted to join the fight threw their hat into the ring. The phrase became politically famous in 1912 thanks to Theodore Roosevelt. Deutschlernerblog gives its meanings as „kandidieren; sich zur Wahl stellen; eine Herausforderung oder eine Auseinandersetzung annehmen". Portuguese and Spanish don't keep the hat. It's Sunday, July twenty-sixth. This week's rotation theme is politics, law, and health, and today it's the Brazilian calendar that's writing the vocabulary lesson. Brazil is right in the middle of its party-convention season — in Portuguese, the convenções partidárias. Eleven entries today, three of them verbs with full conjugation, plus three refreshers.

The story behind it, in two sentences. On Saturday, the Partido Liberal officially named Flávio Bolsonaro its presidential candidate, in São Paulo. The report on it flags two open problems right away: he still needs a running mate, and he doesn't have the backing from centrist parties that his father once had. Today, the PSD in São Paulo is nominating the former governor of Goiás, Ronaldo Caiado.

Today's false friend is convention. In Portuguese the party convention is a convenção, in Spanish a convención, and both mean exactly that. The German word Konvention can't. Duden gives three meanings: an international treaty, a rule of social conduct, and a rule in fencing with foil or saber. Nothing about a party gathering. The right word is der Parteitag, and the tag in it has nothing to do with the day of the week — it comes from the verb tagen, to hold a session, the same building block as in Bundestag.

Today's Brazilian word is a chapa. In Brazil, president and vice president don't run side by side — they run as one single thing. Brazil's electoral code requires that registration always happen, word for word, em chapa única e indivisível, as a single, indivisible chapa. German needs a whole clause for that, English says ticket, Latin American Spanish says la fórmula. And that's exactly why the missing running mate is real news: without him, the chapa is incomplete.

The word itself turns out to be surprisingly broad. Priberam lists more than twenty meanings: sheet metal, a printing plate, a license plate, a grill plate, in Brazilian slang even a set of dentures, and as a form of address, meu chapa means something like "my buddy." For the election, the printing plate is the easiest image to hold onto: a set of names, printed as one piece.

Today's three verbs are to nominate, to field, and to ally oneself. And they show off a nice contrast in imagery. German stands a candidate up, straight and vertical. Portuguese and Spanish throw him. Lançar and lanzar both start out meaning to throw with force — that's literally Priberam's first definition. When someone in Brazil lança a candidacy, they're hurling a name into the arena.

The spelling today runs in reverse from yesterday. Yesterday, amadurecer picked up a cedilla before o — eu amadureço. Today the cedilla drops: lançar becomes eu lancei, no cedilla at all. Indicar needs its hard k before e too, so it inserts a u — eu indiquei. And Spanish lanzar swaps the z for a c — yo lancé. Three spellings, one job: protect the sound.

The finest distinction sits in the third verb. Portuguese writes eu me alio with no accent mark; Spanish writes yo me alío with one, on the i. Both are stressed the same way, on the i. It's just that Spanish would read the i-o as one syllable, a diphthong, and stress it wrong, so it needs the mark. In the nosotros form it disappears again — nos aliamos. Derive the Spanish form from the Portuguese one, and you're guaranteed to get it wrong.

Three more notes on the calendar and on gender. The round of voting is o turno in Brazil — a shift — and la vuelta in Mexico — a lap. The TSE lists October fourth for the first round and October twenty-fifth for the runoff. And watch the gender: die Partei is feminine in German, masculine in Portuguese and Spanish. In Spanish, el partido also means the soccer match; in Portuguese, the match is a partida instead.

Today's three refreshers are candidate, party, and coalition.
