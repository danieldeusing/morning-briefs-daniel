Vocab: Rate Decision — einbrechen, einpreisen, nachgeben

Monday, July 27 — rotation theme Economy & Finance, and this time the occasion supplies itself. This morning's economy brief leads with an oil-price plunge: Brent at $92.11, 6.4% below Friday's close of $98.38 — two days before the Fed decides on its target range of 3.50–3.75% on Wednesday, July 29, at 15:00 BRT. That knocks out the pillar the rate-hike bet of the past week was standing on. Today's vocabulary hangs on exactly that: 9 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, plus 2 refreshers from July 6. Throughline: German presses a whole sentence into one word (einpreisen, die Zinssenkung), Portuguese and Spanish unpack it again — except for a single Brazilian verb that's shorter than anything German has for it.

  • FALSE FRIEND Billion ≠ billion — Duden defines die Billion as „eine Million Millionen", i.e., 1012. English billion and Brazilian bilhão are only 109 — German eine Milliarde. Portuguese and Spanish split apart right here: bilhão = 109, billón = 1012. duden.de · rebeccajowers.com
  • CONJUGATION eu despenquei, yo descuento — two completely different irregularities in one brief. despencar only swaps letters to rescue the sound (c→qu before e). descontar swaps the stem vowel itself (o→ue), but only under stress — nosotros descontamos stays plain. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net
  • CONJUGATION ist eingebrochen, but hat nachgegeben — both separable, both in today's brief, different auxiliary verbs. verbformen.de: „Als Hilfsverb von einbrechen wird 'sein' verwendet" versus „Als Hilfsverb von nachgeben wird 'haben' verwendet". verbformen.de
  • BR-PT a commodity — Brazilian financial Portuguese borrows the English word and makes it feminine. Priberam lists it as nome feminino, plural commodities, origin „palavra inglesa". Spanish sticks with la materia prima, German with der Rohstoff. priberam.org
  • PHRASE Zielband · intervalo · rango objetivo — the same Fed range, three technical terms. Brazilian financial press writes intervalo entre 3,50% e 3,75%, the Spanish-language press el rango objetivo. Nobody says banda. diariolibre.com
  • IDIOM den Gürtel enger schnallen — for once, the same image in all four languages. Duden: „sich in seinen Bedürfnissen einschränken"; Priberam on apertar o cinto: „Reduzir gastos, sobretudo em época de crise"; the Instituto Cervantes on apretarse el cinturón: „Reducir los gastos". duden.de · cvc.cervantes.es
  • BR-PT Kohle · grana · lana — four languages, four materials, one meaning: money. Coal, dyed cloth, wool, dough. And lana is explicitly Latin American (Mexico, Chile, Peru, El Salvador), not Spain. priberam.org · es.wiktionary.org

The Three Verbs Ahead of the Rate Decision

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br, and conjugator.reverso.net

Pricing in: When traders believe something is going to happen, they already act today as if it had happened. The expectation is then already baked into the current price — it's priced in. Imagine the newspaper says on Monday that it will rain on Friday: the umbrella already costs more on Tuesday, not just on Friday. That's why the news itself often no longer moves the market at all — and that's why the reverse case is the interesting one: if the expectation falls away, the price has to build it back in. All three of today's verbs are separable: ein·brechen, ein·preisen, nach·geben. In a main clause the prefix wanders to the end (der Kurs bricht ein), and in the participle the ge- wedges itself in between: eingebrochen, eingepreist, nachgegeben. Portuguese and Spanish don't have this trick — they use a separate, indivisible verb for each meaning. sein or haben in the perfect tense: German builds the perfect tense with haben, except for verbs of motion or change of state — those take sein. A price that plunges changes state: ist eingebrochen. A price that eases merely does something: hat nachgegeben. The Romance languages don't have this problem — there it all runs through ter or haber.

to plunge, to slump

When the price plunges, the bet on a rate hike is dead on the spot.

despencar

O preço do petróleo despencou 6,4 por cento nesta manhã.

Quando o preço despenca, a aposta em uma alta de juros morre na hora.

Depois do fim de semana, as ações de petróleo também despencaram.

El precio del petróleo se desplomó 6,4 por ciento esta mañana.

Refresher from July 6 The same word, three completely different images. German einbrechen leads a double life: a burglar breaks into a house, and a price breaks down — both take sein in the perfect. Brazilian despencar comes from falling from a height and is the standard word in the business press for a steep drop. Mexican desplomarse is packed with plomo, lead: something falls like a plumb bob, straight down and without brakes. The occasion is real — Brent stands at $92.11 today after Friday's close of $98.38. Memory aid: despencar and desplomarse always go down. For "to shoot up," Brazil says disparar. verbformen.de · conjugacao.com.br

Verb DE weak & separable, perfect with haben (hat eingepreist) — not like preisen/pries/gepriesen · PT-BR precificar: c→qu before e · ES-MX descontar: stem change o→ue under stress
einpreisen

Der Markt preist heute nur noch eine Zinspause ein.

Vergangene Woche hatte er noch eine Erhöhung eingepreist.

Wenn alle dasselbe einpreisen, bewegt die Nachricht den Kurs nicht mehr.

to price in

The market is only pricing in a pause today.

Last week it had still priced in a hike.

When everyone prices in the same thing, the news no longer moves the price.

precificar

O mercado precifica hoje apenas uma pausa nos juros.

Na semana passada, ele ainda tinha precificado uma alta.

Quando todo mundo precifica a mesma coisa, a notícia não mexe mais no preço.

descontar

El mercado hoy solo descuenta una pausa en las tasas.

La semana pasada todavía había descontado un alza.

Cuando todos descuentan lo mismo, la noticia ya no mueve el precio.

Ambiguous in Spanish Descontar otherwise means "to deduct" — in market jargon it means "to price in." Anyone who reads el mercado ya descontó una subida de tasas and thinks of a discount is wrong: Yahoo Finanzas explains the phrase as „los participantes del mercado ya han anticipado y reflejado en los precios… la posibilidad de que los tipos de interés suban" — the expectation is already in the price. Portuguese takes the more transparent route and builds its own verb out of preço; InfoMoney: „Quando se diz que 'o mercado já precificou uma alta de juros', significa que os investidores… já incorporaram essa expectativa em seus preços de ativos." German does it the same way as Portuguese — Preis plus prefix. A side trap within German itself: einpreisen is regular (preiste ein, hat eingepreist) and does not follow the irregular preisen (pries, gepriesen) from jemanden preisen = "to praise someone." infomoney.com.br · es.finance.yahoo.com

Verb DE strong & separable, perfect with haben (hat nachgegeben) · PT-BR and ES-MX both ceder, both regular · press alternatives: PT recuar, ES retroceder
nachgeben

Der Dollar gibt heute nach: der DXY steht bei 101,21.

Am Freitag hatte der Real dem Druck noch nicht nachgegeben.

Gibt der Ölpreis weiter nach, klingt die Fed am Mittwoch entspannter.

to ease, to give way

The dollar is easing today: the DXY stands at 101.21.

On Friday the real had not yet given way to the pressure.

If the oil price keeps easing, the Fed will sound calmer on Wednesday.

ceder

O dólar cede hoje: o DXY está em 101,21.

Na sexta-feira, o real ainda não tinha cedido à pressão.

Se o preço do petróleo continuar cedendo, o Fed vai soar mais tranquilo na quarta.

ceder

El dólar cede hoy: el DXY está en 101,21.

El viernes, el real todavía no había cedido a la presión.

Si el precio del petróleo sigue cediendo, la Fed va a sonar más tranquila el miércoles.

Today's Auxiliary-Verb Pair einbrechen takes sein, nachgeben takes haben — even though both are talking about the same falling price. verbformen.de is unambiguous here: „Als Hilfsverb von nachgeben wird 'haben' verwendet", preterite er gab nach, participle nachgegeben. The difference is the size of the step: a price that einbricht is a different price afterward — a change of state, hence sein. A price that nachgibt merely retreats a bit and could come back tomorrow — a plain action, hence haben. Portuguese and Spanish carry exactly this nuance in ceder: to give way, to give up resistance, to retreat a step. In Brazilian market coverage you also often read recuar, and in Mexican coverage retroceder — both literally "to step back." That's press usage, not a dictionary rule this brief verified; as a learning form, ceder is the better choice because it looks identical in both languages. Figure to match: DXY 101.21, down 0.25% versus Friday. verbformen.de · conjugacao.com.br

False Friend The most expensive translation mistake you can make in a piece of economic writing — it costs three zeros. Duden on Billion: „eine Million Millionen", i.e. 1012. English billion, by contrast, is only 109 and is called Milliarde in German. The memory table:
109 — DE eine Milliarde · EN one billion · PT-BR um bilhão · ES-MX mil millones.
1012 — DE eine Billion · EN one trillion · PT-BR um trilhão · ES-MX un billón.
The punchline: Portuguese and Spanish, otherwise nearly twins, split apart on exactly this point. Brazil counts on the short scale like England, Spanish on the long scale like Germany. In practice: anyone converting Brazil's figures into Spanish has to turn bilhão into mil millones, never into billón — precisely the case with the budget figures that today's economy brief reports in billions of reais. duden.de/Billion · der-die-das-deutsch.com · rebeccajowers.com

der Leitzins

Key interest rate: the price at which banks borrow money from the central bank. It steers every other interest rate — loans, savings accounts, mortgages — in the same direction, the way the lead car in a convoy sets the pace for everyone behind it. When it rises, every loan in the country gets more expensive; when it falls, they get cheaper.
What the Three Languages Call the Fed's Range On Wednesday the Fed won't name a single rate, but a range — currently 3.50–3.75%. German calls this das Zielband or der Zielkorridor. Brazilian financial press doesn't use banda here; it writes o intervalo entre 3,50% e 3,75%; the Spanish-language press talks about the rango objetivo, as diariolibre did on the FOMC's July meeting. Second thing to remember: Brazil has a proper name for its key rate, a Selic, treated like a surname — a Selic está em 14,25%, since the unanimous Copom decision of June 16/17, the third cut in a row. This figure could not be verified today against the Brazilian primary source: agenciabrasil.ebc.com.br is offline because of the electoral blackout period. It's documented via midiamax and via today's economy brief, both of which cite 14.25% as of June 17. Third thing to remember, the movement itself: die Zinssenkung → o corte de juros / el recorte de tasas, die Zinserhöhung → a alta de juros / el alza de tasas. German screws two words together; the Romance languages need noun + preposition + noun — and Brazil talks about juros where Mexico says tasas. The midiamax headline shows both Brazilian building blocks: „Decisão unânime do Copom baixa taxa Selic para 14,25% ao ano". A word of caution on Spanish el alza: feminine, but with a masculine article, because it begins with a stressed a — like el agua. diariolibre.com · midiamax.com.br

Brazilian Brazilian financial Portuguese imports the English word — and makes it feminine. Priberam lists commodity as nome feminino, origin „palavra inglesa", plural commodities, meaning: „Matéria-prima ou mercadoria primária produzida em grande quantidade, cujo preço é regulado pela oferta e pela procura internacionais". Spanish, by contrast, reaches for materia prima, German for der Rohstoff — die Commodity only exists there in banking jargon. Noun REVIEW last on July 6 · DE f, mostly without a plural · PT-BR a volatilidade, f · ES-MX la volatilidad, f
die Volatilität

Sechs Prozent an einem Tag — das ist Volatilität, kein Trend.

Vor einer Notenbanksitzung steigt die Volatilität fast immer.

the volatility

Six percent in a single day — that is volatility, not a trend.

Volatility almost always rises ahead of a central bank meeting.

a volatilidade

Seis por cento em um dia só — isso é volatilidade, não é tendência.

Antes de uma reunião do banco central, a volatilidade quase sempre aumenta.

la volatilidad

Seis por ciento en un solo día — eso es volatilidad, no es tendencia.

Antes de una reunión del banco central, la volatilidad casi siempre sube.

Volatility: how hard a price jumps back and forth — not which direction it moves. Two cars can both arrive at the same place in an hour; one drives smoothly, the other jolts over potholes. The route is the same, the volatility isn't. That's why a single minus-6.4-percent day isn't a downtrend yet — for now it's just one deep pothole.
Refresher from July 6 — with a Building-Block Kit This word is the key to hundreds of others. The German ending -ität regularly corresponds to Portuguese -idade, Spanish -idad, and English -ity: Volatilität → volatilidade → volatilidad → volatility. The same pattern carries Universität / universidade / universidad and Qualität / qualidade / calidad. Watch out with Liquidität: there the pattern breaks, and both Portuguese and Spanish switch to liquidez. They're all feminine — in German as in both target languages. So anyone who knows a German -ität word can guess the Romance form and will usually be right.

Register — Don't Write This on an Invoice Four languages, four materials, one meaning. Three Dictionaries, One Image After a week full of words that split apart, here finally is one that holds together. Duden under Gürtel: „den Gürtel enger schnallen (umgangssprachlich: sich in seinen Bedürfnissen einschränken)", example „die Bevölkerung musste den Gürtel enger schnallen". Priberam under cinto: „Apertar o cinto — Reduzir gastos, sobretudo em época de crise". And the Instituto Cervantes's repertoire lists, as number 214: „Apretarse el cinturón — Reducir los gastos (se dice principalmente cuando es por dificultades económicas)". The fine differences lie in the grammar: German and Portuguese buckle the belt like an object, Spanish turns it into a reflexive apretarse — you tighten it around yourself. Portuguese skips the comparative step entirely: no enger, no más, just apertar o cinto. And a false trail: o cinto de segurança is the car's seatbelt — it has nothing to do with saving money. duden.de/Gürtel · priberam.org/cinto · cvc.cervantes.es

It's Monday, July twenty-seventh. The rotation theme is economy and finance, and today the occasion supplies itself. The economy brief leads with an oil-price plunge: Brent stands at ninety-two dollars and eleven cents, six point four percent below Friday's close of ninety-eight dollars and thirty-eight cents. Nine entries today, three of them verbs with full conjugation, plus two refreshers.

The schedule is what makes this vocabulary urgent. On Wednesday, July twenty-ninth, at three p.m. Brasília time, the Fed decides on its target range of three point five to three point seventy-five percent. With the oil price, the exact pillar that last week's rate-hike bet was standing on falls away. Today's three verbs tell that story in the right order: to plunge, to price in, to ease.

The first verb is a refresher from July sixth: einbrechen, in the sense of a price. In Brazilian Portuguese that's despencar, in Mexican Spanish desplomarse. Both always go down, never up. Despencar comes from falling from a height and is the standard word in the Brazilian business press for a steep drop. Desplomarse is packed with plomo, lead: something falls like a plumb bob, straight down and without brakes.

The second verb is the most important one today: einpreisen, to price in. It means an expectation is already baked into today's price. Imagine the newspaper says on Monday that it will rain on Friday. Then the umbrella already costs more on Tuesday, not just on Friday. Portuguese says precificar, built from preço, price. Spanish, though, says descontar, and that's a trap: descontar usually means to deduct or to give a discount. Anyone who reads el mercado ya descontó una subida de tasas must not think of a discount.

The third verb is nachgeben, to ease, and together with the first it forms today's auxiliary-verb pair. Einbrechen takes sein: der Kurs ist eingebrochen. Nachgeben takes haben: der Kurs hat nachgegeben. Verbformen dot de is unambiguous about that. The difference is the size of the step. Something that plunges is a different thing afterward — that's a change of state. Something that eases only retreats a bit and could come back tomorrow. Portuguese and Spanish use the same word for this, ceder, regular in both languages. The occasion behind it is real: the dollar index stands today at one hundred one point two one, a quarter percent below Friday.

Today's spelling brings together two completely different irregularities in one brief. Despencar and precificar just swap letters to rescue the sound: eu despenquei, eu precifiquei, with a u slipped in before the e. Descontar, on the other hand, swaps the stem vowel itself: yo descuento, with ue instead of o — but only under stress. In the nosotros form it stays plain: nosotros descontamos. And in the preterite the diphthong disappears entirely: yo desconté.

Today's false friend is the most expensive one you can make in a piece of economic writing. It costs three zeros. Duden defines the German Billion as a million millions, that is, ten to the twelfth power. English billion, by contrast, is only ten to the ninth power and is called Milliarde in German. The punchline: Portuguese and Spanish, otherwise nearly twins, split apart on exactly this point. Brazil counts like England — one bilhão is ten to the ninth. Spanish counts like Germany — one billón is ten to the twelfth. Anyone converting Brazilian figures into Spanish must always turn bilhão into mil millones, never into billón.

Add to that three technical terms for Wednesday. The key interest rate is the price at which banks borrow money from the central bank, and it steers every other interest rate in the country in the same direction. In Brazilian Portuguese it's a taxa básica de juros, and it even has a proper name, a Selic, which has stood at fourteen point two five percent since June seventeenth. In Mexican Spanish it's la tasa de referencia. The Fed's range itself is called intervalo in Brazil, rango objetivo in Mexico, Zielband in Germany. Nobody says banda. One caveat on the Selic figure: the Brazilian primary source is offline because of the electoral blackout period; it's documented via midiamax and via today's economy brief.

Two observations about how the languages are built. First: German screws Zins and Senkung together into one word; Portuguese and Spanish need noun, preposition, noun — corte de juros, recorte de tasas. Anyone starting from German is best off unpacking the compound from back to front. Second, the vocabulary fork between the countries: Brazil talks about juros, Mexico about tasas. And Brazil imports the English word for commodity outright and makes it feminine — a commodity, plural commodities, pronounced còmóditi.

Also getting a refresher today is Volatilität from July sixth. It means how hard a price jumps back and forth, not which direction — so a single minus-six-percent day still isn't a downtrend, just a deep pothole for now. And it's the memory key for hundreds of other words: German minus ität becomes Portuguese minus idade and Spanish minus idad. Add to that a slang word in four materials: die Kohle, a grana, la lana, dough. Coal, dyed cloth, wool, dough — none of the four languages simply says money. And finally, the idiom that for once carries the same image everywhere: den Gürtel enger schnallen, apertar o cinto, apretarse el cinturón. Tomorrow the rotation turns to software and technology. Until then, today's sentence: o mercado já precificou tudo, agora é esperar a quarta-feira.
