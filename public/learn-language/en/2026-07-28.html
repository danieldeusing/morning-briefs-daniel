Vocab: Age Rating — freigeben, nachweisen, veröffentlichen

Tuesday, July 28 — weekly theme Software & Technology, and today's hook falls exactly on the calendar date. Roblox is rolling its AI building feature Build out into public alpha: available to age-verified users age 9 and up in New Zealand, while anyone who wants to publish a finished game worldwide has to be 16 — also age-verified. Add the counterpoint from the weekend: on Sunday, July 26, the App Store, Apple Music, Apple TV, and AppleCare all went down at once for several hours, partly due to routine maintenance work, and were back up the same day. Two stories, one word field: 11 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, plus 2 refreshers from July 21. The through-line: German bolts a word together from two building blocks (Alters-Freigabe, Wartungs-Arbeiten), while Portuguese and Spanish need half a sentence for the same thing — and of all things, at the age gate it's Brazil that has the more official term, not Germany.

  • FALSE FRIEND Handy ≠ handy — das Handy is a pseudo-Anglicism: in English, handy means "useful, convenient" and is never used for phones. Anyone in Ribeirão Claro who means a phone says celular anyway — in Portuguese as in Spanish. der-die-das-deutsch.com
  • FALSE FRIEND sensibel ≠ sensible — the refresher from 07/21 has a trap that affects only one language. German sensibel, Portuguese sensível, and Spanish sensible all mean "sensitive, delicate." English sensible, by contrast, means "reasonable" — the English word for age data is sensitive.
  • CONJUGATION yo compruebo, but nosotros comprobamos — comprobar ("to prove") is an o→ue stem-changer: under stress the o becomes a diphthong, while the nosotros row keeps the plain stem. In the pretérito the diphthong disappears entirely: yo comprobé. reverso.net
  • CONJUGATION eu publiquei, yo publiqué — publicar is a -car verb: before an e, a u slips in to keep the hard /k/ sound alive. Portuguese and Spanish do the same thing here, including in the subjunctive: publique.
  • BR-PT o aplicativo — Priberam explicitly tags the noun „[Brasil] [Informática]" and names a aplicação as its European Portuguese counterpart. In São Paulo you download an aplicativo; in Lisbon, an aplicação. priberam.org
  • GENDER Wartungsarbeiten (pl.) ↔ a manutenção (sg.) — German thinks of maintenance as many individual jobs and uses the plural; Portuguese and Spanish use an uncountable singular instead. "Wegen Wartungsarbeiten" becomes por causa da manutenção, never a plural.
  • IDIOM auf Herz und Nieren prüfen — Duden: „gründlich, eingehend prüfen; nach Psalm 7, 10" ("to examine thoroughly, in detail; after Psalm 7:10"). Brazil reaches for a comb instead of a scalpel: passar a pente-fino, literally to go through with a lice comb. duden.de · priberam.org

The Three Verbs at the Age Gate

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br, and conjugator.reverso.net

Age-verified alpha phase: An alpha phase is the very first public round of a piece of software — not finished yet, kept deliberately small so bugs hurt while they're still cheap. Picture a restaurant that lets only the neighbors sample the food for a week before it opens for real. Age-verified means: before you're even let in, you have to prove how old you are — not just claim it, prove it. Roblox is doing both at once today: you can get in starting at age 9, but making your own game visible to the whole world requires age 16, and in both cases only with a verified age. That's exactly the order of today's three verbs: the provider releases the feature, the user proves their age, and only then may they publish their game. about.roblox.com · techcrunch.com

Roblox libera la función para Nueva Zelanda hoy.

El juego solo se liberó para usuarios de dieciséis años en adelante.

Si la prueba pasa, liberamos la actualización mañana.

Two meanings, one word German freigeben carries two things that constantly get mixed up in everyday use. First: releasing a piece of software to users — eine Version freigeben. Second: opening an age barrier — ab 16 freigegeben. Portuguese and Spanish handle both meanings with the same verb, liberar — Brazil's standard word in the tech press: o recurso foi liberado. There's just one trap: liberar also means "to free, to release (a person)" — liberar um preso is a prisoner, not a feature. The hook is real: Roblox is releasing Build today in New Zealand for age-verified users age nine and up. about.roblox.com

Today's stem change Spanish comprobar is an o→ue stem-changer — and it doesn't show up on the conjugator script's trap list, so it's flagged here by hand. The rule is the same as with contar → cuento or consolar → consuelo: when the syllable carries the stress, the o turns into ue. Reverso shows exactly that pattern — yo compruebo, tú compruebas, él comprueba, ustedes comprueban, but nosotros comprobamos with no diphthong, because there the stress falls on the ending. In the pretérito it disappears completely: yo comprobé. Portuguese comprovar skips this whole dance and stays regular throughout — the difference between the two languages here is a single letter in the infinitive (b versus v) and an entire conjugation type. reverso.net · conjugacao.com.br

Refresher from 07/21 Back then it was about security vulnerabilities that get published; today it's a game that gets published — and both times the same grammar trap is hiding inside. Veröffentlichen has an inseparable prefix (ver-), so it stays glued to the verb in a sentence: ich veröffentliche, never ich öffentliche ver. And the past participle skips the ge-: hat veröffentlicht, not *hat geveröffentlicht. That's exactly what sets it apart from this brief's separable verbs — freigeben → hat freigegeben, nachweisen → hat nachgewiesen. Three verbs, the same page, two participle rules. Memory trick: whatever you stress when speaking (FREIgeben, NACHweisen) splits apart; whatever sits unstressed up front (ver-, be-, ent-) stays put. The hook: anyone who wants to publish a game worldwide on Roblox has to be age-verified and at least sixteen. about.roblox.com

Brazil has the more official term here German bolts two building blocks together (Alter + Freigabe) and uses it to mean all sorts of things; Brazil has a fixed legal term instead. According to Agência Brasil, the classificação indicativa is „atribuída a conteúdos de diversões e espetáculos públicos, obras audiovisuais e demais produtos classificáveis", and its tiers are called Livre, 6, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18. Important for today: since Portaria nº 1.048/2025 of October 15, 2025, the review explicitly extends to the functions and interaction mechanisms of digital products — that is, to apps and video games, not just films anymore. Which means the Brazilian framework for a case like today's Roblox alpha is already on the books. A translation caution: indicativa means "advisory," not "binding" — it's a recommendation to parents, not a sales ban. Anyone who wants to say the same thing in Spanish uses the neutral clasificación por edad. agenciabrasil.ebc.com.br

Rating ≠ check The two words look similar and sit at opposite ends of the same process. The verbs for this sit on the left: freigeben for one side, nachweisen for the other. In Portuguese, verificação is the neutral word; if you mean the action itself, you say comprovar a idade. In Spanish that's comprobar la edad — this is the dictionary phrasing, not an official government term, and it isn't presented here as one. techcrunch.com

Refresher from 07/21 — with a trap Three out of four languages agree; English is the one that breaks rank. German sensibel, Portuguese sensível, and Spanish sensible all mean "sensitive, delicate, in need of protection." English sensible, by contrast, means "reasonable, level-headed" — the costliest mix-up in this whole word field: whoever writes sensible data in an English privacy document is really saying "reasonable data." The right word is sensitive data. Conversely, German vernünftig is sensato in both Portuguese and Spanish. Why this matters today: an age check only works if someone uploads a document — which means the single most sensitive kind of data there is flows straight to a gaming platform.

False friend An English-sounding word that doesn't actually exist in English. Das Handy is a pseudo-Anglicism: it sounds English, but Germans invented it. In English, handy is an adjective meaning „useful or convenient" — "a handy tool" is a useful tool, not a phone. If you mean a phone in English, you say mobile phone (British) or cell phone (US). Small comfort for everyday life in Ribeirão Claro: Portuguese and Spanish handle it identically and simply — o celular and el celular, both masculine, both from "radio cell." Only in Spain is it el móvil, and in Portugal o telemóvel — neither applies here. And it matters today because Roblox is rolling out its new building feature explicitly as a tab inside the mobile app, not on desktop. der-die-das-deutsch.com · techcrunch.com

Plural versus singular The number is the whole difference here. German breaks maintenance down into many individual jobs and, in announcements, uses almost exclusively the plural: „wegen Wartungsarbeiten". Portuguese and Spanish use an uncountable singular word instead — a manutenção, el mantenimiento — and a plural sounds wrong there. Anyone who writes „por causa das manutenções" instantly gives themselves away as a translator. A memory trick for the gender: Portuguese feminine (-ção is always feminine), Spanish masculine (-miento is always masculine) — the same concept, opposite gender. It fits today's hook exactly: the Apple outage on 07/26 was partly attributed to routine maintenance.

It's Tuesday, July twenty-eighth. This week's theme is software and technology, and the news hook falls right on today's date. Roblox is rolling its new building feature, called Build, out into public alpha. Eleven entries today, three of them verbs with full conjugation, plus two refreshers from July twenty-first.

The numbers in the Roblox story are the whole vocabulary set for today. The feature is available only in New Zealand at first, and only for age-verified users nine and up. Anyone who wants to make a finished game visible to a worldwide audience, though, has to be at least sixteen, also with a verified age, and the game has to pass a safety review first. The provider releases it, the user proves their age, and only then can they publish. Those are exactly today's three verbs.

The first verb is freigeben, to release. In German it carries two things at once: letting a piece of software loose on users, and opening up an age barrier. Portuguese and Spanish handle both with the same word, liberar, fully regular in both languages. There's still a small trap: liberar also means to free or release a person. If you say liberar um preso, you mean a prisoner, not a feature.

The second verb is nachweisen, to prove rather than just claim. In Brazilian Portuguese that's comprovar, and it stays nicely regular. In Mexican Spanish it's comprobar, and that's today's conjugation trap. Comprobar swaps its stem vowel: the o becomes ue, but only where the stress falls. Yo compruebo, tú compruebas, él comprueba, ustedes comprueban. In the nosotros row it stays plain: nosotros comprobamos. And in the pretérito, the diphthong disappears entirely: yo comprobé.

The third verb is today's refresher from July twenty-first: veröffentlichen, to publish. Back then it was about security vulnerabilities; today it's a game. Both times, the stumbling block is the prefix. Freigeben and nachweisen are separable, so their participles are hat freigegeben and hat nachgewiesen, with the ge- in the middle. Veröffentlichen is inseparable, so it's simply hat veröffentlicht, with no ge- at all. Memory trick: whatever you stress when you say the word splits off; whatever sits unstressed up front stays put. Portuguese and Spanish both say publicar, and both swap in a letter before an e to protect the sound: eu publiquei, yo publiqué.

When it comes to the noun, it's Brazil, of all places, that has the more official term. German just bolts age and rating together into one word. Brazil has a fixed legal term for it, the classificação indicativa, with tiers of free, six, ten, twelve, fourteen, sixteen, and eighteen. Since a regulation from October of twenty twenty-five, that review explicitly covers apps and video games too, not just films anymore. One translation caution: indicativa means advisory, not binding. It's a recommendation to parents, not a sales ban.

Two false friends show up in today's brief. The first is das Handy, an English-sounding word Germans invented themselves. In English, handy is an adjective meaning useful or convenient; it's never used for phones. Portuguese and Spanish make it simpler and identical: o celular and el celular, both masculine. The second is sensibel, today's second refresher from July twenty-first. German, Portuguese, and Spanish all use it to mean sensitive or in need of protection. English sensible, though, means reasonable. If you write sensible data in an English privacy document, you're really saying reasonable data. The correct term is sensitive data.

The weekend supplied the counter-example to the age gate. On Sunday, July twenty-sixth, several Apple services went down together: the App Store, Apple Music, Apple TV, AppleCare, and Apple School Manager. Apple reported everything back up the very same day. Linguistically, the comparison is worth making: German calls it Störung, English outage, and Brazil prefers the softer instabilidade, as if the service were just a little shaky rather than actually down. In Mexican Spanish, people just say la falla.

Finally, the idiom. In German, you check something auf Herz und Nieren — literally, on heart and kidneys. Duden defines that colloquially as examining something thoroughly, and traces it back to Psalm seven, verse ten, where the heart and kidneys were seen as the seat of will and conscience. So German looks inward, while Brazil reaches for a comb instead: passar a pente-fino, to go through with a fine-tooth comb. English happens to land on the very same image, with a fine-tooth comb. No dictionary entry turned up today for the Spanish version, so revisar con lupa appears here only as an everyday phrase, not as documented usage.
