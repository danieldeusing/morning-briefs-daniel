Vocab: New Arrival — gebären, erwarten, stillen

Wednesday, July 29 — rotation theme Family & Everyday Life, and today's topic is the event that remodels a household faster than any move: a baby arrives. This word field is arguably the richest trap field these four languages have to offer: the most famous false friend between Spanish and Portuguese (embarazada means pregnant, embaraçada means embarrassed), the German perfect-tense trap of the year (ich habe geboren — the mother, not the child), and a Brazilian word for "baby tea" where Mexico simply borrows the English term. 11 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, plus 2 refreshers from July 8. The through-line: the Romance languages pack bridal trousseau, school kit, and baby layette into one word — German needs three.

  • FALSE FRIEND embarazada ≠ embaraçada — Spanish embarazada means "pregnant" (Carolina está embarazada de gemelos). Portuguese embaraçada means "embarrassed, tangled, confused" — Priberam: „Causar ou sentir vergonha ou acanhamento". Two neighboring languages, one letter apart, a world of meaning apart. spanishdict.com · priberam.org
  • CONJUGATION ich habe geboren, but ich bin geboren — verbformen.de lists haben as the auxiliary verb for gebären: ich habe geboren. Whoever says ich bin geboren is saying something completely different — that's the child, not the mother. verbformen.de
  • CONJUGATION eu dou à luz, yo doy a luz — for "to give birth," Portuguese and Spanish use the same image ("to give to the light") and the same irregular verb, dar. In the perfect tense the paths split: eu dei versus yo di, nós demos versus nosotros dimos. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net
  • GENDER a gravidez (f) ↔ el embarazo (m) — same concept, opposite gender. Portuguese ends in -ez and is feminine, Spanish ends in -azo and is masculine. With the diaper, the sign flips again: a fralda (f) versus el pañal (m).
  • BR-PT o chá de bebê — Brazil invites you to "baby tea," not a shower: Linguee explicitly lists chá de bebê as „noun, masculine [Braz.] — baby shower". Mexico, by contrast, borrows the English baby shower without comment. linguee.com
  • BR-PT o enxoval — one word for three German ones: Priberam defines it as „Conjunto de roupas de um recém-nascido, de um colegial, etc". So baby layette, school kit, and bridal trousseau in a single term; Spanish does exactly the same with el ajuar. priberam.org
  • IDIOM in anderen Umständen sein — dict.cc gives „to be expecting [coll.] [pregnant]" as the equivalent, alongside in the family way. Brazil has the more direct version: Priberam lists de barriga as „Em estado de gravidez". dict.cc · priberam.org

The Three Verbs of Arrival

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br, and conjugator.reverso.net

Auxiliary verb in the perfect tense: In everyday German, the past tense is built in two parts — auxiliary verb plus participle: ich habe gegessen, ich bin gefahren. Which auxiliary a verb takes isn't a matter of taste; it belongs to the verb the way an article belongs to a noun. Picture every verb bringing its own driver along: most ride with haben, and only a small group (movement and change of state — gehen, aufwachen, sterben) rides with sein. And that's exactly where today's trap sits: gebären describes the biggest change of state there is, but grammatically it's a normal transitive verb with an object — the mother does something to the child. That's why it rides with haben. Portuguese and Spanish don't have this problem at all: there, ter and haber respectively build every compound tense, without exception.
Verb DE strong, irregular (ä–a–o), dual forms gebärst/gebierst, perfect with haben · PT-BR dar à luz (dar irregular) · ES-MX dar a luz (dar irregular)
gebären

Sie hat ihr erstes Kind im Krankenhaus geboren.

Wo möchtest du gebären — zu Hause oder in der Klinik?

Meine Schwester wird nächsten Monat ihr zweites Kind gebären.

to give birth

She gave birth to her first child in the hospital.

Where do you want to give birth — at home or at the clinic?

My sister will give birth to her second child next month.

dar à luz

Ela deu à luz o primeiro filho no hospital.

Onde você quer dar à luz — em casa ou na clínica?

Minha irmã vai dar à luz o segundo filho no mês que vem.

dar a luz

Ella dio a luz a su primer hijo en el hospital.

¿Dónde quieres dar a luz — en casa o en la clínica?

Mi hermana va a dar a luz a su segundo hijo el mes que viene.

The mother has, the child is Two sentences that look almost identical and mean opposite people. Ich habe geboren — that's the mother talking about her own accomplishment; verbformen.de explicitly lists haben as the auxiliary for gebären: „ich habe geboren, du hast geboren, er/sie/es hat geboren". Ich bin geboren, by contrast, isn't a perfect tense of an action at all — it's the passive voice of the child: Ich bin 1986 in Deutschland geboren. Same participle, two completely different roles — only the auxiliary verb decides who's meant. Portuguese and Spanish sidestep the mix-up entirely because they use two different verbs: the mother deu à luz / dio a luz, the child nasceu / nació. So anyone talking about themselves in Ribeirão Claro simply says eu nasci em 1986. verbformen.de

Verb DE weak, inseparable prefix er- → participle without ge-: erwartet · PT-BR / ES-MX esperar, regular, but triple-duty
erwarten

Wir erwarten im Dezember ein Kind.

Sie hat dieses Ergebnis schon lange erwartet.

Erwartest du noch jemanden, oder fangen wir an?

to expect

We are expecting a baby in December.

She has expected this result for a long time.

Are you expecting anyone else, or shall we start?

esperar

Estamos esperando um bebê para dezembro.

Ela já esperava esse resultado havia muito tempo.

Você ainda espera mais alguém, ou a gente começa?

esperar

Estamos esperando un bebé para diciembre.

Ella ya esperaba ese resultado desde hacía mucho tiempo.

¿Todavía esperas a alguien más, o empezamos?

One verb, three German words German cleanly separates warten, erwarten, and hoffen; Portuguese and Spanish throw all three into a single verb. Espero o ônibus is waiting. Espero um bebê is expecting. Espero que sim is hoping — and only in this third meaning does esperar trigger the subjunctive: espero que dê tudo certo, espero que todo salga bien. If you can't hear the difference, you can spot it in the clause that follows: if a que with a subjunctive form comes after it, hope is meant. And on the German side, the reverse trap: erwarten isn't a synonym for warten auf ("to wait for") — "ich warte auf das Baby" sounds like you're standing on a platform, while "wir erwarten ein Kind" is the fixed phrase for expecting a baby. And a stumbling block from word formation: the prefix er- is unstressed and inseparable, which is why the participle is erwartet — without ge-, unlike gewartet. verbformen.de

One German word, three actions The third example sentence shows why this word can't be translated cleanly. verbformen.de lists three meanings for stillen: „etwas (meist Bedürfnisse) beruhigen, abschwächen", „einem Kind die Muttermilch geben", and „bewirken, dass etwas aufhört (zu fließen)". German sees the same underlying motion in all three — bringing something to rest, making it still. Portuguese and Spanish need three separate verbs for that: the baby amamentar / amamantar, thirst matar a sede / quitar la sed, bleeding estancar / cortar la hemorragia. For everyday life with a baby, by the way, the colloquial forms matter more than the dictionary words: in Brazil people mostly say dar de mamar, in Mexico dar pecho — both sound less like a parenting guide and more like something said at the kitchen table. verbformen.de

die Schwangerschaft

Same concept, opposite gender Portuguese and Spanish aren't just different here, they're opposite — and in both cases the ending gives it away. Portuguese abstract nouns ending in -ez are feminine: a gravidez, like a rapidez and a timidez. Spanish nouns ending in -azo are masculine: el embarazo, like el abrazo. Anyone who remembers the ending never has to learn the gender separately. A small extra trap in the plural: gravidez ends in -z and therefore forms gravidezes, not *gravidezs — the same rule as luz → luzes, which you already know from dar à luz.

The most expensive letter between Spanish and Portuguese Two words that are spelled almost the same and sound almost the same — and mean something completely different in neighboring countries. Spanish embarazada means nothing other than "pregnant"; SpanishDict lists it as „[pregnant], [pregnant woman]" with the example „Carolina está embarazada de gemelos". Portuguese embaraçada has nothing to do with pregnancy at all: Priberam collects under it „Causar ou sofrer embaraço ou impedimento", „Tornar ou ficar misturado de forma confusa", and „Causar ou sentir vergonha ou acanhamento" — in other words, hindered, tangled, embarrassed. Anyone who says „minha esposa está embaraçada" is announcing that their wife is embarrassed about something. The trap has a third side, too: because embarazada resembles the English embarrassed, the path from Spanish to English produces the same accident in reverse. Memory aid: only Portuguese has the cedilla, and only Portuguese means embarrassment. The only correct word there is grávida. spanishdict.com · priberam.org

Tea instead of a shower Three languages, three completely different ways of handling the same imported celebration. Brazil translated it fully and reinterpreted it along the way: Linguee lists chá de bebê as „noun, masculine [Braz.] — baby shower". It's Wednesday, July 29th. This week's rotation theme is Family and Everyday Life, and today's topic is the event that remodels a household faster than any move: a baby arrives. There are eleven entries today, including three verbs with full conjugation, plus two refreshers from July 8th.

Let's start with the false friend, because it's the priciest one. Spanish embarazada simply means pregnant. SpanishDict gives the example: Carolina está embarazada de gemelos — Carolina is pregnant with twins. Portuguese embaraçada, spelled with a cedilla, has nothing to do with pregnancy at all. Priberam defines it as hindered, tangled, or embarrassed. So if you tell someone in Ribeirão Claro minha esposa está embaraçada, you're saying your wife is embarrassed about something. The correct Portuguese word is grávida, full stop. Memory trick: only Portuguese has the cedilla, and only Portuguese means embarrassment.

The first verb of the day is gebären, "to give birth," and it carries the German grammar trap of the day. Verbformen dot de lists haben as the auxiliary: ich habe geboren, du hast geboren, sie hat geboren. But if you say ich bin geboren, you're saying something completely different. That's not the perfect tense of an action — it's the passive voice of the child: ich bin neunzehnhundertsechsundachtzig geboren, "I was born in nineteen eighty-six." Same participle, two totally different roles, and only the auxiliary verb tells you which one is meant. Portuguese and Spanish sidestep the whole problem because they use two separate verbs: the mother deu à luz, the child nasceu.

For "to give birth," both Romance languages use the same image — literally "to give to the light" — and the same irregular verb, dar. In Portuguese: eu dou à luz, ela deu à luz. In Spanish: yo doy a luz, ella dio a luz. Things get interesting in the "we" row. Portuguese uses demos for both the perfect tense and the subjunctive — the two forms are identical. Spanish keeps them apart: nosotros dimos in the preterite, but nosotros demos in the subjunctive. One letter, and the two languages go their separate ways.

The second verb is erwarten, "to expect." German cleanly separates warten, to wait, erwarten, to expect, and hoffen, to hope. Portuguese and Spanish throw all three into a single verb, esperar. Espero o ônibus is waiting for the bus. Espero um bebê is expecting a baby. Espero que sim is hoping so. And only in that third meaning does esperar trigger the subjunctive: espero que dê tudo certo. One side note on German word formation: the prefix er- is unstressed and inseparable, so the participle is erwartet, with no ge- at all, unlike gewartet.

The third verb is stillen, and it's a textbook case of a word that just doesn't translate cleanly. Verbformen dot de gives three meanings: to calm a need, to breastfeed a child, and to make something stop flowing. German sees the same underlying motion in all three — bringing something to rest. Portuguese and Spanish need three separate verbs: amamentar or amamantar for the baby, matar a sede or quitar la sed for thirst, estancar or cortar la hemorragia for bleeding. In everyday speech, though, the colloquial forms matter more: in Brazil people usually say dar de mamar, in Mexico dar pecho.

On gender, today's worth a look at the endings. Portuguese a gravidez is feminine, Spanish el embarazo is masculine — same concept, opposite gender. The ending helps: Portuguese abstract nouns ending in e-z are feminine, Spanish nouns ending in a-z-o are masculine. With the diaper, it flips again, this time with no helpful rule: a fralda is feminine, el pañal is masculine. And each language uses its own image for the action: German wraps, Brazil swaps, Mexico changes.

Two final words German simply has no equivalent for. The first is o chá de bebê. Linguee tags it explicitly as Brazilian for "baby shower." The American "shower" became, in Brazil, a chá, a tea — the same logic as chá de panela. Mexico, by contrast, borrows the English term unchanged and just makes it masculine: el baby shower. The second is o enxoval. Priberam defines it as a set of clothing for a newborn, for a schoolkid, and so on. Spanish ajuar runs exactly parallel and also covers household goods and a bride's trousseau. German needs three words for that: Erstausstattung, Aussteuer, and Hausrat.

Today's idiom shows that every language has its own back door for this kind of news. German goes abstract: in anderen Umständen sein. Dict dot cc equates that with the English "to be expecting" and also lists "in the family way." Brazil does the opposite and points straight at the belly: estar de barriga, which Priberam defines as being in a state of pregnancy. For the home stretch, there's even a dedicated image, com a barriga à boca — almost ready to give birth. One honesty note on the Spanish line: no dictionary source could be tracked down today — the RAE returned error 403. So estar esperando is presented here only as common everyday speech.
