Friday, July 31 — rotation theme Travel & Traffic, eighth installment, and today's hook comes straight out of Minas Gerais: since Sunday, eight people have died in three serious crashes on the BR-116 highway, reports O Estado de Minas on July 30. School vacation is ending, the federal highways are packed, and for exactly this window the PRF has sent 300 drones over Brazil's busiest routes. That gives us today's word field: how to talk about speed limits, checkpoints, fines, and accidents in Portuguese and Spanish — including one word that comes from German and means something completely different in Brazil than it does here. 12 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, plus 3 refreshers from July 3 and July 10.

  • FALSE FRIEND der Blitzer ≠ a blitz — in German, a Blitzer is a speed camera; in Brazil, a blitz is a police checkpoint. And the word really is German: Priberam lists it as „palavra alemã Blitz, redução de Blitzkrieg". priberam.org
  • BR-PT a parada / o ponto ↔ a paragem — Brazil says parada or ponto de ônibus, Portugal says paragem de autocarro. A trap for German and English eyes: it has nothing to do with a parade. ciberduvidas.iscte-iul.pt
  • CONJUGATION ich bin verunglückt, aber ich habe überschritten — verunglücken takes sein in the perfect (change of state), überschreiten takes haben. Portuguese and Spanish don't have this problem. verbformen.de
  • PHRASE o pardal — Brazilian nickname for the fixed speed camera: "the sparrow," because the box sits on its pole like a bird perched on a wire. guiadoscuriosos.com.br
  • The hook: eight dead on the BR-116 since Sunday, in three serious crashes — a single head-on collision alone killed five people. From January to May, Minas Gerais counted 3,756 crashes on federal highways, 259 of them fatal. em.com.br
  • The number behind a multa: more than 50% over the limit costs R$ 880.41 plus seven points — R$ 2,641.23 on a repeat offense. Up to 20% over, it's R$ 130.16. radarnobrasil.com

Three Verbs Between Speed Limit and Crash

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br, and conjugator.reverso.net

haben or sein — the rule behind it: German perfect-tense forms need an auxiliary verb, and which one a verb takes isn't a matter of taste — it's a fixed property of the verb. Rule of thumb: verbs that describe an action — possibly with an object — take haben: ich habe das Tempolimit überschritten, die Polizei hat mich angehalten. Verbs that describe a change of state, something that happens to you, take sein: ich bin verunglückt. Portuguese and Spanish don't have this problem — there, ter and haber build every compound tense without exception, no matter what the verb means.

One Verb for Speed and Deadlines — Except in English German, Portuguese, and Spanish use the same verb for "driving too fast" and "missing a deadline": die Frist überschreiten, exceder o prazo, exceder el plazo. English splits this in two: you exceed a limit, but you miss a deadline. Anyone who says "exceed the deadline" in English will be understood, but it sounds translated.

The Tolerance Built into the Math Before a Brazilian speed camera even registers a violation, a measurement tolerance is deducted: 7 km/h up to 100 km/h, 7% above that. Only the remainder is measured against the limit and sorted into a fine tier. That's not a free pass, it's measurement technology — and the German word for it is built from two parts: speed + limitation, long since replaced in everyday speech by the shorter Tempolimit. radarnobrasil.com

The 2026 Table — Three Tiers, Three Prices Brazil grades the speeding fine not by km/h, but by percent over the limit: up to 20% over: R$ 130.16 and 4 points (média) · 20 to 50% over: R$ 195.23 and 5 points (grave) · more than 50% over: R$ 880.41 and 7 points (gravíssima), R$ 2,641.23 on a repeat offense. For comparison: the heaviest tier costs almost seven times as much as the lightest — that progression is the real deterrent mechanism. A German Word That Emigrated — and Changed Its Meaning To a German ear, a blitz is the most dangerous entry of the day. Priberam lists the word explicitly as a Germanism: „palavra alemã Blitz, redução de Blitzkrieg, guerra-relâmpago" — and defines the Brazilian meaning as „operação policial rápida e inesperada", a fast, unannounced police operation. It has nothing to do with taking photos: at a blitz, officers stand by the road and check drivers and their papers. The German Blitzer — the speed camera — is instead called radar or pardal in Portuguese. Anyone in Brazil who translates "I got flashed" literally is telling a completely different story than they think. priberam.org

The Stop That Isn't a Parade Two traps overlap here. First, the dialectal one: Ciberdúvidas explains that Brazilian parada refers to exactly what Portugal calls paragem — and that Brazil also says ponto ("ponto de táxi e de ônibus"), while the vehicle itself is called ônibus in Brazil and autocarro in Portugal. In everyday Ribeirão Claro you'll practically only hear ponto. Second, the false-friend trap: anyone thinking in German or English reads parada as a parade, a procession — but it actually means stopping. And there's a third layer, for the Mexican branch: in Mexico the city bus is colloquially el camión, which in Portuguese (o caminhão) means a truck. One sentence, three language regions, three different vehicles. ciberduvidas.iscte-iul.pt

Four Languages, Four Images Same idea, four completely different metaphors: German thinks legally, of the fresh act, English thinks of red hands (blood on the fingers), Spanish thinks of hands in the dough — someone caught sneaking a taste. Portuguese is the most matter-of-fact and borrows the Latin flagrante delicto directly, which in everyday speech has turned into the handy verb flagrar: „o radar me flagrou". You'll read that exact verb constantly in Brazilian traffic reports.

It's Friday, July thirty-first. Today's rotation theme is travel and traffic, the eighth installment — and this time it isn't about traveling itself, but about the rules of the road: speed limits, checkpoints, fines, and crashes.

The hook comes out of Minas Gerais. On the BR-116 highway, eight people have died in three serious crashes since Sunday, July twenty-sixth, according to the newspaper O Estado de Minas. The worst single case was a head-on collision between a small car and a van from the municipal health department of Rubim: five dead, six injured. This isn't a fluke week. From January to May, the state counted three thousand seven hundred fifty-six crashes on federal highways, two hundred fifty-nine of them fatal — an average of one point six traffic deaths a day.

Fittingly, the federal highway police sent three hundred drones over Brazil's busiest routes for the school-vacation period, as Correio Braziliense reported in early July. So anyone talking about the road these weeks is really talking about surveillance.

Today's vocabulary builds on three verbs. Überschreiten, "to exceed" — exceder in both Portuguese and Spanish, a fully regular verb in both languages, but strong and inseparable in German, so ich habe überschritten, with no ge in the participle. Verunglücken, "to crash" — acidentar-se in Portuguese, accidentarse in Spanish, with a double c. And, as a refresher from July third, anhalten, "to stop" — parar in both Portuguese and Spanish.

The most important conjugation rule of the day concerns the German auxiliary verb. Überschreiten takes haben, because it's an action with an object. Verunglücken takes sein, because something happens to you: ich bin verunglückt, never ich habe verunglückt. Portuguese and Spanish don't have this problem at all — there, ter and haber build every compound tense.

But the most interesting entry is a noun, and a German one at that. In Brazil, a roadside police checkpoint is called a blitz. The Priberam dictionary lists the word explicitly as a German import, a short form of Blitzkrieg. It just doesn't mean what a German would expect: the Blitzer, the speed camera itself, is called radar in Portuguese — or colloquially pardal, sparrow, because the box shape on its thin pole looks like a bird perched on a wire.

Then there are the numbers behind the word multa. Brazil grades the speeding fine by percent over the limit: up to twenty percent over, one hundred thirty point one six reais and four points; between twenty and fifty percent, one hundred ninety-five point two three reais and five points; over fifty percent, eight hundred eighty point four one reais and seven points. On a repeat offense, that becomes two thousand six hundred forty-one reais. Important context: these figures come from two matching fine-lookup portals; a cross-check against the legal text wasn't possible today.

Two more traps worth taking with you. First: a parada means a stop, not a parade — in everyday Brazilian speech people mostly say ponto de ônibus anyway, while Portugal says paragem de autocarro. Second: in Mexico, el camión is the city bus, while o caminhão in Portuguese means a truck. On top of that come the refreshers "driving licence" and "ID check" from July third and tenth, plus an idiom: to be caught red-handed, ser pego em flagrante in Portuguese.
