Three Verbs from Admission to Recovery

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br, and conjugator.reverso.net

Why the German participle looks different two ways today: The German perfect tense normally builds its participle with ge- up front: gemacht, gesagt, gekauft. Two groups break that rule — and both show up side by side today. First, separable verbs: with einweisen, the ge- wedges itself between the prefix and the stem, giving eingewiesen. Second, foreign verbs ending in -ieren: with operieren, the ge- simply disappears, and the participle is just operiert. Mental image: the ge- is like a piece of luggage. With separable verbs, it finds a spot in the middle; with -ieren verbs, it doesn't fit anywhere and stays home. Portuguese and Spanish don't have this problem — there, the participle always dutifully ends in -ado or -ido.

A German verb with three jobs — and a Portuguese one with just one Einweisen is broader in German than it looks: you admit someone to hospital, you induct a new colleague into their role (= train them in), and at the airport the person with the light wands marshals the aircraft in. Three situations, one word. Internar can't do that — it only means "to admit to a closed institution": hospital, clinic, boarding school. For training someone in, Portuguese needs treinar or orientar. In exchange, internar is more at home in everyday Brazilian Portuguese: ele está internado no longer needs a "no hospital." The matching noun is a internação — Priberam lists it as synonymous with the Portugal-standard internamento; in Brazil you'll practically only hear the -ção form: internação hospitalar. priberam.org

Three Prepositions for the Same Knee The surgeon operates, the patient gets operated on — all four languages push the patient into the passive voice. The trap sits in the preposition. German operates an ("on") a body part: am Knie operiert. Portuguese and Spanish take de ("of"): operada do joelho, operada de la rodilla — and use the same preposition for the condition instead of the body part too: operado de apendicite. English needs yet another word: operate on the knee. As for the noun: German says die Operation, colloquially die OP; Brazil almost always says a cirurgia — a operação sounds technical, even military; Mexico allows both la cirugía and la operación. And what you sleep through: die Narkose = a anestesia = la anestesia, general anesthesia = anestesia geral, local anesthesia = anestesia local. verbformen.de

Refresher from July 9 — from sore muscles to recovery On July 9, sich erholen came up in a sports context: recovering after training. Same verb, different register — today you're recovering from surgery. What's new is a look at where the reflexive pronoun sits: European Portuguese attaches it to the verb (recupero-me), Brazil puts it in front (me recuperei, ele se recuperou) and even opens whole sentences with it — "Me recuperei rápido" — something Portuguese school grammar doesn't love. A Station Where No Train Stops Duden lists several meanings for die Station — a stop, a notable point along a process, and explicitly "a ward of a hospital." In the clinical context, only the last one applies: auf Station liegen ("to be on the ward"), der Stationsarzt ("the ward physician"). Anyone thinking in English hears "station" and pictures a train platform — there, the ward is called ward. Portuguese and Spanish take a third path and don't call it a "station" at all: a enfermaria is the nursing ward — from the root enfermo ("the sick person") comes the whole word family at once: o enfermeiro / a enfermeira (the nurse), a enfermagem (nursing as a field). Alongside that stands a ala, the wing of the building; Spanish says la sala or el pabellón. And the ICU has its own abbreviation in each language region: Brazil says a UTI (Unidade de Terapia Intensiva), Portugal says a UCI (Unidade de Cuidados Intensivos) — one of the clearest BR/PT-PT dividing lines in hospital vocabulary. So in Ribeirão Claro: Ele está na UTI. duden.de · pt.wikipedia.org

One Word, Two Worlds: Wound and Association Duden lists der Verband both as a wound dressing ("einen Verband anlegen") and as an organization — federal association, trade association, military unit. In German that's the same word; Portuguese and Spanish split it cleanly: o curativo / el vendaje for the wound, a associação / la asociación (or a federação) for the organization. Priberam defines curativo as "aplicação periódica de cuidados e remédios a feridas" — so it describes the action more than the object, and that's exactly why Brazilians say fazer um curativo: to dress a wound. The plain material is called a atadura (gauze bandage) or a gaze. For German, remember the linking-s of the word family: der Verband, but das Verbandszeug (dressing kit) and die Verbandsschere (bandage scissors). duden.de · priberam.org

o laudo — a word you'll need everywhere in Brazil Priberam defines laudo quite broadly as "texto ou documento com um parecer técnico" — in other words, any document carrying a professional judgment. That's exactly why you'll run into the word far beyond medicine: laudo pericial, laudo técnico, laudo de vistoria for an apartment or vehicle inspection. Anyone buying a car or renting an apartment in Brazil will eventually be holding a laudo. German spreads the same idea across three words — medically, the findings; technically, the expert report; legally, the forensic report — Spanish does the same: el resultado for a lab value, el informe for a report, el dictamen for an expert opinion. And a bit of doctor jargon to take with you: "no findings" (o. B.) means "everything's fine" — Portuguese sem alterações, Spanish sin alteraciones. priberam.org

constipado — the trap with three layers First layer, English: constipated means one thing only — backed up in the bowels. Never having a cold. Second layer, Portuguese: Priberam lists constipação in the sense of "Inflamação catarral da membrana mucosa das fossas nasais" — that is, a cold — and marks this reading explicitly as the Portugal usage, with resfriado as the Brazilian equivalent. Alongside it sits the second, general-medical meaning "dificuldade em defecar," synonym prisão de ventre. In Brazil, it's mostly this second sense that applies: anyone in Ribeirão Claro who says estou constipado risks being understood as constipated — say estou resfriado or estou gripado instead. Third layer, Spanish: estar constipado means having a cold, but in a Spain-flavored way; in Mexico people say tengo gripa. The Academia Mexicana de la Lengua notes: "Gripe y gripa son voces correctas. La primera es propia del español de España y de la mayoría de los países de América, mientras que la segunda se emplea mayoritariamente en Colombia y México." Caveat: the RAE page for constiparse couldn't be pulled up directly today (dle.rae.es is blocking access), so the Spanish reading here rests only on a search-engine snippet. priberam.org · academia.org.mx

Discharge is planned for Friday if the blood values stay stable.

Na alta você recebe o relatório médico e o atestado.

el alta is feminine — despite the "el" Two lessons are packed into this one word. First, the semantics: German die Entlassung means both getting fired from a job and being discharged from the hospital — the same word for two very different days. Portuguese and Spanish keep these separate: a alta / el alta refers exclusively to a hospital discharge; getting fired is a demissão / el despido. Priberam defines alta as "permissão para o doente sair do hospital ou para terminar um tratamento"; the standard verb is dar alta (the doctor discharges) or receber alta / ter alta (the patient gets discharged) — "o médico já deu alta". Second, the Spanish grammar trap: alta is a feminine noun but requires the article form el because it begins with a stressed a- — the same rule as el agua or el área. The gender still stays feminine, so does the adjective agreement: the RAE makes it clear, "el sustantivo «alta» es femenino, por lo que lo indicado es decir «alta médica», «las altas»." Concretely: el alta médica, never *el alta médico, but in the plural, las altas. Portuguese doesn't have this rule at all — there it's simply a alta. priberam.org · rae.es/dpd

Refresher from July 5 — and a word you'll hear more often in Brazil On July 5, rehab came up in an employment-law context (being on sick leave, unable to work). Today it sits in its medical place, right after discharge. Linguistically, it's worth looking at the short forms: German shortens Rehabilitation to die Reha and treats it like an institution — you "go into rehab," "come into a rehab clinic." Anyone in Brazil talking about follow-up care after surgery is more likely to say fisio than reabilitação. Not confirmed: whether the Brazilian health system has a level institutionally comparable to the German rehab clinic is a systems question — it couldn't be cleanly confirmed today and is deliberately not claimed here.

Refresher from July 5 — three languages, three angles on the same piece of paper You know this word from the court/employment-law issue; today it sits in its natural spot, at the end of a hospital stay. What's interesting is what each language names. German turns the doctor's action into a noun: krankschreiben → die Krankschreibung; you get signed off sick — the doctor does it, the patient has it done to them. Portuguese names the document: o atestado, from "attest," to certify; in everyday speech people just say "o médico me deu atestado." Once you have the three angles in mind (action – document – condition), you won't mix them up again.

One Horsehair, Four Languages All four reach for the same image — and all four point back to the same ancient anecdote. The German Wiktionary entry for "an einem seidenen Faden hängen" points to the Sword of Damocles: a sword hung over the courtier's seat, held up by a single horsehair; the phrase means "to be acutely threatened by a concrete danger." German turned the horsehair into a silk thread, English into a thread, Portuguese into um fio — the Dicionário Informal describes estar por um fio as "estar em uma situação precária, instável." Spanish uses the verb pender (to hang, to dangle), hence pendiente. One difference in register is worth noting: the German idiom is almost exclusively dramatic — life, existence, a contract. The Brazilian por um fio is more everyday; "escapei por um fio" simply means "that was close." wiktionary.org · dicionarioinformal.com.br

It's Sunday, August second. Today's rotation theme is politics, law, and health, the seventh installment — and this time we're headed into the hospital. Not the emergency room: the emergency line, SAMU, and first aid were already covered back on June twenty-eighth. Today is about what comes after. Admission, the ward, surgery, findings, discharge.

The hook is a month old. In early July, Brazil's Ministry of Health announced a national mobilization of three point nine five billion reais for the public health system, the SUS. Six hundred sixty-eight point two million of that is earmarked for vehicles, including new SAMU ambulances for every state.

Worth flagging, and it's a small story in itself: those figures could only be checked today against a single mirror source, the newspaper Jornal de Brasília. The government's own site, gov dot b r, now shows only a restricted-content shell for the original article, and the state news agencies Agência Gov and Agência Brasil are completely offline — officially in compliance with election law. Brazil is heading into its October election, and the clampdown on government messaging during campaign season is very much being felt right now. So take the numbers as a hook, not as proof.

Today's vocabulary rests on three verbs. First, einweisen, "to admit" — internar in both Portuguese and Spanish. Second, operieren, "to operate" — operar in both. And third, as a refresher from July ninth, sich erholen, "to recover" — se recuperar in Portuguese, recuperarse in Spanish. The key grammar rule concerns the German past participle. Normally it starts with ge-; today two groups break that rule side by side. Separable verbs push the ge- into the middle: einweisen becomes eingewiesen. Foreign verbs ending in i-e-r-e-n drop it entirely: operieren becomes operiert, never geoperiert — the same pattern as studiert, telefoniert, reserviert.

The most useful entry for daily life in Ribeirão Claro is the verb internar. In Brazilian Portuguese it no longer needs a location tacked on. Ele está internado already means: he's in the hospital. The matching noun is a internação; Priberam treats it as synonymous with the Portugal-standard internamento, but in Brazil you'll practically only hear the -ção form. And one more Brazilian quirk from the same hallway: the intensive care unit is a UTI in Brazil, but a UCI in Portugal. So ask for the UTI.

Two traps worth taking with you. The first is the false friend constipado, and it has three layers. English "constipated" means only one thing — backed up in the bowels. Priberam lists the Portuguese constipação in the sense of a cold explicitly as a Portugal usage, naming resfriado as the Brazilian equivalent — alongside a second meaning, difficulty passing stool. In Brazil, it's mostly that second sense that applies. So say estou resfriado or estou gripado instead. In Mexican Spanish it's usually tengo gripa; the Academia Mexicana de la Lengua confirms that gripa is used mainly in Mexico and Colombia, while the rest of the Spanish-speaking world says gripe.

The second trap is grammatical, and it concerns discharge. In Portuguese it's a alta, in Spanish el alta. The "el" is misleading: the word is feminine and only takes the article el because it starts with a stressed a, exactly like el agua or el área. The RAE makes clear that alta is feminine — so it's el alta médica, but las altas in the plural. The matching verb is dar alta, the doctor discharges; the patient receives it, receber alta.

On top of that: a word with a double meaning — der Verband is, in German, both a wound dressing and an association, while Portuguese cleanly separates curativo from associação — the all-purpose Brazilian word o laudo for any kind of expert findings, and today's idiom: to hang by a thread, estar por um fio, pender de un hilo. All three trace back to the Sword of Damocles, which hung by nothing but a single horsehair.
