Three Verbs from the Committee Room

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br, and conjugator.reverso.net

Why two of these verbs split apart and one doesn't: German prefixes come in two kinds, and you can hear the difference. If the prefix is stressed — ÁBstimmen, ÁNheben — it's separable: in a main clause it tears itself loose and flies to the end of the sentence (ich stimme ab, ich hebe an), and in the participle the ge- wedges itself in between: abgestimmt, angehoben. If it's unstressed — verSCHÄRfen —, it's inseparable, welded on: ich verschärfe, participle with no ge- at all: verschärft. Mental image: the stressed prefix is a magnet that ends up stuck at the end of the sentence; the unstressed one is a weld seam. Portuguese and Spanish don't have this problem at all — there the verb stays in one piece.

One Sound, Two Fixes — and Why German Needs "Sharp" for It Endurecer literally means "to make hard" (from duro), and both Iberian languages run into the same problem in the 1st person singular: before an o, the c would suddenly sound like a k. Portuguese fixes that with the cedilla — eu endureço, and throughout the subjunctive: endureça, endureçamos, endureçam. Spanish inserts a z: yo endurezco, subjunctive endurezca, endurezcamos, endurezcan; Reverso files the verb under the parecer model. In practice that means: outside the 1st-person present and the subjunctive, both languages run almost identically — you only need to remember two spots. German builds its word from the opposite direction: not "to make hard," but "to make sharp," part of the die Schärfe family. Register: verschärfen fits for rules, laws, controls, and situations (die Lage verschärft sich), but not for screws — those you tighten with anziehen. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net

The Rare Double: Strong and Separable — Plus the Counterpart Anheben is the prefixed version of heben and inherits its ablaut series e–o–o: ich hebe an – ich hob an – ich habe angehoben. Verbformen.de also lists the archaic variant ich hub an — you'll hear that at most in elevated literature ("da hub er an zu reden"), while the business section always uses hob an. The subjunctive II ich höbe an is likewise literary; in everyday speech people say ich würde anheben. What matters today is the opposite pair anheben ↔ senken (PT elevar ↔ reduzir/cortar, ES subir ↔ bajar/recortar). That's exactly what this week hinges on: three Fed members wanted to raise rates on July 29, while the market expects the Copom to cut on Wednesday, from 14.25 to 14.00 percent — two central banks, opposite directions, one verb pair. Brazilian headlines often use the shorter subir for this ("o BC subiu os juros"). verbformen.de · tradingeconomics.com

Barrel, Brake, Skeleton — a Budget in Four Words

4 entries · from the oil barrel through Germany's spending brake to Brazil's fiscal rule

Primary surplus: The government takes in money (taxes) and spends it (salaries, pensions, roads); if more is left over at the end, that's a surplus. "Primary" means: before interest on old debt gets paid. Picture it this way: you go through your household budget but deliberately leave out the installments on your old car loan — whatever's left over then is your "primary surplus." It shows whether your day-to-day finances carry themselves, independent of legacy debt. Brazil's target for 2026: 0.25% of GDP, roughly R$ 34.3 billion.[1]

When the Measure Comes Out of the Pub Duden lists das Barrel as "a unit of volume used in Britain and the US" and, in the same breath, translates it itself as "Fass, Tonne" — for petroleum in the US, 158.9871 liters. German reports use both, though je Fass sounds more natural. Gender: das Fass, but o/el barril — in Portuguese and Spanish the oil barrel is masculine. German Builds a Machine, Brazil Builds a Room German thinks about budget discipline mechanically: you can attach -bremse ("brake") to almost anything and instantly understand it — die Schuldenbremse (the debt brake written into the Grundgesetz), die Preisbremse (price brake), die Notbremse (emergency brake). Hear Ausgabenbremse and you picture a vehicle rolling too fast. Portuguese and Spanish reach for a part of the house instead: o teto / el techo, the ceiling that spending bumps its head against — feminine for the brake, masculine for the roof. For Brazil that's more than a metaphor: the teto de gastos was the actual constitutional rule from 2016 to 2023, until the arcabouço fiscal replaced it (next entry). If "teto de gastos" still comes up today, it usually refers to the old rule. senado.leg.br

The Prettiest Word in the Brazilian Business Section Arcabouço isn't a technical term — it's a body word. Priberam's first definition is "conjunto dos ossos de um vertebrado," the bony skeleton of a vertebrate, followed by "estrutura de madeira de uma construção," the wooden frame of a building. So Brazil calls its fiscal rule, quite simply, "the skeleton" — German would reach for the dry Fiskalregel, Spanish for the sober regla fiscal. Anyone following the news here should keep an ear out for arcabouço: it comes up daily, and in this sense it's a homegrown Brazilian coinage from 2023. And the rule itself, stated correctly: it's not a flat 2.5-percent allowance, but a corridor. Spending growth is tied to 70% of revenue growth over the past twelve months, capped in real terms at no more than 2.5% and no less than 0.6% above inflation — the Senate describes exactly this range. So when people talk about tightening it, they mean the ceiling of that corridor, not a new law. priberam.org · senado.leg.br · camara.leg.br

One Latin Word, Three Stress Patterns — and a Counterpart Superávit comes straight from Latin ("it was left over") and traveled unchanged into both Iberian languages; the only thing that stands out is the accent mark Portuguese and Spanish have to add to keep the stress on the second syllable: superávit. Refresher from July 12 — One German Word Replacing Two Romance Ones On July 12, die Stimme came up in an election context; today shows why it earns a second appearance. German uses one word for two different things — the sound coming out of your throat and your right to cast a vote. Portuguese and Spanish split them cleanly: a voz / la voz is the sound, o voto / el voto the act of voting. Mix them up and you get "I cast my vote" → *dou minha voz, which means you're giving away your speaking voice. Correct: eu dou meu voto or eu voto. Still, the split isn't entirely clean — when a Brazilian says „ele tem voz ativa", they mean a say in the matter, so the German double meaning sneaks back in after all. Compounds to take with you: die Stimmabgabe (casting a vote), die Stimmenmehrheit (majority of votes), der Stimmzettel (ballot), stimmberechtigt (eligible to vote) — all built on Stimme, none of them about sound.

All Three Are Riding the Same Horse Duden defines der Zügel as "a leather strap attached to the snaffle or curb bit, used for steering or guiding the horse" and lists the set phrases: die Zügel straffer anziehen (to get stricter), die Zügel schleifen lassen (to get careless), die Zügel fest in der Hand haben (to keep a firm grip on things). Priberam describes rédea in parallel fashion — figuratively "direcção, poder, governo" — and documents tomar as rédeas as well as à rédea solta ("unbridled"); the everyday form apertar as rédeas, though, doesn't appear in that entry. Register difference: German die Zügel anziehen sounds like a boss, a state, or a strict household; apertar as rédeas you'll also hear at home ("o pai apertou as rédeas"). The reverse direction is a matching image in all three: die Zügel lockern = afrouxar as rédeas = aflojar las riendas. duden.de · priberam.org

It's Monday, August third. The rotation theme is economy and finance, episode eleven — and today's hook is close to home, straight out of that same day's economy brief. Oil prices dropped five point three one percent in a single day, from eighty-seven dollars ninety-three cents on July thirty-first to eighty-three dollars twenty-six cents per barrel. Oil is Brazil's biggest export, and the real weakened right along with it.

The euro costs five point eight five eight two reais this Monday morning. The European Central Bank's last reference rate is from Friday, at five point eight zero nine five — just under one percent lower. That's exactly where today's first language point comes in, an old friend and a false friend at the same time: aktuell, in Portuguese atual, in Spanish actual. All three mean current. English actual, though, means something else — real, genuine. The current rate is the live market price; the fix is the once-a-day reference value. Anyone converting a euro bill into reais should know which of the two they're quoting.

Three calendar events shape this week and hand us its three verbs. First, the Copom, Brazil's rate-setting committee, meets Tuesday and Wednesday; the Selic has stood at fourteen point two five percent since June seventeenth, and the market expects a cut to fourteen percent. Second, the US Federal Reserve held at three point five zero to three point seven five percent on July twenty-ninth — but the vote was nine to three. Hammack, Kashkari, and Logan wanted to raise it. And third, Brazil's treasury is weighing whether to tighten the spending rule.

That gives us the three verbs: abstimmen, verschärfen, anheben. The grammar rule behind them is one of the most useful in all of German. When the prefix is stressed — AB-stimmen, AN-heben — it's separable: in a main clause it tears itself loose and flies to the end of the sentence. Ich stimme ab. Ich hebe an. And in the participle, the ge- wedges itself right into the middle: abgestimmt, angehoben. When the prefix is unstressed, as in ver-SCHÄRfen, it's welded on, and the participle skips the ge- entirely: verschärft.

A word of warning about abstimmen: it has two jobs. Über etwas abstimmen means to vote — votar in both Portuguese and Spanish. But etwas mit jemandem abstimmen means to coordinate something with somebody — and there Portuguese switches to combinar or alinhar, Spanish to acordar. The preposition tells you which one you're dealing with.

The nicest conjugation moment today sits inside verschärfen, which is endurecer in both Portuguese and Spanish — literally, "to make hard." Both languages run into the exact same problem in the first-person singular: before an o, the c would suddenly sound like a k. Portuguese fixes it with a cedilla and writes eu endureço; Spanish inserts a z instead: yo endurezco. One problem, two solutions — everywhere else in the table, the two languages run almost identically.

Three nouns come straight out of the budget itself. First, das Fass, the barrel: Duden lists Barrel as a unit of volume and translates it right there as Fass or Tonne — for petroleum in the US, that's a hundred fifty-eight point nine nine liters. In Portuguese and Spanish it's o barril, masculine, plural barris. Then die Ausgabenbremse, the spending brake — German thinks about budget discipline mechanically, the same way it does with Schuldenbremse or Preisbremse, while Portuguese and Spanish reach for a part of the house instead: o teto de gastos, el techo de gasto, the ceiling.

And then the prettiest word in the Brazilian business pages: o arcabouço fiscal. Priberam defines arcabouço first as the bony skeleton of a vertebrate, and only after that as the wooden frame of a building. So Brazil calls its budget rule, quite literally, the skeleton. Worth getting right about the rule itself: it isn't a flat two-point-five-percent allowance, it's a corridor. Spending growth is tied to seventy percent of revenue growth over the past twelve months, capped in real terms at no more than two point five percent, and no less than zero point six percent above inflation. So when people talk about tightening it right now, they mean the ceiling of that corridor.

And to close, today's idiom, which fits everything that came before it: die Zügel anziehen, to tighten the reins. In Brazilian Portuguese, apertar as rédeas; in Spanish, apretar las riendas. Duden lists die Zügel straffer anziehen as the phrase for getting stricter, and Priberam describes rédea figuratively as direction, power, government. Three languages, one horse.
