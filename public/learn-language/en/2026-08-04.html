Vocab: Accessibility — kennzeichnen, leitor de tela, legenda

Tuesday, August 4 — rotation theme Software & Technology, eleventh installment, and this time it's the vocabulary you actually need while building your own pages: digital accessibility. The hook isn't a news flash but a deadline that's been running for about a year: Germany's Barrierefreiheitsstärkungsgesetz (Accessibility Strengthening Act) of July 16, 2021 — implementing EU Directive 2019/882 — took effect on June 28, 2025, and now binds e-commerce services, e-books, and consumer hardware to accessibility standards, among others. The technical yardstick behind it is even older and more concrete: the W3C's WCAG require a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text. These are exactly the words that go missing day to day between a ticket, a code review, and a client call — in German and in Portuguese. 12 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, plus 2 refreshers from July 14 and July 21.

  • CONJUGATION bedient, angepasst, gekennzeichnet — three German verbs, three different past participles. bedienen gets no ge- at all, anpassen wedges it into the middle, and kennzeichnen — despite being stressed on the first syllable — dutifully puts it up front. The reason isn't stress — it's word formation. More on that below. verbformen.de
  • FALSE FRIEND die Fabrik ≠ fabric — English fabric means Stoff (cloth), while German Fabrik means factory. Both trace back to Latin fabrica (workshop), but they drifted in opposite directions. Portuguese and Spanish stick with the German sense: a fábrica, la fábrica. duden.de · yourdailygerman.com
  • BR-PT leitor de tela, not leitor de ecrã — the screen reader is called leitor de tela in Brazil and leitor de ecrã in Portugal. The same fault line as celular/telemóvel. Spanish: lector de pantalla. pt.wikipedia.org
  • GENDER das Kontrastverhältnis, but a relação de contraste — German welds two nouns into a single neuter compound, while Portuguese and Spanish build a feminine phrase with de. You read it out differently too: "four point five to one" — quatro vírgula cinco para um — cuatro coma cinco a uno. w3c.br
  • BR-PT subtitles = legenda — in film, the Brazilian word for subtitle is a legenda, not o subtítulo. Priberam lists the caption under the same headword too: one Portuguese word covers two German ones. Spanish splits it again: los subtítulos vs. el pie de foto. priberam.org
  • PHRASE barrierefrei has its own web-specific meaning in the Duden — not just "step-free," but explicitly "(of websites) designed so that people with disabilities can use it without extra difficulty or outside help." A German word that picked up a second meaning on the web. duden.de

Three verbs, three different participles

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br and conjugator.reverso.net

Where the little "ge-" goes — and the exception nobody explains: German past participles are built with ge-: machen → gemacht. Once a prefix enters the picture, there are three cases. First, an unstressed prefix (be-, ver-, ent-): the ge- drops entirely — bedienen → bedient. Second, a stressed, separable prefix (an-, ab-, auf-): the ge- wedges into the middle — anpassen → angepasst. Third — and this is where stress alone doesn't help — verbs derived from a noun. kennzeichnen comes from das Kennzeichen (the marker/label), frühstücken from das Frühstück (breakfast). The whole word is the stem, it can't be split apart — and the ge- goes right up front as normal: gekennzeichnet, gefrühstückt. Memory hook: prefixes can be plucked off, a noun-derived verb is cast from a single piece. Portuguese and Spanish don't have this problem — there the participle always stays -ado/-ido.

The difference between "I adjust it" and "I adapt myself" Transitively, anpassen means "to adjust, to bring into line": I change a thing so it fits — ajustar in both Romance languages. Reflexively the meaning shifts: sich anpassen means "to fit in, to conform to a situation," and for that Portuguese and Spanish don't say ajustar-se but se adaptar and adaptarse — a gente se adapta, uno se adapta. The connotation is even slightly negative in German ("er hat sich zu sehr angepasst" = too little backbone), while in Portuguese se adaptar is neutral-to-positive. And watch the object: German adjusts something an something else (dative with an), Portuguese uses ajustar algo a algo, Spanish the same. verbformen.de · conjugacao.com.br

One German verb, three Romance ones — and a label that sticks Kennzeichnen covers two things that Portuguese and Spanish strictly separate. Sense one is "to mark, to tag with a feature" — that's rotular (PT, from rótulo, the label on a bottle) or etiquetar (ES, from la etiqueta). Five words that make a page operable

5 nouns · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · terms checked against the official WCAG translation by W3C Brazil

WCAG: the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines — the W3C's international rulebook for when a website is usable for people with visual, hearing, or motor impairments. Think of a building code: it doesn't dictate what your house looks like, but it does require the door to be wide enough for a wheelchair and the stairs to have a railing. WCAG does the same thing for the web — and it's pleasantly concrete: text must stand out from its background by at least a 4.5:1 ratio, every function must be reachable via keyboard, every image needs a text description.[1]
Noun GENDER DE m, pl. -e (everyday jargon: der Alt-Text) · EN the alt text · PT-BR o texto alternativo, m · ES-MX el texto alternativo, m
der Alternativtext

Ohne Alternativtext ist das Bild für den Screenreader stumm.

Der Alternativtext beschreibt, was das Bild zeigt — nicht, dass es ein Bild ist.

the alt text

Without alt text the image is mute to the screen reader.

Alt text describes what the image shows — not that it is an image.

o texto alternativo

Sem texto alternativo, a imagem fica muda para o leitor de tela.

O texto alternativo descreve o que a imagem mostra — não que ela é uma imagem.

el texto alternativo

Sin texto alternativo, la imagen queda muda para el lector de pantalla.

El texto alternativo describe lo que muestra la imagen, no que es una imagen.

One word against two — the German compound Here's a small example of what sets German apart from the Romance languages: Alternativ + Text get welded into a single word, and the gender comes from the last component — der Text, so der Alternativtext. Portuguese and Spanish keep two words and put the adjective after the noun: texto alternativo. That's the base rule for almost every technical term today. To the point: alt text is not an image title — "Photo" helps nobody; if an image is purely decorative, it needs an empty alt attribute so the screen reader skips it. The official Brazilian WCAG translation puts it tersely: "Texto alternativo (alt text) deve ser utilizado em imagens." w3c.br

tela or ecrã — the same fault line as celular/telemóvel Portuguese Wikipedia puts it plainly: "Para o português brasileiro especificamente, o termo utilizado é leitor de tela (em vez de leitor de ecrã, que é a versão do português europeu)." The difference isn't in leitor, it's in the screen: in Brazil it's a tela (feminine, originally "canvas"), in Portugal o ecrã (masculine, from French écran). Say ecrã in Ribeirão Claro and you'll instantly sound like you're from Lisbon. Spanish takes a third route: la pantalla, originally "lampshade." And German? Bildschirmleser is what the dictionaries say, but in an actual team conversation practically everyone says Screenreader. pt.wikipedia.org · userway.org

Para texto grande, basta uma relação de 3:1.

How to read a ratio out loud — three languages, three little words The number is the same everywhere, the connector isn't. German: "four point five to one." Portuguese: quatro vírgula cinco para um. Spanish: cuatro coma cinco a uno — the same a as in a soccer score, dos a uno. Mix up the connector and you'll still be understood, but you'll instantly sound translated. To the point: 4.5:1 is level AA for normal text, 3:1 applies to large text, and the strictest level AAA requires 7:1.[1] Grammatically it's worth looking at gender: German compresses Kontrast + Verhältnis into a single neuter (gender comes from the last component, das Verhältnis), while the Romance languages build a feminine phrase with de. On the side: o contraste and el contraste are masculine on their own — only the head noun relação/relación makes the whole phrase feminine. w3c.br · developer.mozilla.org

The single key and the whole board German distinguishes die Taste (the single key) from die Tastatur (the whole board) — both feminine. Portuguese and Spanish make the same distinction, but flip the gender for the whole board: a tecla / o teclado, la tecla / el teclado. The two-minute self-test: put the mouse aside, use only Tab, Enter, and the arrow keys. Wherever the focus outline disappears or you get stuck, the page is broken.

One Portuguese word for two German ones — and a trap on the bookshelf Priberam lists legenda under several senses: as "informação escrita que comenta ou ajuda a compreender, identificar ou interpretar uma imagem, um mapa" — that's the German Bildunterschrift, a caption — and as "texto que acompanha imagens de filme, programa, espectáculo […] para fazer a tradução do que é dito em língua estrangeira" — that's the German Untertitel, a subtitle. One word, two German concepts. Spanish, by contrast, splits it cleanly: los subtítulos for film, el pie de foto under an image. On a movie poster you'll see legendado for that — as opposed to dublado, dubbed. priberam.org

A German word that picked up a second meaning on the web The Duden lists barrierefrei in two separate senses. The first is architectural: "(of buildings, transit, other facilities) showing no barriers, obstacles, or similar." The second is explicitly digital: "(of websites) designed so that people with disabilities can use it without extra difficulty or outside help." Portuguese and Spanish get by with a single adjective — acessível, accesible — covering everything from a ramp to a website. Watch out with the negation: unzugänglich means something quite different when applied to a person, namely "closed off, aloof" (ein unzugänglicher Chef, an unapproachable boss). In technical writing, people therefore play it safe and say nicht barrierefrei. And the legal framework is no longer a nice-to-have: the Barrierefreiheitsstärkungsgesetz of July 16, 2021, Germany's implementation of Directive (EU) 2019/882, has applied since June 28, 2025 and covers e-commerce services, e-books, and consumer hardware, among others.[2] duden.de · gesetze-im-internet.de

Two words, one Latin ancestor, opposite directions The second example sentence holds both words at once: The fabric for the curtains comes from a factory. English fabric = der Stoff (PT o tecido, ES la tela); German Fabrik = factory. The Duden traces the origin: via French fabrique from Latin fabrica, "craft, workshop," from faber, "craftsman." English took that same root through "building," then "the weave a thing is made of," and finally "cloth" — the meaning migrated from the place of manufacture to the product of manufacture. Portuguese and Spanish stayed with the place. In software life: a product name like Fabric refers to the English weave, not the factory hall — and the German Fabrikmethode design pattern is the factory method in English. duden.de · yourdailygerman.com

The third English sense that German doesn't have On July 21, kritisch appeared here as the severity level of a security vulnerability. Today's refresher shows the second sense — etwas kritisch sehen, to view something skeptically — and one real trap. English critical has a third sense that German doesn't share: "essential, decisive." "It's critical that we ship on Friday" doesn't mean "es ist kritisch, dass wir Freitag liefern" — which would sound like a red alert — but rather "es ist entscheidend" (it's essential). Portuguese and Spanish behave like German here: for "essential" they say crucial or fundamental, not crítico. Rule of thumb: severity and skepticism → kritisch/crítico. Essentialness → entscheidend/crucial.

One word for the bus and for Jira — but only in German On July 14, das Ticket came up in a Scrum context. What was left open then: German uses the same word for both a transit fare and a bug-tracker item. Portuguese and Spanish separate the two. In Brazil, a bus fare is a passagem or o bilhete, a movie ticket o ingresso — and the support case is o chamado ("abrir um chamado") or, borrowed from English, o ticket. It's Tuesday, August fourth. The rotation theme is Software and Technology, eleventh installment — and this time it's about digital accessibility. That means the vocabulary you need when a website has to be not just pretty, but usable — for people who listen to a screen reader instead of looking at a screen, or who can't use a mouse.

The hook isn't breaking news but a deadline that's been running for a while now. Germany's Accessibility Strengthening Act of July sixteenth, twenty twenty-one implements the European directive twenty nineteen slash eight eighty-two, and it took effect on June twenty-eighth, twenty twenty-five. It covers e-commerce services, e-books, and consumer hardware, among other things. The technical benchmark behind it is even older and much more concrete: the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines from the W3C require a contrast ratio of at least four point five to one for normal text. Large text only needs three to one, and the strictest level requires seven to one.

Today's three verbs are bedienen, anpassen, and kennzeichnen — to operate, to adjust, and to label — and they were picked on purpose, because each one builds its participle differently. Bedienen has the unstressed prefix be-, which is welded on for good: the participle is bedient, with no ge- at all. Anpassen has the stressed, separable prefix an-: there the ge- wedges into the middle, angepasst. And kennzeichnen is the case that stress alone doesn't explain.

Kennzeichnen looks like it has a separable prefix, and the first syllable is stressed too. And yet it's inseparable, and the participle is gekennzeichnet, with ge- right up front. The reason: the verb is derived from a noun, from das Kennzeichen, the marker. The whole word is the stem, and it simply can't be split apart. Frühstücken works the same way, from das Frühstück, breakfast: ich habe gefrühstückt. Memory hook: a prefix can be plucked off, a noun-derived verb is cast from a single piece.

In Portuguese and Spanish, all three verbs are completely regular. Bedienen becomes operar in both languages, anpassen becomes ajustar in both, and kennzeichnen splits: Portuguese uses rotular, from rótulo, the label on a bottle, Spanish uses etiquetar, from la etiqueta. But watch out — kennzeichnen also means "to characterize." What characterizes an accessible page? For that, both languages say caracterizar, never rotular.

Among the nouns, the nicest Brazilian difference sits inside the word for screen reader. In Brazil it's leitor de tela; in European Portuguese it's leitor de ecrã instead. It's the same fault line as celular versus telemóvel: in Brazil the screen is a tela, feminine, originally meaning canvas; in Portugal it's o ecrã, masculine, borrowed from French. Spanish takes a third path with la pantalla. And in German, the dictionary says Bildschirmleser, but in real conversation practically everyone just says Screenreader.

Two small grammar observations carry a lot of weight. First: German compresses technical terms into a single word and takes the gender from the last component. Der Alternativtext is masculine because der Text is masculine. Das Kontrastverhältnis is neuter because das Verhältnis is neuter. Portuguese and Spanish keep two words and tack the adjective on afterward: texto alternativo. Second: you read a ratio out loud with a different little connecting word in each language. German says four point five to one, Portuguese quatro vírgula cinco para um, Spanish cuatro coma cinco a uno — the same a as in a soccer score, dos a uno.

The word for subtitle carries a nice little shift. Priberam lists legenda for both a photo caption and a film subtitle — one Portuguese word covers two German ones. Spanish splits it again: subtítulos in film, pie de foto under an image. And watch out on the bookshelf: the subtitle of a book is not legenda in Portuguese, it's subtítulo.

Today's false friend is die Fabrik. English fabric actually means Stoff, cloth, not factory. Both words trace back to the Latin fabrica, workshop, but they drifted in opposite directions: English moved from the place of manufacture to the woven material, while German stayed with the place. Portuguese and Spanish also stayed with the place: a fábrica and la fábrica. In everyday development that means: a product called Fabric refers to the English weave, not the factory floor — and the German Fabrikmethode design pattern is called the factory method in English.
