Vocab: Housework — staubsaugen, fazer faxina, lavar los trastes

Wednesday, August 5 — rotation theme Family & Everyday Life, twelfth installment, and this time it's the vocabulary that gets used every single day in a household and still never shows up in a language course: housework and how it gets divided. The hook isn't a news flash but a number that shapes the conversation at Brazilian kitchen tables: according to the IBGE's PNAD Contínua 2022, women spent an average of 21.3 hours per week on housework and caregiving, men 11.7 — a gap of 9.6 hours.[1] Anyone living here hears the words that go with it constantly: fazer faxina, lavar a louça, quem passa o aspirador hoje? So today, ten entries — 4 verbs with full conjugation, three nouns, and one adjective pair, plus 2 refreshers from July 29 and July 15.

  • CONJUGATION staubsaugen is inseparable — ich staubsauge, participle gestaubsaugt. Even though it looks exactly like abwaschen (ich wasche ab, abgewaschen). The reason: Staub isn't a prefix, it's a noun welded onto the verb. verbformen.de
  • PHRASE German packs the housework into the verb, Portuguese puts it next to it — staubsaugen = passar o aspirador de pó = pasar la aspiradora; abwaschen = lavar a louça = lavar los trastes. One word against three. Anyone looking for a one-word translation is looking in vain — you learn the pair.
  • FALSE FRIEND das Gift ≠ gift — the English word gift means Geschenk (present), the German word Gift means poison. Practically relevant right here: cleaning products are Gift, Portuguese veneno, and belong out of reach. thelocal.de
  • BR-PT a geladeira, not o frigorífico — Brazil names the fridge after the feeling you get opening it (gelado, ice-cold), Portugal after the machine's job. The same fault line as celular/telemóvel. palavrascomhistoria.com.br
  • GENDER el bote de basura (MX), not el cubo de basura (Spain) — and in Portuguese the gender switches too: der Mülleimer, but a lixeira. Three languages, three images for the same container. linguno.com
  • CONJUGATION abwaschen is strong — wäscht ab – wusch ab – hat abgewaschen, ablaut a–u–a plus umlaut in the 2nd and 3rd person singular. The subjunctive II sounds even more old-fashioned: ich wüsche ab. The only irregular verb of the day. verbformen.de
  • IDIOM „Ordnung ist das halbe Leben" — no fixed equivalent proverb could be confirmed in Portuguese or Spanish. The everyday equivalent is cada coisa no seu lugar / cada cosa en su lugar — an approximation, not a dictionary match. fremdwort.de

Four verbs, four household chores

4 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br, and conjugator.reverso.net

Why one German word turns into three in Portuguese: German builds a dedicated verb for every household chore — staubsaugen, abwaschen, aufräumen. The whole activity is packed into the word. Portuguese and Spanish do the opposite: they take one general verb (fazer, passar, lavar, arrumar) and attach the object that says which chore is meant — passar o aspirador, lavar a louça, fazer faxina. Picture it this way: German is a toolbox with its own dedicated wrench for every bolt; Portuguese is a screwdriver with swappable bits — the handle stays the same, the bit tells you what you're doing. In practice, that means: don't look for the one-word translation of staubsaugen — learn the verb-plus-object pair as a single unit.

Putzen, limpar — and the Saturday called faxina Putzen is broader in German than it looks: you don't just clean the apartment, you also putzen your teeth (sich die Zähne) and your nose (sich die Nase). That's exactly where Portuguese doesn't follow with limpar but switches verbs instead — escovar os dentes, assoar o nariz; Spanish lavarse los dientes, sonarse la nariz. Rule of thumb: surfaces → limpar, body → its own verb. In Brazil there's also a dedicated noun for the big weekly clean: a faxina, fazer faxina — in German, der Großputz. What's charming is what the dictionary drags along with it: Priberam lists faxina among other things as „Serviço de condução de rancho ou limpeza nas casernas" and faxineiro as „Aquele que tem serviço de faxina nos quartéis" — the word comes from the barracks. In everyday Brazilian life, a faxineira has long meant the cleaning lady who comes to the house, and o dia da faxina is Saturday morning. priberam.org · conjugacao.com.br

arrumar has a second life in Brazil If you file arrumar away as just "aufräumen" (tidy up), you'll miss half of what you hear. In Brazil, arrumar also simply means "get, manage, pull off": arrumar um emprego (find a job), arrumar tempo (free up time), arrumar confusão (stir up trouble). And reflexively it becomes something else entirely: vou me arrumar means "I'm getting ready, I'm getting dressed" — not "I'm tidying myself up." In Spanish, ordenar stays with tidying, but picks up the secondary meaning "to order, command" (el juez ordenó…); for plain tidying-away, Mexicans often say recoger — recoge tus cosas. By the way: the imperative in the first example sentence shows a genuine Brazilian quirk — spoken, it's the indicative form arruma, not the grammatically "correct" arrume. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net

Three words for doing the dishes — and in Mexico the dishes are called something else German offers several routes: abwaschen, spülen (Geschirr spülen), in the south abspülen, or as a noun, den Abwasch machen. Portuguese and Spanish absolutely need the object: lavar alone just means "to wash" — only lavar a louça turns it into doing the dishes, where a louça, like the German Geschirr, is a collective term. In Spanish, lavar los platos is the pan-Hispanic form; in Mexico, everyday speech says lavar los trastes, and trastes covers more than plates — glasses, cutlery, and pots all count. The dishwasher is a máquina de lavar louça (BR) or el lavavajillas (MX). spanishdict.com · conjugacao.com.br

The verb that looks separable — and isn't Verbformen.de is unambiguous: "'staubsaugen' is an inseparable verb. The prefix 'staub' always stays attached to the verb." So ich staubsauge, and the participle gestaubsaugt, with ge- right up front — even though the word seems to follow the same pattern as abwaschen. In everyday speech you hear passar o aspirador de pó more often anyway. verbformen.de · dicio.com.br

Four words, from the trash can to poison

4 new entries · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · regional variants checked against Brazilian and Mexican sources

How the three languages name household appliances: German welds activity and object into a single word — Staub+Sauger, Wasch+Maschine, Müll+Eimer, Kühl+Schrank — and always takes the gender from the last component: der Schrank → der Kühlschrank. Portuguese attaches a suffix instead. -eira builds the container: lixo (trash) → a lixeira, gelo (ice) → a geladeira. -dor builds the device that does something: aspirar → o aspirador. Spanish mostly uses the feminine -dora variant: la aspiradora, la lavadora. Picture it this way: German presses two words together, Portuguese sticks a tag onto the end. In practice: if you're looking for a Portuguese appliance word, try material plus -eira or verb plus -dor first — the hit rate is surprisingly high.
The word field around the cleaning schedule How housework gets divided isn't a side topic in Brazil — it's a measured quantity: in the IBGE's PNAD Contínua 2022, women spent an average of 21.3 hours per week on afazeres domésticos e cuidado de pessoas, men 11.7.[1] The vocabulary that goes with it: housework = o trabalho doméstico / as tarefas domésticas / las tareas domésticas; how tasks get split = a divisão das tarefas / el reparto de tareas; the cleaning schedule = a escala de limpeza / el calendario de limpieza. Afazeres domésticos is the official term, one almost nobody actually says day to day — what you hear instead is de quem é a vez hoje?, "whose turn is it today?"

In Mexico it's a bote, in Spain a cubo One of those cases where the standard Spanish vocabulary word is simply wrong for the target variant. Linguno puts it succinctly: "Cubo de basura is commonly used in Spain to refer to a garbage can" and "Bote de basura is often used in Mexico and other parts of Latin America." So for es-MX it's el bote de basura — cubo sounds instantly like Madrid. In Portuguese the gender switches too: der Mülleimer, but a lixeira (from o lixo plus the container suffix -eira). Important for actually saying it: you don't take out the lixeira, you take out o lixo — levar o lixo para fora. linguno.com

geladeira or frigorífico — two countries, two ways of thinking Palavras com História nails the difference: „No Brasil, o aparelho é geladeira. Em Portugal, é frigorífico." And explains why right away: „O Brasil partiu do efeito que se sente ao abrir a porta: o frio, o gelado. Portugal partiu da tarefa interna da máquina: produzir frio." So Brazil names the feeling, Portugal the function — and German, third, names the furniture: Kühl + Schrank (cooling cabinet). That's the same fault line as celular vs. telemóvel. In Mexican Spanish it's el refrigerador, shortened to el refri in everyday speech; the freezer compartment is o congelador or el congelador. And the suffix is the same as in lixeira: gelo becomes geladeira. palavrascomhistoria.com.br

The most consequential false friend in the cleaning cupboard The Local describes the effect very vividly: "if they use das Gift instead, they might see some facial expressions ranging from perplexed to shocked — indeed, this false friend means poison, toxin or venom." So if you want to bring a German colleague ein Gift, say ein Geschenk instead — Portuguese um presente, Mexican Spanish un regalo. For the household, the surrounding word chain matters too: giftig = tóxico / venenoso, das Reinigungsmittel = o produto de limpeza / el producto de limpieza, das Gegengift = o antídoto / el antídoto. And the word nobody wants to learn but everyone should know: o Centro de Informação e Assistência Toxicológica, Brazil's poison control hotline. thelocal.de

A German proverb with no confirmed counterpart "Ordnung ist das halbe Leben" (order is half of life) is one of those lines German parents say in their sleep. It's Wednesday, August fifth. The rotation theme is family and everyday life, twelfth installment — and this time it's about housework. That means the words that come up in a household every single day and still never show up in a language course: cleaning, tidying up, doing the dishes, vacuuming. And the question behind it: who actually does what.

The hook is a number that's been shaping the conversation at Brazilian kitchen tables for years. In the twenty twenty-two PNAD Contínua from the IBGE, women spent an average of twenty-one point three hours a week on housework and caregiving, men eleven point seven. That's a gap of nine point six hours a week. No newer survey was available at the time of writing, and the IBGE site itself wasn't directly reachable today — the figures come from the headline of the IBGE report and its reproduction on TV Brasil.

Today's most important observation isn't a vocabulary word, it's a construction principle. German packs every household chore into its own verb: staubsaugen, abwaschen, aufräumen. Portuguese and Spanish do exactly the opposite — they take a general verb and attach the object that says which chore is meant. Staubsaugen becomes passar o aspirador de pó. Abwaschen becomes lavar a louça. Picture it this way: German is a toolbox with its own wrench for every bolt, Portuguese is a screwdriver with swappable bits. In practice, that means: don't look for the one-word translation — learn the verb-and-object pair as a single unit.

Two verbs are worth a closer look for their conjugation today. Abwaschen is the only strong verb of the day: wäscht ab, wusch ab, hat abgewaschen. Umlaut in the second and third person singular, ablaut a-u-a in the simple past and participle. And the subjunctive II here is, for once, not identical in form to the simple past — it's umlauted instead: ich wüsche ab. That sounds bookish today; in speech, people say ich würde abwaschen.

The second is staubsaugen, and that's the real trap. It looks exactly like abwaschen, but according to verbformen.de it's an inseparable verb: ich staubsauge, not ich sauge staub. And the participle is gestaubsaugt, with ge- right up front. The reason is the same as with kennzeichnen from August fourth: Staub isn't a prefix, it's a noun welded onto the verb. There's also the two-word version Staub saugen — there, Staub is simply the object.

Arrumar deserves a second look, because the word has a double life in Brazil. It means to tidy up, but just as often to get or manage something: arrumar um emprego is to find a job, arrumar tempo is to free up time, arrumar confusão is to stir up trouble. And in the reflexive it becomes something else entirely — vou me arrumar means I'm getting ready, I'm getting dressed, not I'm tidying myself up.

Two regional traps really matter today. First, the fridge: in Brazil it's geladeira, in Portugal frigorífico. Palavras com História explains the difference nicely — Brazil named the feeling of opening the door, the cold, the icy chill; Portugal named the machine's job, namely producing cold. German, third, names the piece of furniture: Kühl plus Schrank. Second, the trash can: in Mexican Spanish it's el bote de basura, not el cubo de basura — cubo sounds instantly like Madrid. In Portuguese the gender switches too — der Mülleimer, but a lixeira. There's a useful rule hiding in there: the suffix -eira turns a material into its container. Lixo becomes lixeira, gelo becomes geladeira.

Today's false friend fits perfectly in the cleaning cupboard: das Gift. English gift means Geschenk, German Gift means poison. The Local puts it this way: use the word Gift instead of Geschenk, and you'll get facial expressions ranging from perplexed to shocked. Portuguese and Spanish both say veneno. For the household, the whole word chain belongs with it: giftig is tóxico or venenoso, das Reinigungsmittel is o produto de limpeza, and Brazil's poison control hotline is called Centro de Informação e Assistência Toxicológica.
