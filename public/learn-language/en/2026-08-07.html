Air Travel Vocabulary: einsteigen, despachar, documentar

Friday, August 7 — rotation theme Travel & Traffic, ninth installment, and today we finally head into the air: the last two Fridays stayed on the road (storms behind the wheel, then traffic rules and fines), today's vocabulary runs from the check-in counter to the baggage carousel. The hook is a piece of Brazilian law that's better known before you need it: under ANAC Resolução 400/2016, Art. 27, assistance during a flight delay isn't an airline's goodwill gesture but a duty on the clock — from 1 hour, means of communication; from 2 hours, meals; from 4 hours, hotel and transfer.[1] The word for it is assistência material; saying it at the counter means speaking the language of the regulation instead of the language of a request. Ten entries: 3 verbs with full conjugation, five new words from the counter to the exit, 2 refreshers from July 24 and 31.

  • PHRASE assistência material — the Brazilian legal term for "assistance during a delay." It's defined verbatim in the regulation and follows a schedule: superior a 1 (uma) hora: facilidades de comunicação · superior a 2 (duas) horas: alimentação · superior a 4 (quatro) horas: serviço de hospedagem … e traslado.[1]
  • CONJUGATION embarcar → eu embarquei, not *eu embarcei — before e, the c becomes qu, otherwise the hard c would turn into an s sound. Same rule in the subjunctive: que eu embarque. Identical in Spanish: embarqué.[2]
  • FALSE FRIEND éxito ≠ exit — Spanish el éxito and Portuguese o êxito mean success. The exit is la salida / a saída. Curious detail: both come from Latin exitus, "a going out" — English kept the door, Iberian Romance kept the outcome of a thing.[3]
  • PHRASE One motion, three verbs — checking your suitcase is Gepäck aufgeben in German, despachar a bagagem ("to dispatch it") in Brazil, and documentar el equipaje ("to document it") in Mexico. ANAC's official term for the result is bagagem despachada.[4]
  • BR-PT o ônibus, not o autocarro — and in Mexico people say el camión. That's exactly where the trap lies: el camión is the bus in Mexico, while o caminhão is the truck in Brazil. One letter apart, two different vehicles.[5]
  • CONJUGATION The flight delays itself — German requires the reflexive pronoun, Spanish does too (el vuelo se retrasó), Brazilian Portuguese doesn't: o voo atrasou. Three grammars for the same lost hour.
  • IDIOM voar baixo — literally "to fly low," but it means to speed: andar/correr em alta velocidade. On a flying-themed day, this is the nicest word of all, since the only flying it describes happens on the road.[6]

Three Verbs, One Flight

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br, and conjugator.reverso.net

Why each language has a different picture for checking a suitcase: The process is the same everywhere — set the suitcase down, weigh it, tag it, watch it disappear onto the belt. But each language picked out a different detail to name. German looks at the handover: you gibst the luggage auf, from now on someone else carries it. Brazilian Portuguese looks at the dispatch: despachar means "to send on its way" — the same word used for packages and freight. Mexican Spanish looks at the paperwork: documentar, "to furnish with a document" — you get the tag with the number, and that number is the suitcase. Picture it this way: the same baggage chute, three cameras — one filming the hands, one the belt, one the receipt. In practice: don't hunt for the one-word translation of aufgeben; instead learn the verb-object pair per language — Gepäck aufgeben, despachar a bagagem, documentar el equipaje.

einsteigen

Wir steigen in zwanzig Minuten ein — das Gate ist schon offen.

Gestern bin ich als Letzter eingestiegen und hatte keinen Platz mehr für den Koffer.

Steigen Sie bitte über den vorderen Eingang ein.

to board

We board in twenty minutes — the gate is already open.

Yesterday I was the last one to board and there was no space left for my suitcase.

Please board through the front door.

embarcar

A gente embarca em vinte minutos — o portão já está aberto.

Ontem eu embarquei por último e não sobrou espaço para a mala.

Por favor, embarque pela porta da frente.

abordar

Abordamos en veinte minutos — la puerta ya está abierta.

Ayer abordé al final y ya no quedaba espacio para la maleta.

Por favor, aborde por la puerta delantera.

One German verb for anything with wheels — three Romance verbs by vehicle type Einsteigen is comfortably broad in German: onto the bus, into the car, onto the train, onto the plane — always the same word. Portuguese and Spanish sort by vehicle. Embarcar originally belongs to the ship (barco) and today covers planes, ships, and formally intercity buses; for the city bus, Brazilians say entrar no ônibus or, colloquially, subir no ônibus. In Mexican Spanish, abordar is the airport word — it's built into pase de abordar and puerta de abordar — while Spain sticks with embarcar and tarjeta de embarque; for the bus, Mexicans say subirse al camión. Rule of thumb: if there's a terminal with a boarding pass, it's embarcar/abordar — otherwise entrar/subir.

aufgeben also means to give up — four meanings, four different verbs No word of the day is as overloaded in German: das Gepäck aufgeben (check it in), einen Brief aufgeben (mail it), eine Anzeige aufgeben (place it) — and nicht aufgeben! (don't give up). What's one verb in German is four in Portuguese: despachar a bagagem, postar uma carta, publicar um anúncio, desistir. Spanish does the same: documentar, enviar, poner un anuncio, rendirse. Translate "I give up" as despacho and you'll say something correct at the airport and something nonsensical in an argument. Regionally: Telediario México describes el equipaje documentado as "toda maleta u objeto que viaja en la zona de carga"; in Spain it's facturar el equipaje. In Brazil, despachar is also the official legal term — ANAC, in Art. 13 of Resolução 400, uses bagagem despachada and classifies its transport as a "contrato acessório oferecido pelo transportador," i.e., a separate side contract alongside the flight.[4]

Der Flug verspätet sich schon wieder.

Wenn ich mich verspäte, rufe ich dich an.

Wir haben uns wegen des Staus auf dem Weg zum Flughafen verspätet.

to be delayed

The flight is delayed again.

If I'm running late, I'll call you.

We were delayed by the traffic jam on the way to the airport.

atrasar

O voo atrasou de novo.

Se eu atrasar, eu te ligo.

A gente se atrasou por causa do congestionamento no caminho do aeroporto.

El vuelo se retrasó otra vez.

Si me retraso, te llamo.

Nos retrasamos por el tráfico rumbo al aeropuerto.

Who's actually delaying here — the person or the flight? The three languages distribute the reflexive pronoun differently, and that's exactly what gives away a learner. German always needs it: der Flug verspätet sich, ich verspäte mich — a bare "der Flug verspätet" doesn't exist. Mexican Spanish needs it for both, too: el vuelo se retrasó, me retrasé (also common: el vuelo se demoró). Brazilian Portuguese splits the two: for things, atrasar stands without a pronoun — o voo atrasou — while for people it's reflexive: eu me atrasei. Two side notes: Se eu atrasar isn't an infinitive but the Portuguese future subjunctive after se ("if"), which in German is simply present tense. And the noun next to it is on every departure board: die Verspätung = o atraso = el retraso — exactly the measurement the ANAC clock reads for assistência material.[1]

Five Words from the Counter to the Exit

3 nouns, 1 false friend, 1 idiom · gender and plural in the header

assistência material: Sounds like an insurance term, but it simply means: what the airline must give you while you wait. Picture it like a parking meter running backward, spitting something out at every click — except here the airline pays. Under Art. 27 of ANAC Resolução 400/2016: superior a 1 (uma) hora → facilidades de comunicação, meaning phone or internet; superior a 2 (duas) horas → alimentação, a meal or a voucher depending on the time of day; superior a 4 (quatro) horas → serviço de hospedagem, em caso de pernoite, e traslado, meaning hotel plus transport if an overnight stay is required.[1] For speaking at the counter, what matters is this: it's not a favor but an obligation — saying assistência material instead of "can you help me" is quoting the regulation. Also defined there: preterição, denying boarding to a passenger who showed up on time (Art. 22) — in English "denied boarding," colloquially overbooking.[4]

Card, card, permit Three languages, three images for the same slip of paper. German names the location: the card for being on board. Brazilian Portuguese names the process: cartão de embarque, the card for boarding. Mexican Spanish names the authorization: pase isn't a little card, it's a permit. Memory hook: in Mexico the noun matches the verb (abordar → pase de abordar → puerta de abordar); in Spain it matches the other verb (embarcar → tarjeta de embarque). Get the verb right and the noun follows automatically. Gender: die Bordkarte (f), o cartão (m), el pase (m) — German is the outlier.

A word you can't count — and three genders Handgepäck is a collective noun in the singular in German: even when it means a backpack, laptop bag, and jacket together, it's mein Handgepäck, never *meine Handgepäcke; individual pieces are die Gepäckstücke. A bagagem and el equipaje are likewise collective nouns, but each with its own gender: das Handgepäck (n), a bagagem (f), el equipaje (m). Three languages, three genders, one suitcase. In Brazil the pair is legally distinct: ANAC's Art. 14 of Resolução 400 defines bagagem de mão as "aquela transportada na cabine, sob a responsabilidade do passageiro" — in the cabin, passenger's own responsibility — while bagagem despachada falls under a separate contract per Art. 13. That distinction later determines liability.[4]

der Bus

The word that gives away where you learned Portuguese — and the trap behind it Hardly any everyday word splits Brazil and Portugal as sharply as this one: here o ônibus, there o autocarro. Priberam defines autocarro as "Grande veículo automóvel de transporte colectivo de passageiros, urbano, rodoviário ou turístico" and points to ónibus for the Brazilian variant — with a second fault line built in: Brazil spells it ônibus with a circumflex, Portugal ónibus with an acute accent.[5] The word comes from Latin omnibus, "for all" — the vehicle meant for everyone; German trimmed the same root down even further. And now the trap for anyone coming from Spanish: in Mexico, the everyday word for bus is el camión, according to one hypothesis, because the first city bus lines are said to have been converted trucks — though the Academia Mexicana de la Lengua itself doesn't consider this explanation fully settled.[5] In Brazil, by contrast, o caminhão is exclusively the truck. Say in Ribeirão Claro that you're taking the caminhão to Londrina, and you're announcing a ride on the flatbed. Practical notes: a rodoviária is the bus station, o ponto de ônibus the stop; in Mexico it's la central de autobuses and la parada.

éxito means success — and is nevertheless related to "exit" Today's false friend is so persistent because it's half true. Spanish el éxito and Portuguese o êxito mean success today, and in Spanish also "hit." Follow a sign reading éxito at the airport and you won't find a door. The shared origin is real, though: Priberam traces êxito back to Latin exitus — a going out — and still lists, as its first, now archaic meaning, "Saída, fim, termo," only afterward "Resultado feliz."[3] The journey of meaning: a going-out became the outcome of a thing, which became the result, which became the good result. English stopped at the door, Iberian Romance carried on all the way to applause. German kept both stations: der Ausgang is both the door and the result — der Ausgang der Wahl (the election's outcome). Two practical notes: for vehicles German says die Ausfahrt, for people der Ausgang, while saída/salida cover both. And at the airport, desembarque is the arrivals area, a saída the door to the outside.

The only language that flies while speeding Literally, voar baixo means "to fly low"; the image is a low-flying plane buzzing over the asphalt. But it refers exclusively to the ground: Dicionário informal describes the expression as "andar/correr em alta velocidade," slang used mainly by younger speakers.[6] On a flying-themed day, that's the nicest irony in today's vocabulary — the only flying in the sentence happens on the BR-153. German and Spanish stay obediently on the pedal: aufs Gas treten, pisar el acelerador. Portuguese also has the pedal image as a more sober alternative — pisar fundo, "to press down deep" — and the pair is worth knowing: you can say pisar fundo in front of your in-laws, voar baixo more among friends. Grammatically interesting in the second example: ele foi voando baixo — Brazilian Portuguese builds progressive forms with the gerund, where European Portuguese would say a voar.

From the storm to the road to the terminal On July 24, der Stau appeared in a storm context, between hydroplaning and braking distance; today it's the biggest risk between the front door and check-in. Brazilian word choice is inconsistent here: officially and in traffic reports it's o congestionamento, but in conversation almost everyone says o trânsito — even though that technically just means "traffic." Estou no trânsito therefore doesn't mean "I'm in traffic" but "I'm stuck in a traffic jam." Mexican Spanish makes the same shortcut: hay mucho tráfico; the more precise el embotellamiento is heard less often. Second refresher in the example sentence: "to miss the flight" is perder o voo / perder el vuelo — both use "to lose," German doesn't. Whoever does make it, pega o voo — "catches" it.

Penalty, fee, price — at the airport you rarely pay what you think On July 31, das Bußgeld was the state's sanction at the speed camera. At the airport, the same word looks different: what the airline charges for excess baggage isn't a penalty but a price — a taxa de excesso de bagagem, la tarifa por exceso de equipaje. German splits it into three tiers: das Bußgeld (state, sanction), die Gebühr (fee for a service), der Aufpreis (surcharge). Portuguese and Spanish keep multa strictly reserved for the sanction and use taxa/tarifa for anything you pay. Say multa at the counter and you're accusing the airline of punishing you — factually wrong and rarely helpful. It's Friday, August seventh. The rotation theme is travel and traffic, ninth installment — and today we head into the air for the first time. The last two Fridays stayed on the road: storms behind the wheel, then traffic rules and fines. Today the vocabulary runs from the check-in counter to the baggage carousel.

The hook is a piece of Brazilian law that's worth knowing before you need it. Under Article twenty-seven of ANAC's Resolução four hundred, assistance during a flight delay isn't an airline's goodwill gesture — it's an obligation on the clock. After one hour, the airline must provide a way to communicate, like a phone or internet access. After two hours, meals, either as food or as a voucher depending on the time of day. After four hours, a hotel and transfer if an overnight stay is required. The word for this is assistência material. Use it at the counter, and you're speaking the language of the regulation instead of the language of a request.

Today's key observation is, once again, about how words get built. An airport works the same way everywhere, but each language picked out a different detail to name. German focuses on the handover: you give up your luggage. Brazilian Portuguese focuses on the dispatch: despachar a bagagem, the same word used for packages and freight. Mexican Spanish focuses on the paperwork: documentar el equipaje, meaning to furnish it with a document. Same baggage chute, three different cameras. And in Spain, it's different again — facturar.

Among today's conjugations, the boarding verb is the most interesting. German einsteigen is strong and separable, and in the perfect tense it takes sein, not haben: ich bin eingestiegen. Verbformen dot de explicitly lists sein as the auxiliary. Portuguese embarcar looks regular but hides the classic spelling trap: in the first-person past, it's eu embarquei with a q-u, not embarcei — otherwise the hard c would turn into an s sound. In Spanish, the Mexican variant is worth knowing, too: the airport verb there is abordar, not embarcar. It's built into pase de abordar and puerta de abordar, while Spain sticks with embarcar and tarjeta de embarque. Get the verb right, and the noun follows automatically.

The second verb is a special case of German overload: aufgeben. You give up your luggage, you give up a letter by mailing it, you give up a classified ad by placing it — and you give up when you surrender. Four meanings, and in Portuguese, four different verbs: despachar, postar, publicar, desistir. Say "eu despacho" during an argument, and you'll say something correct at the airport and something completely nonsensical everywhere else.

The third verb shows a grammar mismatch that instantly gives away a learner. German always needs the reflexive pronoun: der Flug verspätet sich, the flight delays itself. Mexican Spanish, too: el vuelo se retrasó. Brazilian Portuguese splits the difference — for things, atrasar stands alone, o voo atrasou, but for people it becomes reflexive, eu me atrasei. And the noun next to it is on every departure board: die Verspätung, o atraso, el retraso. That's exactly the measurement the ANAC clock reads.

Today's false friend is half true, which is exactly why it's so persistent. Spanish éxito and Portuguese êxito mean success, not exit — that's la salida and a saída. But the words really are related: Priberam traces êxito back to Latin exitus, a going-out, and still lists, as its first and now archaic meaning, exactly that — saída, fim, termo. A going-out became the outcome of a thing, which became the result, which became the good result. English stopped at the door; Iberian Romance carried on all the way to applause. And German kept both: der Ausgang is both the door and the outcome, as in der Ausgang der Wahl, the outcome of the election.

Today's regional trap is the bus. In Brazil it's o ônibus, in Portugal o autocarro, and Priberam points explicitly to ónibus for the Brazilian spelling — with an acute accent instead of a circumflex, since even the spelling diverges. In Mexico, though, people say el camión — according to one unconfirmed hypothesis, because the first bus lines are said to have been converted trucks. And that's exactly where the confusion lurks: in Brazil, o caminhão is exclusively the truck. Say in Ribeirão Claro that you're taking the caminhão to Londrina, and you're announcing a ride on the flatbed.

Today's refreshers bring back two words from the road. The traffic jam from July twenty-fourth, back then in a storm context, is today the biggest risk between the front door and check-in — with the Brazilian quirk that almost everyone says trânsito, even though that technically just means traffic. And the fine from July thirty-first brings a useful distinction: what the airline charges for excess baggage isn't a multa, it's a taxa. A penalty and a price are two different things, and in both Portuguese and Spanish you hear the difference right away.
