Padaria Vocabulary: kneten, crescer, conservante

Saturday, August 8 — rotation theme Food, Cooking & Restaurant, ninth installment, and today we finally reach the counter that's the real town square in Brazil: the padaria. The last few Saturdays stayed at the weekly market (July 25), the street-food cart (July 18), and the home stove (July 4) — the bakery counter was still missing. The hook is a false friend more embarrassing than most: read sem preservativos on a label, and in everyday Brazilian usage you're reading "no condoms." Priberam actually lists both meanings under preservativo — the food-chemistry sense and the condom; the unambiguous word for the additive is conservante.[1] Ten entries: 3 verbs with full conjugation, five new words from the display case to the register, 2 refreshers from July 18 and 25.

  • FALSE FRIEND preservativo ≠ preservative — Priberam lists under preservativo the „substância química destinada a evitar o desenvolvimento de bactérias, geralmente em alimentos" and the latex condom. On the label and in conversation, the safe word is therefore conservante, which carries exactly the same chemical definition — and only that one.[1][2]
  • CONJUGATION crescer → eu cresço · crecer → yo crezco — as the dough rises, Portuguese and Spanish part ways in the 1st person: PT inserts a cedilla (cresço, que eu cresça), ES inserts a whole extra consonant (crezco, que yo crezca). Both are rescuing the same soft sibilant before the o.[7]
  • BR-PT pão de queijo — Priberam: „Bolinho salgado, geralmente pequeno e assado no forno, feito com polvilho, ovos, leite, manteiga ou óleo vegetal e queijo ralado." So cassava starch instead of wheat flour, naturally gluten-free from the start — and the plural is pães de queijo.[3]
  • PHRASE cafezinho — the coffee served in the small cup has a second, very Brazilian meaning: gorjeta, gratificação. Someone who "gives a cafezinho" is, depending on context, giving a tip — or something better left unreceipted.[4]
  • IDIOM no picnic · não ser sopa · no ser pan comido — the same claim ("this isn't easy"), three foods: sugar, soup, ready-made bread. And the polarity flips: German exists almost exclusively in the negative, while Portuguese and Spanish build outward from the positive case.[6]
  • GENDER la charola instead of la bandeja — the tray is called charola in Mexico (and Bolivia, Honduras, Peru). Second meaning, also Mexican: „credencial que identifica a una persona con autoridad" — the badge.[5]
  • PHRASE a comanda — the slip you get at the padaria's entrance, get stamped at the counter, and pay off at the register. Priberam explicitly flags the word as Brazilian: „Anotação do pedido dos clientes".[8]

Three Verbs, One Dough

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br, and conjugator.reverso.net

Why the dough does something different in every language: Physically, the same thing happens everywhere — the yeast feeds on sugar, releases gas, and the bubbles push the dough apart. But each language remembered a different picture of it. German sees a movement upward and outward: the dough geht auf ("opens up"), the way the sun geht auf, the way a seam geht auf, the way a bud geht auf. Portuguese and Spanish see a living thing: a massa cresce, la masa crece — the dough grows, the way a child grows. English sticks with height: the dough rises. Picture it this way: the same bowl, three captions — "the door opens," "the child gets bigger," "the water level rises." In practice: don't hunt for a one-word translation of aufgehen — learn the pair of verb and subject instead. And if you're ever unsure in everyday Mexican Spanish, just say la masa sube; leudar is bakery jargon and sounds like a spec sheet in the kitchen.

A German verb that opens up in far too many places Aufgehen is one of German's overloaded separable verbs: the sun geht auf, the door geht auf, a seam geht auf, a bud geht auf, the math geht auf, a light geht einem auf ("the penny drops") — and, of course, so does the dough. Portuguese and Spanish spread that across a whole set of separate verbs (nascer/salir for the sun, abrir(-se) for a door or a seam, bater/cuadrar for the math checking out) and reserve crescer/crecer for what grows bigger from the inside. Rule of thumb: if something grows bigger from within, crescer/crecer is right; if something opens up, you need a different verb.

Two verbs with too many jobs — and exactly one meaning in common Streichen and passar are both workhorses, but they overlap in exactly one place: the knife on the bread. German streichen also means to paint a wall (PT pintar) and to strike out a sentence, i.e. delete it (PT cortar, riscar). Portuguese passar, meanwhile, also means to iron (passar roupa), to stop by (passar aqui), to pass an exam (passar no vestibular), and in passar mal, even to feel sick. Mexican untar is narrower and stays with the greasy stuff — but it carries the secondary meaning to grease, in the sense of bribing someone (untarle la mano a alguien), which neatly resurfaces with today's cafezinho. In practice: in the kitchen, all three are interchangeable; outside it, almost never.

Five Words from the Display Case to the Register

1 false friend, 3 nouns, 1 idiom · gender and plural in the header

What a padaria is — and why the word "bakery" is misleading: A German bakery sells bread and closes around midday. A Brazilian padaria is a bakery, café, stand-up snack bar, and small grocery store all in one — it opens before six and is the place where half the neighborhood swings by for a moment each morning. The routine has its own mechanics: at the entrance you're often handed a comanda — Priberam defines the Brazilian word as „Anotação do pedido dos clientes" and „Papel onde essa anotação é feita" — you use it to collect everything you order at the balcão, and you only pay on your way out, at the caixa.[8] The padaria's sweet sister is called confeitaria — a patisserie — and its landmark item is the brigadeiro: according to Portuguese Wikipedia, named after Brigadeiro Eduardo Gomes's 1945 presidential campaign, made from leite condensado, chocolate em pó, manteiga and chocolate sprinkles.[9] Rule of thumb for ordering: padaria = savory plus coffee, confeitaria = sweet plus cake.
FALSE FRIEND Noun DE n, pl. - (die Konservierungsmittel) · EN the preservative · PT-BR o conservante, m, pl. -s · ES-MX el conservante, m (often conservador on Mexican labels)
das Konservierungsmittel

Dieses Brot hat keine Konservierungsmittel — deshalb ist es morgen schon hart.

Auf dem Etikett stehen drei Konservierungsmittel.

the preservative

This bread has no preservatives — that's why it's already hard tomorrow.

There are three preservatives listed on the label.

o conservante

Este pão não tem conservantes — por isso amanhã já está duro.

No rótulo estão listados três conservantes.

el conservante

Este pan no tiene conservantes — por eso mañana ya está duro.

En la etiqueta vienen tres conservantes.

The false friend that's twice right and still wrong The word preservative is exactly why English speakers — and Germans, who use the same loanword — reach for preservativo in Portuguese and Spanish. The catch: dictionary-wise, that's not even wrong. Priberam lists, as the third meaning under preservativo, „Substância química destinada a evitar o desenvolvimento de bactérias, geralmente em alimentos" — and, as the fourth, the „Envoltório de látex … que se coloca no pénis".[1] In spoken Brazilian everyday use, meaning four wins by a wide margin; in Spanish, per the RAE entry, it's essentially the noun's meaning, full stop.[2] The fix: conservante carries, word for word in Priberam, that same chemical definition — and no second one.[1] Rule of thumb for the ingredient list: sem conservantes is marketing copy, sem preservativos is a misunderstanding. In Mexico, labels often read sin conservadores instead — same thing, different suffix.[2]

the cheese ball (pão de queijo)

There's always a pão de queijo with the coffee here.

Six of them, please — fresh out of the oven.

o pão de queijo

Aqui com o café sempre tem pão de queijo.

Seis pães de queijo, por favor — quentinhos do forno.

el pan de queso

Aquí con el café siempre hay pan de queso.

Seis panes de queso, por favor — recién salidos del horno.

Not bread, not a roll — and a plural you have to memorize Translating it as "cheese bread" reliably gets you the wrong plate in Germany: there, that's a wheat roll topped with melted cheese. The pão de queijo is something else — Priberam describes it as „Bolinho salgado, geralmente pequeno e assado no forno, feito com polvilho, ovos, leite, manteiga ou óleo vegetal e queijo ralado".[3] The key ingredient is polvilho: cassava starch, not wheat flour — hence the chewy, hollow texture, and the fact that it's naturally gluten-free. Grammatically, it comes with the irregular plural that instantly gives learners away: pão → pães, so dois pães de queijo, never *dois pão de queijos. Same pattern: o cão → os cães, o alemão → os alemães.

The diminutive that has nothing to do with size -inho in Portuguese and -ito in Mexican Spanish rarely shrink anything for real — they make things friendly. Um cafezinho isn't a miniature espresso cup, it's an invitation to stay a while; um minutinho reliably takes ten minutes. And cafezinho has a second meaning in Brazil that's worth knowing before it catches you off guard: besides the drink, also gorjeta, gratificação — a tip, a small favor, and depending on tone, even the kind of payment better left off the books.[4] So if you hear that someone "gave a cafezinho," pay attention to the context. It's linguistically related to the Mexican untarle la mano a alguien — literally "to grease someone's hand" — from the verb entry next door.

The word that gives away a Mexican speaker — and the second meaning nobody expects Charola belongs in the same lineup as computadora, celular, carro, boleto, and manejar: the pan-Hispanic word is bandeja, but in Mexico people say charola. The Spanish Wiktionary defines it as „Pieza compuesta de una superficie plana, empleada para servir, mostrar, soportar o transportar cosas", flags the usage for Bolivia, Honduras, Mexico, and Peru, and lists bandeja as a synonym.[5] The second, purely Mexican meaning is a movie staple: „Credencial que identifica a una persona con autoridad" — the badge.[5] A small German trap on the side: das Tablett with two t's is the serving tray, das Tablet with one t is the device.
IDIOM Idiom DE used almost exclusively in the negative · PT-BR não ser sopa (positive: ser sopa = a piece of cake) · ES-MX (no) ser pan comido

The first few months of Portuguese were no picnic.

Los primeros meses de portugués no fueron pan comido.

Three foods for the same sentence — and a polarity that flips All three languages reach for food to say "this isn't easy," but each reaches for something different. German takes sugar: Wiktionary explains kein Zuckerschlecken sein as „nicht leicht zu bewerkstelligen sein; anstrengend, mühselig, schwierig, unangenehm sein" and dates the phrase to „ab 1900".[6] Spanish takes ready-made bread: per es.wiktionary, pan comido is „algo muy fácil de hacer o algo ya hecho y listo para usar; algo que no requiere ningún esfuerzo" — already eaten, so already done.[6] Brazilian Portuguese takes soup: ser sopa means "a piece of cake" (related: ser canja, ser mole) — não é sopa, accordingly, means "this is going to be tough."[6] The direction matters for active use: in German, the phrase is heard almost exclusively in the negative, while Portuguese and Spanish build outward from the positive case. English has an image for each side: a piece of cake for easy, no picnic for hard.

On July 18 at the lanchonete, today at the padaria — and a verb that replaces three German ones Same umbrella term, different counter: at the lanchonete, a salgado is lunch; at the padaria, it's what you have alongside your coffee. The key word here is the preparation, and it's worth a look at a verb that gives the padaria its very name: assar. Portuguese needs exactly one word where German has three — assar covers baking (bread), roasting (meat in the oven), and toasting; in Spanish that corresponds to hornear or asar. In practice, that means at the counter: salgado assado is the baked one, salgado frito the fried one. And Priberam's definition of pão de queijo — „assado no forno" — says exactly that about the cheese balls.[3]

Three slips of paper you shouldn't mix up On July 25, the topic was the receipt at the weekly market; at the padaria, the same act comes with three different pieces of paper at once. The comanda isn't a receipt, it's an order slip — Priberam: „Anotação do pedido dos clientes" or „Papel onde essa anotação é feita", explicitly flagged as Brazilian usage.[8] The cupom fiscal is the register receipt the till prints out. And the recibo is the receipt in the narrower sense: confirmation that payment was made. Someone who says a comanda, por favor at the register wants to pay; someone who says o recibo, por favor wants proof of it. In a Mexican store, the till slip is usually just called el ticket in everyday speech, while el recibo tends to be the more formal receipt.

It's Saturday, August eighth. The rotation theme is food, cooking, and restaurants, ninth installment — and today we're finally heading to the counter that functions as Brazil's real town square: the padaria. The last few Saturdays covered the weekly market, the street-food cart, and the home stove. The bakery counter was still missing.

Today's hook is a false friend that's more embarrassing than most. The English word preservative is exactly why English speakers — and Germans, who share the loanword — reach for preservativo in Portuguese and Spanish. Here's the catch: dictionary-wise, that's not even wrong. Priberam actually lists, under preservativo, the chemical substance that keeps bacteria out of food — and right after it, the latex condom. In spoken Brazilian everyday life, the second meaning wins by a mile. The safe word is conservante. It carries, word for word, that same chemical definition in Priberam — and no second one. Rule of thumb for the ingredient list: "sem conservantes" is marketing, "sem preservativos" is a misunderstanding.

But the most interesting story of the day is hiding in the dough. Physically, the same thing happens everywhere — yeast releases gas, and the bubbles push the dough apart. Yet every language remembered a different picture of it. German sees an upward motion: the dough opens up, the way the sun comes up, the way a seam comes undone. Portuguese and Spanish see a living thing: a massa cresce, la masa crece — the dough grows, the way a child grows. If you're ever unsure in everyday Mexican Spanish, just say la masa sube; leudar is bakery jargon and sounds like a spec sheet in the kitchen.

That very verb brings along today's conjugation trap, and Portuguese and Spanish solve it differently. Portuguese crescer slips in a cedilla in the first person: eu cresço, spelled c-cedilla-o, and in the subjunctive, que eu cresça. Spanish crecer inserts a whole extra consonant: yo crezco, que yo crezca. Both are protecting the same soft sibilant sound in front of that dark o, which would otherwise force a hard k sound. Say "eu cresco," and you'll sound like a learner immediately.

The German counterpart, aufgehen, is a verb with far too many jobs. The sun comes up, the door opens, a seam comes undone, the math checks out, a light bulb goes off over your head — and, of course, so does the dough. Portuguese and Spanish spread all of that across separate verbs of their own, and reserve crescer and crecer for what grows bigger from the inside. Rule of thumb: if it grows from within, crescer is right. If it opens up, you need a different verb.

The third verb is butter on bread. German streichen, Brazilian passar, Mexican untar. Here too, the words overlap in exactly one place. Streichen also means painting a wall or striking out a sentence. Passar also means ironing, stopping by, passing an exam, and in passar mal, even feeling sick. Untar stays with the greasy stuff, but carries the secondary meaning of greasing someone's palm — as in, bribing them.

And that brings us to today's nicest word. Cafezinho is coffee in a small cup, but the diminutive has nothing to do with size — it makes things friendly. Um cafezinho is an invitation to stick around for a bit, the same way um minutinho reliably takes ten minutes. But cafezinho has a second meaning in Brazil that's worth knowing before it catches you off guard: a tip, a small favor, and depending on the tone, even the kind of payment nobody talks about out loud. So if someone says they "gave a cafezinho," pay attention to the context.

Two more small words make the difference between watching and actually participating. First, the comanda: the slip you get at the entrance of the padaria, get stamped at the counter, and pay off at the register. Priberam explicitly flags the word as Brazilian and even cites the example sentence: did the customer lose their comanda? Second, the charola. In Mexico, a tray isn't called bandeja — it's a charola. And the very same word also names the badge a police officer flashes. And if you're wondering how hard all this is: in German, it's no picnic; in Portuguese, não é sopa; in Spanish, no es pan comido. Three foods, one sentence.
