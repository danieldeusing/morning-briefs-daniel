Vocab: Legislation — verabschieden, adiar, a jornada

Sunday, August 9 — rotation theme Politics, Law & Health, eighth installment. The last few Sundays were spent in the hospital (August 2) and at the party congress (July 26); today we go behind the scenes, into the machine room of lawmaking itself. The hook is the constitutional amendment Brazil has argued about more than anything else for months: PEC 221/2019, the "end of the escala 6x1." The Câmara dos Deputados waved it through in the second round on May 27, 2026 by 461 to 19 votes — the workweek drops from 44 to 40 hours, with no loss of pay, and two days off a week.[1][2] Since then it has sat in the Senate and gone nowhere: Senate President Davi Alcolumbre routed it through the committees instead of straight to the floor, and there's still no date set for a floor vote.[3] That's where today's vocabulary comes in: to pass a law, to reduce working hours, to postpone a decision. Nine entries: 3 verbs with full conjugation — to postpone among them not a new word, but a return visit from July 5, back then in the courtroom word field —, four new words from jornada to folga, 2 flagged refreshers from July 12 and July 20.

  • FALSE FRIEND jornada ≠ journey — the jornada de trabalho is your working hours, not a journey. Priberam lists both families under one roof: "Caminho que se anda num dia" and "Trabalho correspondente a um dia." Journey is viagem / viaje.[4]
  • CONJUGATION yo apruebo, but nosotros aprobamos — Spanish aprobar only diphthongizes under stress: apruebo, apruebas, aprueba, aprueban — the we-form stays flat. Portuguese doesn't make that jump at all: eu aprovo, nós aprovamos, flat throughout.[9]
  • CONJUGATION yo reduzco · yo reduje — reducir inserts a z in the I-form and has a strong preterite: reduje, redujo, redujeron, never *reducí. Portuguese behaves here (eu reduzi), but shortens the 3rd-person present to the bare stem instead: ele reduz.[9]
  • BR-PT a escala 6x1 — six days on duty, one day off. Priberam uses the same word for the shift roster ("Registo indicativo de horários, turnos…") and for a layover ("o avião faz escala em Barcelona").[6]
  • IDIOM engavetar · to shelve · congelar — the same parliamentary gesture three times, three images: Brazil pushes the matter into the gaveta, the drawer; German and Mexican Spanish freeze it instead.[7]
  • FALSE FRIEND assumir ≠ to assume — assumir means "to take upon oneself" and "to publicly own up to something" (Priberam: "Tomar sobre si ou como seu," "Encarregar-se de"). English to assume in the sense of "to suppose" is normally presumir — though the line isn't perfectly clean: Priberam's sense 5 for assumir also lists "Reconhecer como verdadeiro, possível ou hipotético."[8]
  • PHRASE a folga — Priberam explicitly marks three meanings as Brazilian: "Ausência de trabalho" (the day off), "Coisa fácil" (a piece of cake), and "Falta de vergonha" (nerve) — which is why que folga! isn't an invitation, it's a rebuke.[5]

Three Verbs, One Legislative Path

3 verbs with full conjugation — including to postpone as a repeat from July 5 · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br, and conjugator.reverso.net

What a PEC is — and why it takes so long: An ordinary law changes the rules of the game. A PEC — Proposta de Emenda à Constituição — changes the playing field itself, meaning the constitution. That's why a simple yes isn't enough: the text has to pass through both chambers of Congress — in Congress, each chamber is called a Casa, so the Câmara dos Deputados and the Senado Federal —, and in each one it's decided in two separate rounds (dois turnos, not a shift here, but a voting round) — hence the May 27 headline: "Câmara aprova em dois turnos," and only afterward "O texto segue para votação no Senado."[1] Picture it this way: an ordinary law is a house rule the landlord can change; a constitutional amendment is a load-bearing wall — for that, every owner has to sign off twice, and if anyone changes the blueprint, it goes back to square one. That's exactly where PEC 221/2019 stands: it's cleared the Câmara, but in the Senate, Senate President Davi Alcolumbre routed it through the committees instead of straight to the floor, and there's no floor date set. In August the Senate works in two esforços concentrados — concentrated sitting weeks — from August 10 to 14 and from August 31 to September 3.[3] Three verbs fully describe this path: to pass, to reduce, to postpone.

One German verb, two kinds of goodbye — and a false friend right next door In German, verabschieden does two jobs that Portuguese and Spanish split across two different verbs: Congress verabschiedet a law (aprovar / aprobar), and a guest verabschiedet sich, says goodbye (despedir-se / despedirse). Mix the two up and you accidentally say Congress said goodbye to the law — which, some days, would be the more fitting sentence. Memory hook: aprovar always means approval, even for an exam (fui aprovado no vestibular = "I passed the entrance exam").

Lurking right next to it is a false friend that trips up English speakers especially: assumir. Priberam gives "Tomar sobre si ou como seu," "Encarregar-se de," and, in the reflexive, "Aceitar, mostrar ou reconhecer publicamente determinado estado ou condição."[8] English to assume in the sense of "to suppose" is instead presumir: o deputado assumiu o compromisso means he committed to it — not that he assumed it. The line isn't airtight, though: Priberam's sense 5 for assumir also gives "Reconhecer como verdadeiro, possível ou hipotético," which does overlap with the English meaning;[8] the everyday case still stays "to take upon oneself" and "to publicly own up to something."

Two patterns that go far beyond this one verb Both languages treat the -duzir/-ducir family as a special case, but at different points — once you've got the pattern, you get half a dozen verbs for free. Portuguese cuts the ending in the 3rd person singular present: ele reduz, ele conduz, ele produz, ele traduz — never *reduze; everything else runs regularly. Spanish leaves the present tense alone but builds the I-form with a z (yo reduzco, like conozco) and has a strong preterite ending in -duje: reduje, redujiste, redujo, redujimos, redujeron — conducir → condujo, producir → produjo, and traducir → tradujo all follow the same curve. Along the way the ending loses its i: redujeron, not *redujieron. Anyone who says *reducí sounds like someone deriving their Spanish from Portuguese — exactly the mistake a second-language speaker is most likely to make.

The difference between "later" and "never" In fairness, upfront: this quartet — vertagen · to adjourn · adiar · aplazar — already ran in the brief on July 5, back then in the courtroom word field (the judge adjourns the hearing). What's new today isn't the word itself, but its parliamentary use and the line it draws against what comes next. Vertagen is the parliamentary word: you put the matter off to another day — that's literally built into it, ver-tagen ("day"). In everyday speech people are more likely to say verschieben (an appointment) or aufschieben (a tax return); only for sessions and decisions does vertagen sound natural. Portuguese uses adiar for all three, noun o adiamento. Mexican Spanish has two common words: aplazar (neutral) and posponer (more formal, with the irregular preterite pospuse, following the pattern of poner). The distinction that matters in the news: postponing (vertagen) something means it's coming back. Shelving it (engavetar) means it probably isn't — more on that below.

Four Words Between the Senate Floor and the Shift Roster

1 false friend, 2 nouns, 1 idiom · gender and plural in the header

What escala 6x1 actually means: The name sounds technical, but it refers to a very concrete weekly rhythm: six days on, one day off — rotating, so the day off moves through the week. Anyone working at a supermarket, a pharmacy, or a gas station never gets two days off in a row, and their day off only lands on a Sunday every few weeks. Picture it this way: a week as an egg carton with seven slots — exactly one slot is empty, and it moves one slot over every week. PEC 221/2019 wants to change that: the workweek drops from 44 to 40 hours, sem perda salarial — with no loss of pay — plus "duas folgas semanais, sendo uma preferencialmente aos domingos," meaning two days off a week, one of them preferably on a Sunday.[1] It would be phased in: 60 days after promulgation, first 42 hours, then twelve months later 40 hours, with an eight-hour daily cap.[1] That's why the three words in this block — jornada, folga, escala — are more practically important for everyday life than emenda: they show up in every employment contract and on every schedule pinned to the noticeboard.

A word that confuses the road with the workday — and why that's no accident German speakers don't stumble here, but English speakers do: journey and jornada come from the same root (Latin diurnus, "belonging to the day"), but they've drifted apart. Priberam lists both families side by side: meaning 1 "Caminho que se anda num dia," 2 "Viagem (por terra)" — and 4 "Trabalho correspondente a um dia," 5 "Salário de um dia"; plus 7 "Reunião, colóquio ou congresso" (the Jornadas de Direito do Trabalho are an academic conference, not a hike) and, in sports, the matchday.[4] In everyday Brazilian usage, the work meaning has won out: jornada on its own means working hours; anyone who means a trip says viagem or viaje. Memory hook: in Portuguese, "the day" has two children — the distance you cover in a day, and the work you get done in a day. An employment contract always means the second one.

The same word for the shift roster and the layover Escala parks several German concepts under one roof. Priberam's meaning 4 is our case: "Registo indicativo de horários, turnos ou vezes em que determinadas pessoas devem desempenhar certas funções (ex.: escala de serviço)" — the shift roster. Meaning 9 covers the layover, "o avião faz escala em Barcelona," and meaning 10 its duration, "temos uma escala de cinco horas."[6] On top of that there's the scale as a measurement or musical scale. So context decides everything: minha escala mudou is said by someone whose shift roster changed; meu voo tem escala em Guarulhos by someone who has to make a connection. The political catchphrase of the year, a escala 6x1, is the Brazilian term for the rhythm PEC 221/2019 wants to abolish — Mexican Spanish has no fixed formula for it; there it's rol de turnos or simply horario.

A friendly word with a very unfriendly second meaning At its core, folga means the gap: Priberam gives "Descanso," "Interrupção de trabalho," "Recreio," "Feriado," and — for the related verb — "Desapertar," meaning to loosen, the play in a piece of material. The dictionary explicitly marks three meanings as Brazilian, and they need to be kept apart: "Ausência de trabalho" is the day off (hoje é minha folga), "Coisa fácil" is a piece of cake, and "Falta de vergonha" is nerve — which is why que folga! doesn't mean "how relaxing," it means "what nerve," and a folgado isn't a relaxed person, it's someone who takes way too many liberties.[5] Practical for the schedule: estar de folga = to be off duty, tirar folga = to take a day off, folguista = the fill-in who covers other people's days off. The PEC text accordingly reads "duas folgas semanais, sendo uma preferencialmente aos domingos."[1]

Three languages, the same gesture, three images All three expressions describe the same parliamentary move: a bill isn't rejected — you could argue against a rejection — it's simply left lying around until nobody asks about it anymore. Brazil uses the gaveta, the drawer: engavetar, literally "to drawer." Dicionário Criativo defines the figurative meaning as "Impedir ou retardar o andamento de um processo." German and Mexican Spanish reach for the cold instead: DWDS lists the phrase "jmdn., etw. auf Eis legen," and in Mexico congelar una iniciativa is the standard way of saying a bill is stuck in committee.[7] English puts the paper on a shelf, to shelve. The subtle difference from vertagen above: something postponed gets a new date; something shelved gets no date at all. That's exactly what's going on with PEC 221/2019 — it hasn't officially been stopped, it just has no date.[3]

According to Agência Brasil, the change takes effect 60 days after promulgation — first at 42 weekly hours; only twelve months later comes the step down to 40 hours with an eight-hour daily cap.[1] Two grammar details: first, the auxiliary — treten is a verb of motion, so das Gesetz ist in Kraft getreten, never *hat. Second, the spelling: Kraft stays a capitalized noun here and the verb is written separately — in Kraft treten — only written as one word when it's the noun das Inkrafttreten. Portuguese and Spanish build the phrase identically (entrar em/en vigor); two neighboring words are worth knowing too: a promulgação is the promulgation, a vigência the period something stays in force.

The word that behaves like an adjective — and three kinds of representative On July 12, der/die Abgeordnete belonged to the election-campaign word field; today it delivers the numbers. Grammatically it's the trickiest noun type in German, because it isn't a real noun at all — it's an adjective turned into a noun, and it's declined like an adjective: der Abgeordnete, but ein Abgeordneter; plural die Abgeordneten, but viele Abgeordnete; genitive des Abgeordneten. Same family: der Angestellte (the employee), der Verwandte (the relative), der Deutsche (the German). Portuguese and Spanish make it simpler — deputado and diputado are ordinary nouns with a gender ending. Worth knowing culturally is the three-way split: the deputado federal sits in the Câmara dos Deputados in Brasília, the deputado estadual in the state's Assembleia Legislativa, and the vereador on the city council. The 461-to-19 from the second round are votes from deputados federais; in the first round it was 472 to 22.[1][2]

It's Sunday, August ninth. This week's theme is politics, law, and health — episode eight — and today we go behind the headlines, into the machinery of how a law actually gets made. The hook is the constitutional amendment Brazil has argued about more than anything else for months: PEC two twenty-one, the push to end the escala six-by-one.

Here's where things stand. The Câmara dos Deputados passed the bill in its second round on May twenty-seventh, four hundred sixty-one votes to nineteen — the first round had been four hundred seventy-two to twenty-two. Since then it's been sitting in the Senate, going nowhere. Senate President Davi Alcolumbre routed it through committees instead of sending it straight to the floor, and there's still no date set for a floor vote. The Senate spends August in two concentrated sitting weeks, from the tenth to the fourteenth and from the thirty-first through September third.

What's actually in the bill is simple enough. The workweek drops from forty-four to forty hours, with no cut in pay, and workers get two guaranteed days off a week, one of them ideally on a Sunday. It would phase in: forty-two hours starting sixty days after promulgation, then forty hours a year after that, with an eight-hour daily cap. And the escala six-by-one itself? Six days on, one day off — rotating, so that day off drifts through the week. Anyone working a supermarket or a gas station never gets two days off in a row.

Three verbs describe this whole process. First, to pass a law — in German, verabschieden does double duty: Congress passes a law, and a guest says goodbye. Portuguese and Spanish split that cleanly, aprovar and aprobar for the law, despedir-se and despedirse for the farewell. The trap is in Spanish: aprobar only diphthongizes under stress. Yo apruebo, tú apruebas, él aprueba, ustedes aprueban — but nosotros aprobamos, flat stem.

Second, to reduce, where the traps flip around. Portuguese cuts the third person singular down to the bare stem: ele reduz, ele conduz, ele produz. Spanish leaves the present tense alone but builds the I-form with a z — yo reduzco — and has a strong preterite ending in -duje: yo reduje, él redujo, ellos redujeron. Never reducí. Third, to postpone — strictly speaking a reunion: this trio already ran in the brief on July fifth, back then in a courtroom, today in parliament. Portuguese adiar, Spanish aplazar. With aplazar, the z turns into a c before e: yo aplacé, que yo aplace. Nothing changes when you say it, everything changes when you spell it.

But today's most important word is a false friend, and it trips up English speakers specifically. La jornada de trabalho is your working hours, not a journey. Priberam lists both word families side by side because they share the same root: the day. One branch is the distance you cover in a day; the other is the work you get done in a day. In everyday Brazilian usage, work won. If you mean a trip, you say viagem or viaje.

Two more small words for the noticeboard. Folga is the day off — but Priberam flags three meanings as specifically Brazilian, and one of them is nerve. Que folga doesn't mean how relaxing, it means what nerve. And escala is the shift roster — the same word an airplane uses for a layover. Minha escala mudou is said by someone whose schedule changed; meu voo tem escala em Guarulhos, by someone who has to make a connection.

Finally, the idiom that sums up the whole state of affairs. Brazil pushes a bill into a drawer: engavetar, from gaveta. German and Mexican Spanish freeze it instead — auf Eis legen, congelar. English puts it on a shelf, to shelve. The subtle difference from postponing: something postponed gets a new date; something shelved doesn't. Today's refreshers cover two words from July — to take effect, from the twentieth, which now carries the PEC's phase-in timeline, and member of parliament, from the twelfth, which delivers today's vote count.
