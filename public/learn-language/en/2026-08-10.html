Vocabulary Correction: korrigieren, corrijo, tarifaço

Monday, August 10 — rotation theme Economy & Finance, twelfth installment, and today's occasion is a correction of our own. The same day's economy brief walks back a tariff rate: instead of the 37.5% on Brazilian goods carried until now, only 25% is actually verifiable — from a Section 301 proceeding, effective since Jul 22, on roughly $24 billion in goods value.[1] The forced-labor surcharge that had inflated the number belongs to a separate proceeding. Two steady anchors alongside that: the Selic stands at 14.00% after the unanimous cut on Aug 5,[2] and EUR/BRL trades at 5.8739 Monday morning — 0.15% below Friday's last ECB fix of 5.8826. Out of that comes today's vocabulary field, the one you constantly need when translating economic news: 10 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, including 2 refreshers from July 20.

  • CONJUGATION korrigiert — without ge- Verbformen.de lists korrigieren as a regular verb, and the participle is korrigiert, not *gekorrigiert. The reason is pure stress: if the first syllable isn't stressed, you get no ge-. That holds for every verb ending in -ieren — one small rule that clears up a hundred words at once. verbformen.de
  • FALSE FRIEND der Konzern ≠ concern — Konzern is a corporate group, a conglomerate; the English concern means "worry" or "matter." Write "the Petrobras concern" and in English you've just said "the Petrobras worry." linguee.com
  • BR-PT o tarifaço — Brazil's business press built its own word for the US tariff wave: base tarifa, ending -aço, and there's your tariff sledgehammer. Not a dictionary entry, but living journalist's language — and Mexican Spanish does exactly the same thing with -azo. taxgroup.com.br
  • GENDER one Zinssenkung, three words — German squeezes Zins (interest) plus Senkung (cut) into one noun; Portuguese and Spanish need a word chain: o corte de juros, el recorte de tasas. And watch the lexicon: in Mexico the interest rate is la tasa de interés — only in Spain is it el tipo de interés.
  • CONJUGATION abgleichen is strong and separable — the perfect tense still takes haben Verbformen.de lists the ablaut series ei – i – i: ich gleiche ab, ich glich ab, ich habe abgeglichen. The temptation to guess sein for a strong verb misses here — abgleichen acts on an object, it isn't movement. verbformen.de
  • IDIOM über das Ziel hinausschießen — Duden literally explains the image with the archer who draws the bow too hard, so the arrow lands well past the target. That's exactly what happened with 37.5% instead of 25%. PT-BR says passar do ponto, ES-MX pasarse de la raya. duden.de

Three Verbs from the Correction Column

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br, and conjugator.reverso.net

Why it's korrigiert, not gekorrigiert: The German participle normally sticks a ge- onto the front — gesenkt, abgeglichen, gemacht. But only if the verb is stressed on its first syllable. Say them out loud: SEN-ken, ÁB-gleichen — first syllable, so ge-. Now try korri-GIE-ren: the stress sits at the back, the ge- drops away, and the participle is simply korrigiert. Picture it this way: the ge- is a sticker that only holds on a stressed opening syllable. The trick is worth more than it looks — it applies to every single verb ending in -ieren (studiert, kopiert, reserviert, kalkuliert) and to every verb with an unstressed prefix (verkauft, bestellt, erklärt). Portuguese and Spanish don't have this problem at all: corrigido / corregido.
Verb DE regular, participle without ge-: korrigiert, perfect with haben · PT-BR corrigir, spelling shift g → j in the 1st person · ES-MX corregir, stem shift e → i and g → j

One letter, two problem spots — and Spanish has both Portuguese and Spanish face the same sound problem: a g is soft before e/i, hard before o/a. To keep eu corrijo soft, the g turns into j — in both languages, in the 1st person singular and throughout the subjunctive. Spanish piles a second irregularity on top: the stem shift e → i under stress, as in pedir. Hence yo corrijo, tú corriges, él corrige, but nosotros corregimos — the unstressed stem stays with e. In the preterite the i resurfaces in the 3rd person (él corrigió), while Portuguese says corrigiu there instead. Rule of thumb: *corrigo and *corriga don't exist in either language. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net

senken ≠ sinken — the priciest one-letter mistake in economic German Verbformen.de describes senken as a transitive verb: "to bring into a lower position" — you always senken something, the rate, the price, your gaze. The similar-sounding sinken is intransitive and strong: there, the subject itself falls, with no object, no one causing it. The difference decides who takes the blame in the sentence: „Das Copom hat den Zins gesenkt" names a doer, „Der Zins ist gesunken" leaves it open. The forms diverge too — senken → senkte → hat gesenkt (weak, with haben) versus sinken → sank → ist gesunken (strong, with sein). Portuguese and Spanish solve this with two separate constructions: o banco baixa os juros versus os juros caem. Practical note: BR reports usually use cortar for the rate move (o Copom cortou a Selic) — baixar is the all-purpose neighbor, cortar the technical term. verbformen.de

A strong verb that still takes haben Three traps at once. First, the ablaut series: Verbformen.de lists ei – i – i, so ich gleiche ab, ich glich ab, ich habe abgeglichen, subjunctive II ich gliche ab. Second, the auxiliary: with strong verbs you instinctively reach for sein — but that only applies to movement and change of state (ich bin gefahren). Abgleichen acts on an object, so it's a haben verb: ich habe die Zahlen abgeglichen, never *ich bin abgeglichen. Third, the range of meaning: German bundles together three things that PT and ES split apart — reconciling accounts = conciliar; checking a single figure against a source = more likely conferir or cotejar; syncing schedules = combinar / acordar. When in doubt, conferir — the verb you hear at the register in Brazil ("confere aí"). verbformen.de

Zinssenkung, Konzern, Strafzoll — and a Brazilian Word Monster

4 entries · from the rate move through the false friend to the tariff sledgehammer

Rate cut: A central bank sets the rate at which banks borrow money from it — in Brazil this policy rate is called the Selic. When the bank lowers it, money gets cheaper for everyone: loans cost less, saving pays off less, so people spend more. Picture it this way: the policy rate is the tap at the building's main water line — when the central bank opens it further, more water reaches every apartment in the building, even people who never deal with the central bank directly. The price: if Brazilian assets pay less interest, the real becomes a bit less attractive to foreign capital. On August 5, the Selic was cut unanimously to 14.00%.[2]
Noun GENDER DE f, pl. -en · EN the rate cut · PT-BR o corte de juros, m · ES-MX el recorte de tasas, m
die Zinssenkung

Die Zinssenkung vom 5. August war einstimmig.

Nach der vierten Zinssenkung in Folge steht die Selic bei 14,00 Prozent.

the rate cut

The rate cut of 5 August was unanimous.

After the fourth cut in a row, the Selic stands at 14.00 percent.

o corte de juros

O corte de juros de 5 de agosto foi unânime.

Depois do quarto corte seguido, a Selic está em 14,00 por cento.

el recorte de tasas

El recorte de tasas del 5 de agosto fue unánime.

Después del cuarto recorte seguido, la Selic está en 14,00 por ciento.

One word against three — and a lexicon trap for Mexico German bolts Zins and Senkung together into a single noun; Portuguese and Spanish build a chain with de: corte de juros, recorte de tasas. And the order flips: German names what gets cut first, the Romance languages name the action first. Corte comes with the verb cortar — the BR press writes o Copom cortou a Selic, whereas German never says you "cut" the rate. And the lexicon trap: in Mexico and much of Latin America the interest rate is la tasa de interés, but in Spain it's el tipo de interés — you'll be understood everywhere, but you'll instantly sound like you're from Madrid. Memory aid: tipo de cambio is the exchange rate (everywhere), tasa de interés the interest rate (Latin America). riotimesonline.com

The false friend hiding in every other company email Konzern and concern look like twins and have nothing to do with each other. Linguee spells it out plainly: the German Konzern stands for "a group of companies or holding," while the English concern corresponds to the German Besorgnis or Angelegenheit. In practice: "We have a concern about the tariff" is a worry, not a corporate group. Conversely, you write Konzern in English as corporate group, parent company, or simply the Group — the last one is standard wording in annual reports. Romance languages are more relaxed about it: o/el grupo empresarial, also o conglomerado; in Mexico you'll often hear el corporativo, which also means the corporate headquarters. What keeps the mix-up alive: English concern has an old, now rare meaning of "business" (a going concern). For everyday text, though, concern = worry. linguee.com

Duden writes the motive right into the word Duden defines Strafzoll as "a duty [levied for the purpose of a trade boycott against a country, or over ethical concerns about a raw material or product] on the import of specific goods." Two details are worth noting. First, the bracket: Duden explicitly names ethical concerns as a second reason — precisely the category the forced-labor accusation falls into. Second, the closing phrase "specific goods": a punitive tariff is by definition selective, it hits a list of goods, not all trade. That's why the distinction between 25% on roughly $24 billion in goods value and a blanket surcharge on everything isn't hair-splitting — it's the whole difference.[1] Linguistically: German packs motive (Straf-) and object (-zoll) into one word, while PT and ES attach an adjective — with a gender trap: tarifa is feminine, arancel masculine. duden.de · taxfoundation.org

-aço: the ending that turns any noun into a headline Brazilian business desks don't need a new word — they tack -aço onto an existing one and instantly get "the thing, but big and painful." Tarifa becomes o tarifaço, and the tax-advisory trade press uses it completely matter-of-factly as a technical term: "O tarifaço de Trump ganha um novo e decisivo capítulo." Important: it's not a dictionary entry — you won't find tarifaço in any standard dictionary, but you'll find it in every other headline. Register: journalistic, slightly dramatizing, has no place in a contract. The same machine builds golaço (a stunner of a goal) and pacotaço (a hefty package of measures). Mexican Spanish does exactly the same thing with -azo — the best-known example is el gasolinazo for a gas-price spike. And the ending carries a judgment: whoever uses it isn't just saying "big," but "too big, and I don't like it." German makes do with compounds like Zoll-Keule ("tariff sledgehammer") or Preis-Hammer ("price hammer"). taxgroup.com.br

Zölle, Zwangsarbeit — and an Arrow That Flies Too Far

3 entries · two of them refreshers from July 20

The floor, not the ceiling: When two sources contradict each other — one says 25%, the other 50% — the temptation is strong to go with the higher number because it sounds more dramatic. That's exactly backwards. What's solid is the part both sources agree on: the floor. Picture it this way: you ask two neighbors how deep the river is. One says "at least hip-deep," the other "a good two meters." You wade in assuming hip-depth as the safe bet — not because the two meters is wrong, but because nobody has confirmed it yet. That's exactly how today's economy brief treats 25% as the documented floor and the 50% as an open, unreconciled claim.[1] Word pair to take away: the floor ↔ the ceiling, PT-BR o limite inferior ↔ superior (colloquially o piso ↔ o teto), ES-MX el límite inferior ↔ superior. And the collocation that matters when you're exchanging euros: to sit in the upper third — if the spot rate sits at around 71% of the monthly range, €100 gets you closer to R$ 587 than R$ 578. PT-BR estar no terço superior da faixa.

Duden even explains why the arrow flies too far Duden lists the phrase as „[weit] über das Ziel [hinaus]schießen" and hands you the explanation right along with it: colloquial for "to [far] exceed the limits of what's reasonable or permissible; based on the image of an archer who misses the target because he's drawn the bow too hard [tried too hard], and the arrow flies well past the target." So the archer doesn't fail from laziness but from overzealousness — exactly the kind of mistake you make when you add together two tariff proceedings that have nothing to do with each other. Grammatically, two stumbling blocks: the verb is separable and strong (ich schieße hinaus, ich schoss hinaus, ich bin hinausgeschossen) and takes sein, because it describes movement. And Duden's square brackets aren't decoration: both weit and hinaus can be dropped. The Romance images are different: passar do ponto comes from the kitchen (the sugar syrup has cooked past its point), pasarse de la raya from a line on the ground you're not supposed to cross. duden.de

Refresher from July 20 — one German word, two Romance ones On July 20, der Zoll appeared in a trade context; today shows why it earns a second appearance. German uses one word for two completely different things: the levy on an imported good and the agency that opens your suitcase at the airport. Portuguese and Spanish split them cleanly — a tarifa / o imposto de importação versus a alfândega, el arancel versus la aduana. It's Monday, August tenth. The rotation theme is economy and finance, twelfth installment — and today's occasion is a correction of our own. The economy brief of the same day walks back a tariff figure. Until now it listed a US tariff of thirty-seven point five percent on Brazilian goods. What's actually verifiable is a lot less: twenty-five percent from a trade proceeding, effective since the twenty-second of July, on roughly twenty-four billion dollars in goods value. The reason was an arithmetic error — two separate proceedings were added together as if they were one. That's exactly where today's vocabulary comes from: to correct, to lower, to reconcile.

The first verb hands us the most useful grammar rule of the day. The German participle is korrigiert, not gekorrigiert. The reason is pure stress. If you stress the first syllable, you get a ge-: gesenkt, abgeglichen, gemacht. If the stress shifts to the back, as in korri-gie-ren, the ge- drops away. That holds for every single verb ending in i-e-r-e-n: studiert, kopiert, reserviert, kalkuliert. One rule, a hundred words.

On the Romance side, the same verb has a different problem. Portuguese and Spanish both need to keep the g soft when an o follows. Both solve it the same way: the g becomes a j. So it's eu corrijo and yo corrijo, never corrigo. Spanish piles a second irregularity on top, the stem change from e to i under stress — hence corrijo, corriges, corrige, but nosotros corregimos.

The second verb is senken, and behind it hides the priciest one-letter mistake in economic German. Senken is weak and transitive: you always lower something — the rate, the price, your gaze. Sinken, by contrast, is strong and intransitive: there, the subject itself falls. Das Copom hat den Zins gesenkt names a doer. Der Zins ist gesunken leaves it open. And the forms diverge completely: senkte, hat gesenkt — versus sank, ist gesunken. The concrete occasion: the Selic was cut unanimously to fourteen point zero zero percent on the fifth of August, the fourth cut in a row.

The third verb is abgleichen, the most demanding one. It's strong, with the ablaut series ei, i, i: ich gleiche ab, ich glich ab, ich habe abgeglichen — and separable on top of that. The trap sits in the auxiliary verb: with strong verbs you instinctively reach for sein, but that only applies to movement and change of state. Abgleichen acts on an object, so: ich habe die Zahlen abgeglichen. Portuguese and Spanish split apart what German bundles together: reconciling accounts is conciliar, checking a single figure against a source is more likely conferir, syncing schedules is combinar.

Among the nouns, a classic false friend takes center stage. The German Konzern has nothing to do with the English concern. Konzern is a corporate group, a conglomerate; the English concern means worry or matter. Write "the Petrobras concern" and in English you've just said the Petrobras worry. The correct term is corporate group, or simply the Group. Romance languages keep it relaxed: o grupo empresarial, el grupo empresarial — in Mexico you'll often hear el corporativo.

Today's prettiest word comes from the Brazilian business press: o tarifaço. The ending a-ç-o attaches to an existing noun and turns it into the thing, but big and painful. Tarifa becomes tarifaço, the tariff sledgehammer. You won't find it in any standard dictionary, but you'll find it in every other headline. The same machine builds golaço for a stunner of a goal and pacotaço for a hefty package of measures, and Mexican Spanish does the same with a-z-o — the best-known example is el gasolinazo. Important: the ending carries a judgment. Using it doesn't just mean big, it means too big, and I don't like it.
