Vocabulary Git: zusammenführen, mesclar — and the apruebo Problem

Tuesday, August 11 — rotation theme Software & Technology, twelfth installment. Today is about the five words you use every workday and still can't say cleanly in any foreign language: Branch, Commit, Merge, Pull Request, Rollout. The appeal lies in how the official translations and spoken developer language diverge — GitHub's Portuguese docs write "Mesclar uma solicitação de pull," while in the office in Londrina everyone says "Pull Request"; the official Spanish Git book consistently translates with fusionar and rama, while GitHub's Spanish docs need five words for two English ones: solicitud de incorporación de cambios. Plus today's grammar haul: Spanish aprobar breaks open under stress (apruebo), Portuguese aprovar stays perfectly flat (aprovo) — same root, two paths. 11 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, plus 3 refreshers from July 21.

  • CONJUGATION aprovo vs. apruebo — Portuguese stays flat, Spanish diphthongizes under stress: apruebo, apruebas, aprueba, aprueban — but aprobamos, because there the stress shifts onto the ending. The most common beginner mistake is *apruebamos. reverso.net · conjugacao.com.br
  • CONJUGATION genehmigt with no ge-, zusammengeführt with ge- in the middle — not arbitrary, but stress: zusammenführen stresses the front and is separable, genehmigen stresses the middle and swallows the participle's ge- entirely. verbformen.de
  • FALSE FRIEND eventuell ≠ eventually — German eventuell means "possibly," English eventually means "in the end, at some point." Especially painful for developers: eventual consistency doesn't promise that the database might become consistent, but that it is, eventually. yourdailygerman.com
  • BR-PT deu pau — Brazilian for "gave up the ghost," explicitly listed in the dictionary for computer contexts: "quando alguma coisa para de funcionar" (when something stops working). Literally "there was wood." dicionarioinformal.com.br
  • GENDER der Branch, o branch — but la rama — whoever borrows the English word assigns gender by pattern (German monosyllables from English become masculine: der Chat, der Drink, der Sound). Whoever translates it inherits the gender of their own word — Spanish la rama, feminine. duden.de · docs.github.com
  • BR-PT "Pull Request" stays English — even though official translations exist: GitHub PT writes solicitação de pull, AWS PT-BR even solicitação de extração. Nobody says them in conversation. The flip side: in a contract or ticket text you need to recognize the official form. docs.aws.amazon.com · coodesh.com
  • PHRASE merge means something different three times over — GitHub PT mesclar (to mix), Git book ES fusionar (to fuse), German zusammenführen — the same word is in Familienzusammenführung (family reunification). Three languages, three images for the same process. docs.github.com · git-scm.com

Three Verbs: zusammenführen, genehmigen, ausrollen

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br and conjugator.reverso.net

Why "genehmigen" doesn't get a second ge-: The German participle is built with ge-: führen → geführt. But whether and where the ge- lands isn't decided by meaning — it's decided by the stress on the first syllable. Zusammenführen stresses the front, and the prefix is separable — so the ge- wedges itself into the middle: zusammengeführt (and in a sentence, the prefix flies to the end: ich führe die Zweige zusammen). Genehmigen stresses the second syllable; the first is an unstressed, permanently fused ge- from the adjective genehm. A second ge- would be a stutter — hence simply genehmigt. Rule of thumb: first syllable stressed → the ge- travels; first syllable unstressed → no ge- at all. And the Spanish o→ue spring: Picture the word stem as a little spring. When the stress lands on it, it snaps open: aprobar → yo apruebo, tú apruebas, él aprueba, ustedes aprueban. When the stress slides onto the ending, the spring stays closed: nosotros aprobamos. The exact same movement in the Subjuntivo: apruebe … aprobemos. And in the Pretérito, where everything is stressed at the back, no movement at all: aprobé, aprobó. Portuguese never built in this spring — aprovo and aprovamos both run flat throughout. Anyone switching from Portuguese to Spanish reliably produces *aprobo as a result; anyone trying too hard produces *apruebamos. Both are wrong forms.
Verb DE weak, separable (zusammen-) → past participle zusammengeführt, perfect with haben · PT-BR mesclar, regular -ar · ES-MX fusionar, regular -ar

Heute führen wir den Feature-Branch mit dem Hauptzweig zusammen.

Gestern führte der Kollege zwei Branches ohne einen einzigen Konflikt zusammen.

to merge

Today we are merging the feature branch into the main branch.

mesclar

Hoje nós mesclamos o branch de recurso com o principal.

One process, three images — mixing, fusing, merging Each language explains the merge with a different everyday image. Portuguese mesclar comes from mescla, the mixture — you stir two colors into each other; GitHub's Brazilian docs use exactly this verb: "Mesclar uma solicitação de pull quando as alterações propostas estiverem prontas." Spanish fusionar is the fusing — the same word used for a fusión de empresas, a corporate merger; the official Spanish Git book writes "La herramienta git merge se utiliza para fusionar uno o más ramas dentro de la rama que tienes activa." German zusammenführen is the image of bringing back together — it's the same word buried in Familienzusammenführung (family reunification). In spoken developer German, though, you almost always hear the English verb instead ("das mergen wir morgen" — we'll merge that tomorrow); zusammenführen is the word you need in documentation, tickets, and customer emails. docs.github.com · git-scm.com

The trap: *apruebamos doesn't exist Aprobar is the reason this verb made it into today's brief at all. Reverso confirms the shift in black and white: the o→ue alternation shows up in the singular forms and in the third-person plural — apruebo, apruebas, aprueba, aprueban — while nosotros keeps the plain o: aprobamos. Whoever treats the diphthongization as a property of the verb instead of a consequence of stress reliably ends up producing *apruebamos. Portuguese aprovar skips this whole dance and stays flat in every person. Second meaning, shared by both Romance languages but not by German: aprovar/aprobar also means to pass an exam — fui aprovado no vestibular, aprobé el examen. In German, an exam is never "genehmigt" — it's bestanden. reverso.net · conjugacao.com.br

Four Nouns, Four Gender Decisions

Branch · Commit · Pull Request · Merge-Konflikt — the words where every language decides differently whether to borrow or translate

Borrow or translate — and where the gender comes from: When a language adopts an English technical term, it has two options, and both automatically fix the gender. First, borrowing: the word arrives as-is, and gender gets assigned by pattern. In German, monosyllabic nominalizations from English verbs are mostly masculine — der Chat, der Drink, der Sound — hence also der Branch, der Commit, der Bug. Forms ending in -ing, by contrast, run neuter: das Timing, das Casting. Brazil borrows too, and masculinizes: GitHub's Portuguese docs write "o branch main padrão." Second, translating: then the new word simply inherits the gender of the native term. Spanish translates branch as la rama — the branch of a tree, feminine — and suddenly the same thing is feminine. The practical consequence for you: you have to know, per language, whether it borrows or translates before you pick the article. Guessing reliably lands you on "la branch" or "el rama."
GENDER Noun DE m, pl. -es/-s (loanword; German der Zweig) · EN the branch · PT-BR o branch, m (officially also o ramo, m / a ramificação, f) · ES-MX la rama, f
der Branch

Für jedes Ticket legen wir einen eigenen Branch an.

Der Hauptbranch heißt seit Jahren main, nicht mehr master.

la rama

Para cada ticket creamos una rama aparte.

Same thing, three genders Duden phrases the German rule this way: nominalizations from English verbs consisting of just one syllable are mostly masculine — der Chat, der Drink, der Sound. Branch fits exactly into this pattern, hence der Branch. Brazil likewise keeps the English word and makes it masculine; GitHub's Portuguese docs use both levels side by side: "Os branches permitem que você desenvolva recursos" alongside the translation "uma ramificação." Spanish translates consistently: la rama, both in the official Git book ("fusionar uno o más ramas dentro de la rama que tienes activa") and in GitHub's Spanish docs. Rule of thumb: where the English word stays, it's der/o; where it's translated, the native word decides — and rama is the branch of a tree, feminine. duden.de · docs.github.com · git-scm.com

Heads up: cometer is not to commit The English to commit originally means to pledge yourself, to commit to something — the commit is the moment you say: this state now counts. But the Latin-related cometer (PT) and cometer (ES) developed in a completely different direction: it means to commit [a crime/mistake], almost always with a negative object — cometer um erro, cometer un delito. A "cometer um commit" is therefore not wordplay, just plain wrong; in everyday speech both languages say fazer um commit / hacer un commit, or use the English verb directly (commitar, commitear). In German: committen, or — old-fashioned but correct — einchecken. And the pledging sense of the English original lives on in the noun commitment, which has nothing to do with version control.

Two registers: what people say and what's in the docs Hardly any term shows the gap between official translation and spoken language as clearly as this one. Officially, the Portuguese form absolutely exists — GitHub writes "Mesclar uma solicitação de pull quando as alterações propostas estiverem prontas," and AWS goes even further in its Brazilian CodeCommit docs with solicitação de extração. Yet Brazilian developers use the English term in everyday work regardless. Spanish takes the translation to the extreme: GitHub's Spanish docs consistently say solicitud de incorporación de cambios — five words for two. Rule of thumb: in conversation, in chat, and in ticket titles you use pull request; in a contract, a proposal, or a government document you need to recognize the long form and map it correctly. docs.github.com · docs.aws.amazon.com · docs.github.com/es
Noun DE m, pl. -e (hyphenated compound, gender from the last element: der Konflikt) · EN the merge conflict · PT-BR o conflito de merge, m · ES-MX el conflicto de fusión, m
der Merge-Konflikt

Der Merge-Konflikt steckt genau in der Zeile, die wir beide angefasst haben.

Lös den Konflikt lokal, bevor du wieder hochlädst.

the merge conflict

The merge conflict is in exactly the line we both touched.

Resolve the conflict locally before you push again.

o conflito de merge

O conflito de merge está justamente na linha que nós dois mexemos.

Resolva o conflito localmente antes de subir de novo.

el conflicto de fusión

El conflicto de fusión está justo en la línea que los dos tocamos.

Resuelve el conflicto localmente antes de volver a subirlo.

Hyphen versus de Here you can see, in miniature, the structural difference between the three languages. German welds the English loanword and the native noun together with a hyphen — Merge-Konflikt — and takes the gender from the last element: der Konflikt, hence der Merge-Konflikt. Portuguese and Spanish instead link with de: conflito de merge, conflicto de fusión. Spanish uses the translated core here (fusión, matching fusionar), while Brazil leaves the English word in place. When it comes to resolving it, all three agree: lösen, resolver, resolver.

FALSE FRIEND Adjective/Adverb DE eventuell = possibly, under certain circumstances · EN possibly / potentially — not eventually! · PT-BR possivelmente / talvez · ES-MX posiblemente / tal vez
eventuell

Eventuell müssen wir den Rollout verschieben.

Bring eventuell noch einen zweiten Reviewer mit.

Der Bug verschwand schließlich von selbst — nicht „eventuell".

possibly / potentially

We may possibly have to postpone the rollout.

Maybe bring a second reviewer along.

The bug eventually disappeared on its own — that is „schließlich".

possivelmente / talvez

Possivelmente teremos que adiar o lançamento.

Talvez traga um segundo revisor junto.

No fim das contas, o bug sumiu sozinho.

posiblemente / tal vez

Posiblemente tengamos que posponer el lanzamiento.

Tal vez trae a un segundo revisor.

Al final, el bug desapareció solo.

The classic — and why it hits developers twice The rule in one sentence: German eventuell is best translated as maybe, possible/possibly, or potential/potentially, while English eventual/eventually corresponds to the German words schließlich, letztendlich, or irgendwann. Both trace back to the Latin eventus, the outcome of a thing — German stayed with the uncertainty, English drifted toward the result. This gets expensive in technical jargon: eventual consistency doesn't promise that the distributed database might possibly become consistent — it promises that it's guaranteed to become so, eventually. The correct German rendering is schlussendliche Konsistenz ("final consistency"); read it as eventuelle Konsistenz instead and you've turned the guarantee into a guess. One extra trap: Portuguese and Spanish also have eventualmente, but with a third shade of meaning — there it carries an undertone of "occasionally, now and then." If you specifically mean "maybe," use talvez or tal vez. yourdailygerman.com

"Deu pau" — the most Brazilian way to say something's broken The informal dictionary describes dar pau as an expression for something that stops working, and explicitly names the computer context: when a program fails or doesn't run properly. Pau is the stick, the piece of wood — the phrase paints the image of a stick jammed into the spokes. What matters is the construction: the element that gets conjugated is dar, not pau. Hence deu pau (Pretérito), vai dar pau (future), and after se it's se der pau — one of the rare spots where the Brazilian Futuro do Subjuntivo genuinely shows up in everyday speech. Watch the register: dar pau is colloquial; in an incident report you'd write o sistema falhou or caiu. In Mexican Spanish the everyday equivalent is colgarse (the machine hangs/freezes) — and if you want it cruder, se tronó. dicionarioinformal.com.br

The rotation theme is software and technology, twelfth installment — and this time it's about your Git workflow, the words you type every single workday: branch, commit, merge, pull request, rollout. Eleven entries today, three of them verbs with full conjugation, plus three refreshers from July twenty-first.

The thread running through today's issue is a simple observation: for these terms, the official translations and the way developers actually talk have drifted apart. GitHub's Brazilian documentation properly writes mesclar uma solicitação de pull. In the office, everyone just says pull request anyway. And the Spanish version takes it to the extreme: there, a pull request becomes solicitud de incorporación de cambios — five words for two.

Today's key conjugation is a pair that reads like a textbook example. To approve is aprovar in Portuguese, aprobar in Spanish. Same root, same meaning — but Spanish breaks open under stress: yo apruebo, tú apruebas, él aprueba, ustedes aprueban. Portuguese stays perfectly flat: eu aprovo, você aprova, eles aprovam.

The trick behind this isn't vocabulary, it's physics. Picture the word stem as a tiny spring. When the stress lands on it, it snaps open, and the o becomes ue. When the stress slides onto the ending, the spring stays closed. That's exactly why it's nosotros aprobamos, with a plain o — and never apruebamos. The subjunctive makes the same move: apruebe, but aprobemos. And in the preterite, where the stress always falls at the back anyway, nothing happens at all: aprobé, aprobó.

The second grammar piece today is German. Zusammenführen and genehmigen build their participles differently, and once again the reason is stress. Zusammenführen stresses the first syllable, and the prefix is separable — so the ge- wedges itself into the middle, zusammengeführt, and in a sentence zusammen flies to the end: ich führe die Zweige zusammen. Genehmigen stresses the second syllable; the ge- at the front is permanently fused and comes from the adjective genehm. A second ge- would just be a stutter, so it's simply genehmigt.

When it comes to merging, every language pictures something different. Portuguese mesclar comes from mescla, a mixture — you stir two colors together. Spanish fusionar is fusing, the same word used for a corporate merger. And the German zusammenführen is the image of bringing things back together; it's the same word buried inside Familienzusammenführung, family reunification. Three languages, three metaphors for the same process.

With the nouns, it all comes down to one question: does the language borrow the English word, or translate it? If it borrows, gender gets assigned by pattern. In German, one-syllable English nominalizations mostly turn masculine — der Chat, der Drink, der Sound — so also der Branch and der Commit. Brazil borrows too, and says o branch. Spanish, on the other hand, translates, and inherits the gender of its own word: la rama, the branch of a tree, feminine. Same thing, two different genders.

There's a trap hiding inside commit. The English to commit means to pledge yourself — a commit is the moment you say: this state now counts. The Portuguese and Spanish cometer sounds related but means to commit a crime, almost always with a negative object: cometer um erro, cometer un delito. So people say fazer um commit or hacer un commit — never cometer.

Today's false friend is eventuell. German eventuell means possibly; English eventually means in the end, or at some point. Both trace back to the Latin eventus, the outcome of a thing — German stuck with the uncertainty, English drifted toward the result. For developers, this gets genuinely expensive in one specific spot: eventual consistency doesn't promise that a database might possibly become consistent — it promises that it's guaranteed to, eventually.

And to close out, today's most Brazilian phrase: dar pau. The informal dictionary describes it as an expression for something that stops working, explicitly including computers. Pau is a stick, a piece of wood. What gets conjugated is dar, not pau: deu pau, vai dar pau, and after se it's se der pau — colloquial, though; in an incident report you'd write o sistema caiu.
