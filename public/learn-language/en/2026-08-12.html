Vocabulary "Kid Is Sick": Taking a Temperature, tossir, constipado

Wednesday, August 12 — rotation theme Family & Everyday Life, thirteenth installment. Today the calendar isn't picking the topic, the situation is: Fiocruz's InfoGripe bulletin for epidemiological week 30 still keeps Paraná in the alert-and-risk band, and within the four-week window, 54.1% of positive SRAG cases (severe respiratory illness) trace back to RSV — the pathogen group that hits infants hardest. With a one-month-old baby in the house, that's not news, it's a packing list. So here are the words you need this exact week: taking a temperature, coughing, pediatrician, prescription, home remedy — plus today's three grammar hauls: Portuguese tossir, which only breaks in the I-form (eu tusso); the verb to measure, irregular in all three languages and irregular in a different spot each time, and the Spanish false friend constipado, which means "having a cold," not what English speakers assume. 10 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, plus 2 refreshers from July 2 and July 29.

  • FALSE FRIEND constipado ≠ constipated — the RAE simply defines constipado as catarro or resfriado: saying "estoy constipado" in Spanish means you have a cold. English constipated (bowel blockage) is estreñido in Spanish. And in BR Portuguese, constipado is ambiguous — better to say resfriado there instead. dle.rae.es · dicio.com.br
  • CONJUGATION eu tusso, but você tosse — tossir raises the stem vowel o→u exactly where the ending brings an -o or the subjunctive's -a: eu tusso, que eu tussa, que nós tussamos — everywhere else it stays tosse, tossiu, tossia. Spanish toser doesn't make this jump: yo toso, flat throughout. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net
  • CONJUGATION to measure breaks three different ways — German in the second and third person (du misst) and in the past tense (maß, gemessen); Portuguese only in the I-form and the subjunctive (eu meço, que eu meça); Spanish under stress and in the third-person preterite (yo mido, él midió). Same word medir, two different break points. verbformen.de · conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net
  • BR-PT estar de molho — Brazilian for "being laid up, staying in bed"; the informal dictionary translates the phrase directly as "doente; acamado" (sick; bedridden). Literally it means "being soaked" — the image comes from the kitchen, where beans sit in water overnight. German and Spanish instead reach for the image of standing guard: das Bett hüten, guardar cama. dicionarioinformal.com.br
  • PHRASE fever: three languages, three verbs — German you have a fever, Spanish you also have one (tiene fiebre), and in Brazilian everyday speech you are with one: estar com febre. That same formula carries half the sick-visit vocabulary: estou com tosse, estou com dor de garganta, está com febre.
  • BR-PT gripe is not resfriado — Brazil cleanly separates what German lumps together: gripe is the flu (sudden onset, high fever, body aches), resfriado the mild, slow-building cold caused by rhinoviruses; sneezing and coriza point to resfriado. The German "grippaler Infekt" is precisely not the flu. bvsms.saude.gov.br
  • GENDER o pediatra / a pediatra — a word ending in -a that still carries both genders; only the article changes. German instead builds a whole new word (Kinderarzt → Kinderärztin), umlaut and all. linguee.de

Three Verbs: husten, messen, verschreiben

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br and conjugator.reverso.net

Why a verb "breaks" at all: Irregularity is almost never arbitrary — it's an old pronunciation shortcut that froze into a rule. Picture the word stem as clay squeezed from behind by the ending: where the ending attaches a bright -o, it pushes the stem vowel up with it — toss- becomes tuss- (eu tusso), med- becomes meç- (eu meço). Where the ending is unstressed, the stem stays put: você tosse, nós medimos. That's why the I-form is the single most important form to learn — in Portuguese it decides the entire subjunctive: once you know eu tusso, you automatically know que eu tussa, que nós tussamos. The subjunctive is built from the I-form stem, not from the infinitive. In German, the break sits somewhere else, namely in the second and third person singular: ich messe, but du misst, er misst — the same shift as geben → du gibst. Today's rule of thumb: Portuguese breaks in the I-form, German breaks in the you-form, Spanish breaks under stress.
Verb DE weak, stem ends in -t → linking -e (du hustest), perfect with haben · PT-BR tossir, irregular: o→u in the I-form and throughout the subjunctive · ES-MX toser, fully regular -er verb

The trap: eu tusso, but você tosse The conjugator explicitly lists tossir as irregular — and the irregularity sits in exactly two spots: the I-form of the present (eu tusso) and the entire present subjunctive (que eu tussa … que nós tussamos). Everywhere else the o stays put: você tosse, eu tossi, eu tossia. Same family as dormir → eu durmo and vestir → eu visto. Spanish filed toser as a plain -er verb and doesn't make the jump at all: yo toso, nosotros tosemos. Anyone coming from Spanish reliably produces *eu tosso in Portuguese — wrong. And there's a gender shift hiding in the same word field: der Husten is masculine in German, while a tosse and la tos are feminine. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net

One verb, three break points — and a collocation trap This is today's most instructive verb, because it's irregular in all three languages — and irregular in a different spot each time. German: strong verb, the stem vowel lifts to i in the second and third person (du misst, er misst), the past tense runs through ablaut: maß, gemessen. Portuguese: the break sits exclusively in the I-form, where d turns into ç (eu meço) — and because the subjunctive is built from exactly that stem, it carries the change all the way through: que eu meça, que nós meçamos. Everything else stays flat: você mede, nós medimos, eu medi. Spanish: same word medir, but the shift is e→i and shows up under stress (yo mido, but nosotros medimos) plus in the third-person preterite (él midió, ellos midieron). Remember the three I-forms as a trio: ich messe · eu meço · yo mido. Collocation: German Fieber messen, Portuguese medir a febre / a temperatura — in everyday Mexican Spanish, though, you'll mostly hear tomar la temperatura, just like in English. verbformen.de · conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net

No second ge- — and why "recipe" splits in two in English Verschreiben is a textbook case for the German participle rule: the prefix ver- is inseparable and unstressed, so the participle gets no ge- at all — it's verschrieben, never *geverschrieben. The stem is strong and runs through the ablaut ei–ie–ie, like the whole schreiben family (bleiben–blieb–geblieben). Bonus second meaning: sich verschreiben means "to make a slip of the pen" — in German, ver- often marks a failure (sich verlaufen, sich versprechen). The nicest asymmetry sits in the noun: German Rezept, Portuguese a receita, and Spanish la receta all cover both meanings — medical prescription and cooking recipe. Only English splits it into prescription and recipe. So if you hear "a receita" in Brazil, context alone tells you whether it's headed for the pharmacy or the kitchen. verbformen.de · conjugacao.com.br

Four Words for the Pediatrician Visit

Fever · pediatrician · home remedy · cold — the vocabulary from thermometer to pharmacy

Why "I have a fever" needs three different verbs in three languages: German treats symptoms as possession — you have a fever, the same way you have a bicycle. Spanish does exactly the same: tiene fiebre. Brazilian Portuguese treats them as a temporary state and builds them with estar com, literally "to be with": estou com febre, estou com tosse, o bebê está com o nariz entupido. That's logical, since estar is already the verb for everything temporary — estou cansado (tired right now) versus sou cansativo (permanently exhausting to be around). In practice that means: if you want just one formula ready in the waiting room, take "está com …" and tack the symptom on. Second building rule: German glues words together where the Romance languages need a preposition — Husten + Saft versus jarabe para la tos, Hausmittel versus remédio caseiro, where an adjective carries what German's first noun handles alone. If you break compounds apart first ("syrup for coughing," "remedy from home"), you'll land on the right meaning almost every time.

Three languages, three verbs — and an English euphemism Compare the four cells side by side, that's the whole lesson: German hat Fieber, Spanish tiene fiebre, Brazilian Portuguese está com febre. Tem febre does exist, but it sounds clinical and written, while está com febre is the everyday formula. Add to that the gender switch: fever is neuter in German, feminine in both Romance languages — a febre alta, la fiebre alta. English even throws in a euphemism: "He's got a temperature" doesn't mean "he has a temperature" (everyone has one) — it means he has a fever. And in Brazil, the word antitérmico is worth knowing — the fever reducer, it's printed on every box.

A word ending in -a that can still be masculine Pediatra belongs to the class of comuns de dois gêneros nouns: the form stays the same, only the article decides — o pediatra (he), a pediatra (she). The same pattern carries a whole family of professions: o/a dentista, o/a jornalista, o/a motorista. To German ears o pediatra feels wrong because the word ends in -a — that's exactly the trap. German goes the opposite route and builds a brand-new word for the feminine form: Kinderarzt → Kinderärztin, umlaut included. On the side, the English spelling trap: American pediatrician, British paediatrician with an ae. Practical for Brazil: consulta is the appointment, encaminhamento the referral, and the visit happens either at a private clínica or a public posto de saúde. linguee.de

Free grammar from the health portal: é importante que … sejam The sentence Brazilian health sites keep writing on this exact topic doubles as a perfect grammar lesson: "é importante que os remédios caseiros sejam recomendados … de acordo com a orientação do pediatra." Look at the verb — not são, but sejam. Phrases like é importante que, é necessário que, é melhor que force the subjunctive in Portuguese every time, because they don't report a fact, they express a judgment or a wish. The exact same mechanism in Spanish: es importante que sean. And in German? The subjunctive simply drops out at this point — "es ist wichtig, dass sie empfohlen werden." Once you've filed the trigger away as a signal word (importante · necessário · melhor · preciso + que), you'll hit the right mood almost automatically afterward. As for the substance: the quoted sentence says exactly what the entries above already say — home remedies, yes, but coordinated with the pediatrician. tuasaude.com

gripe is not resfriado — and "grippaler Infekt" is not the flu This distinction is sharper in Brazilian everyday speech than in German, and it's stated exactly this way on the Ministry of Health's website: gripe is caused by the influenza virus, begins suddenly and severely — high fever, body aches, dry cough. Resfriado usually comes from rhinoviruses, builds gradually, and stays mild; sneezing and coriza (a runny nose) point more toward resfriado. So saying "estou gripado" signals something noticeably more serious than "estou resfriado." German gets it backwards: colloquially, any strong head cold gets called "Grippe," even though the technical term grippaler Infekt by definition does not mean influenza. So choose deliberately: gripe means "I suspect the flu," resfriado means "just a harmless cold." bvsms.saude.gov.br

The most expensive false friend in the sickroom: constipado The Spanish Academy simply defines constipado as catarro or resfriado — that is, as a cold. Saying "estoy constipado" in a Spanish-speaking waiting room tells people you have a runny nose. English speakers, on the other hand, hear their own constipated and understand a bowel blockage — the most embarrassing misunderstanding this word field has to offer. The correct mapping: English constipated → Spanish estreñido/a, Portuguese com prisão de ventre, German Verstopfung haben. And Brazil adds an extra trap: in BR Portuguese, constipado isn't the clean opposite case, it's ambiguous — it can mean either a cold or bowel constipation. Rule for here: in Brazil, say resfriado and skip constipado entirely. German has the same double meaning — verstopfte Nase and Verstopfung share one word; only the accompanying noun makes clear which one is meant. dle.rae.es · dicio.com.br · ciberduvidas.iscte-iul.pt

Kitchen versus guard duty — two images for the same week in bed The informal dictionary translates de molho directly as "doente; acamado" — sick, bedridden. The image comes from the kitchen: molho is the sauce, but de molho means "soaked," the way beans sit in water overnight. So being de molho figuratively means sitting in water yourself until you're useful again — a very patient image. You hear it just as often for injuries: a player sidelined for three weeks fica de molho. German and Spanish both reach for guard duty instead: you hüten (guard) the bed — the same verb used for herding sheep — or guarda cama. The difference sits in the construction: in German the bed is the object, while in Portuguese the fixed phrase pairs with either ficar (a change of state) or estar (a state). dicionarioinformal.com.br

Same thing, opposite gender Portuguese and Spanish are closely related and still disagree here: a fralda is feminine, el pañal masculine — because they're two completely different words, not variants of the same one. Pañal traces back to paño, cloth. Anyone coming from Portuguese reflexively says *la pañal — wrong. In the store you distinguish fralda descartável from fralda de pano in Brazil, pañal desechable from pañal de tela in Mexico. The connection to today's topic is no accident: with a sick baby, the diaper is one of the few things that tells you something without a word.

It's Wednesday, August twelfth. The rotation theme is family and everyday life, thirteenth installment — and this time the calendar isn't picking the topic, the situation is. Fiocruz's InfoGripe bulletin for epidemiological week thirty still keeps the state of Paraná in the alert-and-risk band, and within the four-week window, fifty-four point one percent of positive SRAG cases, severe respiratory illness, trace back to RSV, the pathogen group that hits infants hardest. With a one-month-old baby in the house, that's not news — it's a packing list.

So here are this week's must-know words: taking a temperature, coughing, pediatrician, prescription, home remedy. Ten entries today, three of them verbs with full conjugation, plus two refreshers from July second and July twenty-ninth.

Today's most instructive verb is to measure. It's irregular in all three languages — and irregular in a different spot each time. In German it breaks in the second and third person: ich messe, but du misst, er misst; in the past tense it's maß and gemessen. In Portuguese it breaks only in the I-form, where the d turns into a c with a cedilla: eu meço. And in Spanish it breaks under stress, the e becomes an i: yo mido, and in the third-person past, él midió.

Remember the three I-forms as a trio: ich messe, eu meço, yo mido. That's the whole trick, because in Portuguese the I-form decides the entire subjunctive — once you know eu meço, you automatically know que eu meça and que nós meçamos. The subjunctive is built from the I-form stem, never from the infinitive.

The same mechanism shows up in the second verb, to cough. Portuguese tossir raises the stem vowel from o to u, but only in the I-form and throughout the subjunctive: eu tusso, que eu tussa, que nós tussamos. Everywhere else the o stays put: você tosse, eles tossem, eu tossi. It belongs to the same family as dormir and durmo. Spanish toser doesn't make that jump at all — there it's simply yo toso. Anyone coming from Spanish reliably produces a wrong eu tosso in Portuguese as a result.

The third verb is to prescribe, receitar in Portuguese, recetar in Spanish. German shows off its participle rule here in pure form: the prefix ver- is inseparable and unstressed, so the participle gets no ge- at all. It's verschrieben, never geverschrieben. A nice side effect: sich verschreiben also means to make a slip of the pen. And the noun hides a real asymmetry: Rezept, receita, and receta each cover both meanings, the medical prescription and the cooking recipe. Only English splits it into prescription and recipe.

Today's false friend is the most embarrassing one this word field has to offer. The Spanish Academy simply defines constipado as catarro or resfriado — that is, as a cold. Saying estoy constipado in Spanish tells people you have a runny nose. English speakers hear their own constipated and understand a bowel blockage. The correct word would be estreñido in Spanish and com prisão de ventre in Portuguese. Extra trap for Brazil: there, constipado is ambiguous and can mean either. So here, just say resfriado and skip constipado altogether.

One sentence pattern is especially worth learning. In German you have a fever, in Spanish you also have one, tiene fiebre — but in Brazilian everyday speech, you are with one: está com febre. That same formula carries half the sick-visit vocabulary: estou com tosse, estou com dor de garganta, o bebê está com o nariz entupido. If you only want one construction ready in the waiting room, take está com and tack the symptom onto it.

And one more distinction that's sharper in Brazilian Portuguese than in German: gripe is not the same as resfriado. Gripe is caused by the influenza virus, begins suddenly and severely, with high fever and body aches. Resfriado comes from rhinoviruses, builds gradually, and stays mild; sneezing and a runny nose point more toward resfriado. So saying estou gripado means something noticeably more serious. German gets it backwards, since grippaler Infekt, by definition, is not actually the flu.

And to close out, today's most Brazilian phrase: ficar de molho. The informal dictionary translates it as sick, bedridden. Literally it means to be soaked, like beans sitting in water overnight.
