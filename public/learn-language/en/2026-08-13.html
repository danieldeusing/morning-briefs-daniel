Put together, that's exactly the vocabulary you need for Globo Esporte in the evening and the kicker ticker in the morning: subir, perder, o empate, o Z4, a torcida. 10 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, plus 2 refreshers from July 23.

  • FALSE FRIEND o time ≠ time — the Brazilian word for team is o time, and according to Priberam it comes directly from the English team. If you think in English or German, you reflexively read "time" as the clock word. In Portugal, on the other hand, people say a equipa — time is explicitly marked in the dictionary as [Brazil]. priberam.org
  • CONJUGATION eu perco, but yo pierdo — the same verb, two completely different break points. Portuguese swaps the stem perd- for perc-, but only in the I-form and the subjunctive (eu perco, que eu perca). Spanish never does that — instead the vowel splits under stress (pierdo, pierdes, pierde) — and stays flat in nosotros perdemos. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net
  • BR-PT Z4 — Brazilian shorthand for the zona de rebaixamento: the bottom four places of the 20-team table, which drop straight into Série B. "Estar no Z4" is what English clunkily calls "being in the relegation zone." sorteador.com.br
  • CONJUGATION defender: written identically, built differently — in Portuguese, defender is, per the conjugator, regular (eu defendo, nós defendemos); in Spanish it's an e→ie stem-changer (yo defiendo, but nosotros defendemos). Anyone coming from Spanish reliably says *eu defiendo in Portuguese — wrong. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net
  • BR-PT a torcida — a collective singular for the entire fan base, something English doesn't really have; the individual is o torcedor. European Portuguese instead uses os adeptos or a claque. The root is the Latin torqueo, "to twist, to wring" — from wringing your hands with tension. priberam.org
  • IDIOM pendurar as chuteiras — literally "to hang up the cleats," i.e., to end one's career. The slang dictionary equates it simply with se aposentar and explicitly lets it apply to any activity, not just sports — exactly like the English "to hang up one's boots." qualeagiria.com.br
  • PHRASE acréscimos is plural — stoppage time in Brazil is usually called os acréscimos, literally "the additions" (also tempo adicionado). Mexican Spanish uses the singular: el tiempo añadido, and in broadcasts often el descuento. appsbyalejo.com · olympics.com

Three Verbs: aufsteigen, verlieren, verteidigen

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br and conjugator.reverso.net

Why the same verb breaks in different places across two languages: Portuguese and Spanish are siblings with two completely different repair strategies, and today you see both side by side. Spanish reacts to stress. When the word accent hits the stem vowel, it splits: e becomes ie — pierdo, pierdes, pierde, pierden. When the stress moves to the ending, the stem stays intact: nosotros perdemos. Portuguese, by contrast, reacts to the ending. The I-form attaches an -o, and that changes the consonant in front of it: perd- becomes perc- (eu perco), just as med- became meç- yesterday. Because the entire subjunctive is built from that I-form stem, it carries the change along: que eu perca, que nós percamos. Practical consequence: in Spanish you need to know which persons carry the stress (the four outer ones do, nosotros doesn't). In Portuguese, the I-form is enough — the rest follows. A third pattern joins in today: subir changes neither consonant nor stress, but instead lowers the vowel in the third person — eu subo, but ele sobe. Rule of thumb: Spanish breaks under stress, Portuguese breaks before the ending.
CONJUGATION Verb DE separable & strong (auf|steigen, ei–ie–ie), perfect with sein: ich bin aufgestiegen · PT-BR subir, irregular: vowel lowering u→o in the third person (ele sobe) · ES-MX ascender, stem change e→ie (yo asciendo)
aufsteigen

Zwei Klubs steigen am Ende der Saison in die Série A auf.

Sein Verein ist letztes Jahr zum ersten Mal aufgestiegen.

Wenn sie heute gewinnen, steigen sie auf.

to go up / to be promoted

Two clubs go up to Série A at the end of the season.

His club went up for the first time last year.

If they win today, they go up.

subir

Dois clubes sobem para a Série A no fim da temporada.

O time dele subiu pela primeira vez no ano passado.

Se eles ganharem hoje, sobem.

ascender

Dos clubes ascienden a la Série A al final de la temporada.

Su equipo ascendió por primera vez el año pasado.

Si ganan hoy, ascienden.

Two traps at once: bin aufgestiegen — and ele sobe German: aufsteigen describes a change of location, and such verbs form the perfect tense with sein: ich bin aufgestiegen, never *ich habe aufgestiegen. On top of that, the prefix auf- is stressed and therefore separable — in a main clause it flies to the end (sie steigen auf), and in the participle the ge- wedges itself into the middle (aufgestiegen). Portuguese: subir belongs to the -ir verbs with vowel lowering. The I-form keeps the u (eu subo), but in the third person it lowers to o: ele sobe, eles sobem. That's the reverse of yesterday's tossir → eu tusso — there the vowel rose in the I-form, here it lowers in the he-form. The subjunctive follows the u: que eu suba, que nós subamos. Spanish: ascender diphthongizes under stress — asciendo … ascienden, but nosotros ascendemos; the past tense runs smoothly: ascendí, ascendió. verbformen.de · conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net

CONJUGATION Verb DE strong & inseparable (ie–o–o): verlieren–verlor–verloren, participle without ge-, perfect with haben · PT-BR perder, irregular: stem perd- → perc- in eu perco + throughout the subjunctive · ES-MX perder, stem change e→ie (yo pierdo)

Jedes Mal, wenn ich gegen sie spiele, verliere ich.

Verlier den Ball bloß nicht in der eigenen Hälfte!

to lose

perder

O Palmeiras perdeu só dois jogos nesta temporada.

Toda vez que eu jogo contra eles, eu perco.

Não perca a bola no seu próprio campo!

perder

El Palmeiras ha perdido solo dos partidos esta temporada.

Cada vez que juego contra ellos, pierdo.

¡No pierdas el balón en tu propio campo!

Today's lesson: one verb, two break points No other word shows the difference between the two Romance languages as clearly. Portuguese breaks at the consonant, and only before the I-form ending: the conjugator explicitly lists a stem change from perd- to perc-, and it happens in exactly two places — in eu perco and throughout the Presente do subjuntivo (que eu perca … que nós percamos). Everywhere else the d stays: você perde, nós perdemos, eu perdi. Spanish breaks at the vowel, and only under stress: pierdo, pierdes, pierde, pierden — but nosotros perdemos, because there the ending carries the stress; in the pretérito the diphthong disappears entirely (perdí, perdió). The practical test is the imperative, because in Portuguese it's built from the subjunctive: não perca a bola versus no pierdas el balón — two sentences, the same meaning, two completely different stems. German adds its own trap: ver- is unstressed and inseparable, so the participle gets no ge- — verloren, never *geverloren. As an adjective it lives on: ein verlorener Ball, verlorene Zeit. verbformen.de · conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net

Same word, built differently — the costliest mix-up for Spanish speakers Defender is spelled letter-for-letter identically in both Romance languages, yet behaves in opposite ways. Portuguese: the conjugator classifies it as regular — eu defendo, você defende, nós defendemos, subjunctive que eu defenda. No stem change and no spelling change. Spanish: the same infinitive, but e→ie under stress — yo defiendo, tú defiendes, ustedes defienden, only nosotros defendemos stays bare. Anyone with the Spanish form in mind reflexively produces *eu defiendo in Portuguese. This is today's second case of the same pattern: perder and defender are both e→ie verbs in Spanish, but built completely differently in Portuguese — one irregular at the consonant, the other perfectly regular. German: despite its complicated looks, verteidigen is a well-behaved weak verb; ver- is again unstressed and inseparable, so it's verteidigt, not *geverteidigt. A free second meaning: in the courtroom, the lawyer verteidigt the defendant — o advogado defende o réu, exactly the same image. verbformen.de · conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net

Reading the Table: Unentschieden and Abstieg

2 nouns, straight from the Brasileirão standings after round 22

How a soccer table works — in one paragraph, so the words below make sense: over the course of a season, every club plays every other club twice, once at home, once away. A win is worth three points, a draw one, a loss none. Add them up and you get the standings — the table, PT a tabela, ES la tabla. Palmeiras has 14 wins × 3 plus 6 draws × 1 = 48 points. Whoever's on top becomes champion; whoever's at the bottom has to go down: in Brazil the bottom four of the 20 clubs drop to Série B, and those exact spots are colloquially called Z4. That's why subir ("to go up") is the everyday Brazilian word for promotion, and o rebaixamento ("the demotion") the word for relegation. A game "in hand": Flamengo has 42 points from 21 games, Palmeiras 48 from 22. Flamengo could theoretically still add three points — that's why Brazilian reports always add "com um jogo a menos." The six-point lead is therefore less stable than it looks. The word for it in Brazil is a vantagem ("the advantage"), and the preposition de is mandatory: seis pontos de vantagem. The three collocations that show up in every report: abrir (to extend), manter (to hold), and diminuir a vantagem (to narrow).
PT = ES Noun DE n, pl. - (unchanged) · PT-BR o empate, m · ES-MX el empate, m — identical in both languages, both from the verb empatar
das Unentschieden

Gegen Internacional gab es nur ein Unentschieden: 0:0.

Ein Unentschieden reicht ihnen, um Tabellenführer zu bleiben.

the draw / the tie

Against Internacional it was only a draw: 0-0.

A draw is enough for them to stay top of the table.

o empate

Contra o Internacional ficou só no empate: 0 a 0.

Um empate basta para eles continuarem na liderança.

el empate

Contra el Internacional quedó solo en empate: 0-0.

Un empate les basta para seguir en el liderato.

One word for two languages — and a German monster This is the rare case where Portuguese and Spanish don't differ at all: o empate and el empate, both masculine, spelled the same, both back-formed from the verb empatar, which according to Priberam came in via the Italian impattare; the sports sense is listed there as "Igualdade de pontos ou partidas ganhas entre dois ou mais jogadores" (equality of points or matches won between two or more players). Anyone who knows the verb gets three sentences for free: empatar em 0 a 0, o jogo empatou, eles conseguiram empatar. German, by contrast, builds a nominalized participle with a built-in negation — das Unentschieden, "the un-decided" — neuter, and identical in the plural (sechs Unentschieden). English splits regionally: Britons say a draw, Americans a tie. And the scoreline: German connects with "zu" ("null zu null"), Portuguese and Spanish with a — zero a zero, cero a cero. priberam.org · spanishdict.com

Z4 — the Brazilian shorthand that needs no dictionary Every Brazilian broadcast sooner or later drops the abbreviation Z4: the zona de rebaixamento, the bottom four spots of the 20-team table, from which it's a straight drop to Série B. You hear it used like a location — estar no Z4, sair do Z4, entrar no Z4. German has nothing this compact; "Abstiegszone," "Abstiegsplatz," "Abstiegskampf" is as close as it gets. Word formation: rebaixamento comes from baixo (low) — "the lowering," the same idea as the Spanish descenso and the German Abstieg. Note the construction: in Portuguese a club gets relegated — o clube foi rebaixado, passive — while German and Spanish go down actively: der Klub steigt ab, el club desciende. sorteador.com.br

Who Plays, Who Watches — and Who Calls It Quits

A false friend, a collective noun, and an idiom

FALSE FRIEND Noun DE f, pl. -en · PT-BR o time, m (from the English team; PT-Portugal: a equipa) · ES-MX el equipo, m
die Mannschaft

Für welche Mannschaft bist du?

Die Mannschaft hat zu Hause noch kein Spiel verloren.

the team

Which team do you support?

The team hasn't lost a home game yet.

o time

Para que time você torce?

O time ainda não perdeu nenhum jogo em casa.

el equipo

¿A qué equipo le vas?

El equipo todavía no ha perdido ningún partido en casa.

The classic: o time has nothing to do with time Priberam defines time as "Grupo de pessoas que praticam a mesma modalidade desportiva e entram juntas numa competição" (a group of people who practice the same sport and enter a competition together) and simply lists its origin as "inglês team" — marked [Brazil], with the European counterpart equipa. An anglicism, then, so thoroughly naturalized that no one hears it as one anymore. For German and English eyes it's still a trap, though, since the written word looks exactly like the English time. Two extra rules for Brazilian Portuguese: a equipe exists there too, but tends to mean a work team or medical team; in soccer you say time. And the question "Who do you support?" has its own construction per language — Brazilian with torcer (para que time você torce?), Mexican with irle a (¿a qué equipo le vas?). Gender jump: German uses die Mannschaft (feminine), but o time and el equipo, both masculine. priberam.org
BR-PT Noun DE pl. (sg. der Fan, m) · PT-BR a torcida, f — collective; individually o torcedor (PT-Portugal: os adeptos, a claque) · ES-MX la afición, f; individually el aficionado
die Fans

The Palmeiras fans filled the stadium.

I have been a fan of this club since I was a kid.

Eu torço para esse time desde criança.

la afición

La afición del Palmeiras llenó el estadio.

Le voy a ese equipo desde niño.

A word German doesn't have A torcida is a collective singular: the entire fan base as one being, which the commentator addresses like a person ("a torcida está nervosa"). The individual is o torcedor / a torcedora. European Portuguese goes a different route — there it's os adeptos, and the organized group is a claque. German has no natural word for this collective: "die Fans" is a plural of many individuals, "der Anhang" sounds like sports-desk jargon, and "die Fankurve" refers to the terrace block itself. Today's nicest etymology: torcida belongs to torcer, from the Latin torqueo — "to twist, to wring, to exert force." The image is of spectators wringing their hands with tension. That's why "to cross your fingers for someone" in Portuguese, even outside sports, is also torcer por alguém: estou torcendo por você. Spanish doesn't follow suit — behind la afición is affection, and "to cross your fingers" there is desear suerte. priberam.org
IDIOM Idiom DE literally "to hang the soccer boots on the nail" · PT-BR pendurar as chuteiras (chuteira = soccer boot) · ES-MX colgar los botines / colgar las botas

Why the second example isn't a soccer player The slang dictionary defines the phrase this way: „pendurar as chuteiras é o mesmo que se aposentar, parar de fazer alguma atividade corriqueira" (to hang up the cleats is the same as retiring, stopping some routine activity) — so it explicitly means not only a player's career ending, but stopping any regular activity. That's exactly where it meets English, where "to hang something up" also combines freely. The difference is the image: Portuguese always names the tool (as chuteiras — even if the speaker is an accountant), German always names the nail. For everyday life, the plain word next to it is worth knowing: se aposentar means "to retire," a aposentadoria is the pension — both come up more often in daily life than on the pitch. Bookkeeping versus reinforcement The refresher is worth it because the image behind it is so different: a Neuzugang is German-plain for "the newly arrived one," an intake entry on the roster. O reforço and el refuerzo, by contrast, mean "the reinforcement," a military image — which is why they also work for things: reforço na defesa, but just as well reforço no orçamento (a budget boost). Practically useful for today: the sentence that will run in every Brazilian report for the next three weeks is „o clube busca um reforço para o ataque" (the club is looking for reinforcement up front). Today's key lesson is the verb "to lose." Portuguese and Spanish spell it the same way, perder, and still break in completely different places. Portuguese swaps the stem perd for perc, but only in the I-form and the subjunctive: eu perco, que eu perca. Spanish never does that — instead, the vowel splits under stress: yo pierdo, tú pierdes, but nosotros perdemos.

You see it most clearly in the imperative, since Portuguese builds that from the subjunctive. In Portuguese it's não perca a bola. In Spanish, no pierdas el balón. Two sentences, the same meaning, two completely different stems. Today's rule of thumb: Spanish breaks under stress, Portuguese breaks before the ending.

The same pattern shows up again with the verb "to defend." Portuguese defender is, according to the conjugator, entirely regular — eu defendo, nós defendemos. Spanish defender, on the other hand, is a stem-changer: yo defiendo, but nosotros defendemos. Anyone coming from Spanish reliably says a wrong eu defiendo in Portuguese as a result. And a third pattern joins in with "to go up": Portuguese subir lowers its vowel in the third person. Eu subo, but ele sobe.

Today's false friend is the classic one for anyone who knows English. The Brazilian word for team is o time, and Priberam simply lists its origin as the English "team," marked as specific to Brazil. In Portugal, people instead say a equipa. Anyone thinking in English reflexively reads it as the clock word — and gets it wrong.

Two Brazilian words are especially useful today. First, the abbreviation Z-four: the zona de rebaixamento, the bottom four places of the twenty-team table, which drop straight into the second division. You say estar no Z-four, or sair do Z-four. Second, a torcida, a collective word for the entire fan base, something English simply doesn't have. It comes from the Latin torqueo, to twist and wring — the image of wringing your hands with tension. That's why crossing your fingers for someone in Portuguese, even outside the stadium, is also torcer por alguém.

Finally, today's idiom: pendurar as chuteiras, to hang up the cleats. The slang dictionary equates it simply with retiring, and lets it apply to any activity, not just sports — exactly like the English "to hang up one's boots."
