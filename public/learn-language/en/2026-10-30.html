Feriadão Vocabulary: sair, chegar, o acostamento

Friday, October 30 — rotation topic Travel & Traffic, installment ten. The occasion is on the road: Monday, November 2, is Finados, so a long weekend starts today. According to a report from October 26, the operator Motiva Autoban expects around 2.263 million vehicles on Anhanguera and Bandeirantes between Friday and Monday. The worst time: today from 4 to 8 p.m. So we are learning the words for the trip, from setting off to the way back: to leave, to arrive, to drive back, plus rush hour, exit, shoulder, and a truck that sounds like a road in Spanish. 10 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, including 3 reviews.

  • FALSE FRIEND a carreta ≠ la carretera — in Brazil, a carreta is the big truck trailer, meaning the semi-truck. In Mexico, la carretera is the country road. The semi-truck there is called el tráiler. dicio.com.br · dem.colmex.mx
  • BR-PT o acostamento — the shoulder. Mexico says almost the same thing: el acotamiento. The DEM even quotes the sign: "No circule por el acotamiento". dicio.com.br · dem.colmex.mx
  • CONJUGATION ich bin abgefahren, ich bin angekommen — all three of today's verbs form the perfect with sein. Whoever moves "is" gone. verbformen.de · verbformen.de
  • CONJUGATION yo salgo, saldré — Spanish salir has a -g- in the first person and throughout the whole subjunctive (salga), and a -d- in the future. Portuguese sair stays free of that consonant: eu saio, sairei. reverso.net · conjugacao.com.br
  • BR-PT o retorno do feriado — the return trip from the holiday. For Monday, the forecast names 3 to 10 p.m. "no retorno do feriado prolongado". On a road sign, however, Retorno is the turnaround point. tribunadejundiai.com.br
  • PHRASE el puente — in Mexico, the long weekend is called el puente, the "bridge" between a holiday and the weekend. Brazil says o feriadão or o feriado prolongado. chilango.com
  • IDIOM a vuelta de rueda — this is how Mexican media report sluggish traffic: "¡A vuelta de rueda!". Literally "one turn of the wheel at a time"; in English: at a snail's pace. nmas.com.mx

Three Verbs for the Trip: to leave, to arrive, to drive back

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked at verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br and conjugator.reverso.net

Why German says "ich bin gefahren" and not "ich habe gefahren": Picture two boxes. One holds verbs where you move from place A to place B: setting off, arriving, driving back. The other holds verbs where you do something without ending up somewhere else: reading, eating, filling up. The movement box takes sein in the perfect, the other takes haben. That is why it is ich bin abgefahren, but ich habe getankt. Portuguese and Spanish do not have this problem: they mostly tell the past with a single form, saí and salí. For German learners the box is a trap; for you it is a native language — but it gets interesting when you have to explain it to the kids.

"Você chegou bem?" — the most important question of the weekend In Brazil, people ask after every trip: Chegou bem? The answer: Cheguei! Mind the u: before an e, g becomes gu, otherwise it would sound like "shegay". Spanish does the same: llegué, lleguen. The third sentence shows two forms that German does not need. After assim que, PT takes the future subjunctive (chegarem); after en cuanto, ES takes the subjunctive (lleguen). And márcame is Mexican for "call me"; Spain says llámame. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net

Rush Hour, Exit, Shoulder — and a False Friend

The words for the country road on a Friday afternoon

What 2.263 million cars mean: Picture a funnel. Everyone dumps sand in at the top at the same time, and only a thin stream fits through at the bottom. That is a highway at rush hour: the road does not get smaller, but everyone wants to leave at the same hour. According to the October 26 report, Motiva Autoban expects around 2.263 million vehicles on Anhanguera and Bandeirantes, from today through Monday. It gets fullest today from 4 to 8 p.m., Saturday from 9 a.m. to 1 p.m., and Monday from 3 to 10 p.m. This is a forecast for two routes near São Paulo, not a live measurement — for the Norte Pioneiro there is no such figure. Whoever knows the funnel drives earlier or later, but not when everyone else drives.
BR-PT Noun DE f, pl. -en · EN rush hour / peak hours · PT-BR o horário de pico, m · ES-MX la hora pico, f (Spain: hora punta)
die Stoßzeit

Die Stoßzeit ist heute zwischen 16 und 20 Uhr.

Außerhalb der Stoßzeit brauchst du nur halb so lang.

rush hour

Rush hour today is between 4 and 8 p.m.

Outside rush hour, it only takes you half as long.

o horário de pico

O horário de pico hoje é das 16h às 20h.

Fora do horário de pico, você leva metade do tempo.

la hora pico

La hora pico hoy es de las 4 a las 8 de la tarde.

Fuera de la hora pico, haces la mitad del tiempo.

"das 16h às 20h" — clock times, Brazilian style Brazil writes clock times with h and counts 24 hours: das 16h às 20h, spoken "das dezesseis às vinte horas". That is exactly how it appears in the forecast. In everyday speech, Mexico counts 12 hours and adds which part of the day: a las 4 de la tarde. For the word itself, both countries use the image of a mountain peak: pico. Spain, by contrast, says hora punta. tribunadejundiai.com.br
Noun DE f, pl. -en · EN the exit · PT-BR a saída, f · ES-MX la salida, f — from sair / salir

Wir haben die Ausfahrt verpasst und müssen jetzt wenden.

Nimm die nächste Ausfahrt Richtung Ourinhos.

the exit

We missed the exit and now we have to turn around.

Take the next exit toward Ourinhos.

a saída

A gente perdeu a saída e agora tem que fazer o retorno.

Pega a próxima saída para Ourinhos.

One word, three German words German distinguishes precisely: the Ausfahrt is on the highway, the Ausgang is in a building, and the Abfahrt is the moment you set off. PT a saída and ES la salida cover all three. That is why a saída para o feriado is the big getaway and not an exit ramp. In the first sentence, note the turnaround: Brazil says fazer o retorno, Mexico dar vuelta en U. And nos pasamos la salida means "we drove past the exit". You will hear the imperative pega instead of pegue in daily speech all the time.

Two countries, one letter apart Acostamento and acotamiento sound almost the same, but they come from different images. The Brazilian word comes from acostar, "to moor", like a boat at the shore: the Dicio dictionary calls it the free space next to the roadway "destinado a parada ou estacionamento de emergência". The Mexican word comes from acotar, "to mark off". The DEM gives the sentence "No circule por el acotamiento". Mexico also has its own verb: orillarse, "to pull over to the edge". And in Mexico a car does not break down, it se descompone. dicio.com.br · dem.colmex.mx

carreta, carretera, tráiler — three traps For Spanish speakers, a carreta sounds like a road, because la carretera is the country road. In Brazil, however, according to Dicio, a carreta is "[Brasil] Grande reboque de caminhão", meaning the big truck trailer. In daily speech, it means the whole semi-truck. Mexico takes the English word: el tráiler, according to the DEM a "camión articulado de grandes proporciones". Careful: in Mexican Spanish, tráiler is also the movie trailer. Snail, tortoise, wheel German and English think of the snail, Brazil of the tortoise: a passo de tartaruga. Mexico thinks of the car itself: a vuelta de rueda means "one turn of the wheel at a time". That is the standard formula of traffic reports. One N+ headline read, for example, "¡A Vuelta de Rueda! Vacacionistas Saturan la Autopista México-Cuernavaca". The phrase fits any Feriadão Monday. Note in the second sentence: with the snail's pace, the verb to arrive returns — chegar, llegar. nmas.com.mx

It's Friday, October thirtieth. Today's rotation topic is travel and traffic, installment ten. The occasion is out on the road: Monday is Finados, so a long weekend starts today. According to a report from October twenty-sixth, the operator Motiva Autoban expects about two point two six three million vehicles on the Anhanguera and Bandeirantes highways, from today through Monday. Ten entries today, three of them verbs with full conjugation, plus three reviews.

The three verbs tell the whole trip: to leave, to arrive, to drive back. In German, all three form the perfect with sein, because you're moving. In Portuguese they're sair, chegar and voltar, and in Mexican Spanish salir, llegar and regresar. Watch the Spanish salir: yo salgo, saldré, que salga. And watch chegar: in cheguei there's a u, so the g stays hard.

When will it get crowded? According to the forecast, today from four to eight p.m., Saturday from nine a.m. to one p.m., and Monday from three to ten p.m. That's rush hour: o horário de pico in Brazil, la hora pico in Mexico. The numbers cover two routes near São Paulo, not the Norte Pioneiro.

Next, two words for the road. The exit is a saída and la salida, the same word as departure. The shoulder is o acostamento in Brazil, and almost the same in Mexico, el acotamiento.

The false friend of the day is carreta. In Brazil, a carreta is the big truck trailer, in other words the semi-truck. In Spanish it sounds like la carretera, the country road. Mexico calls the semi-truck el tráiler.

For the trip home there's the word o retorno. In the forecast, it means the return trip from the holiday, but on a road sign it's the turnaround point. Mexico says el regreso and calls the long weekend el puente. As a review from July, the toll booth is back: a praça de pedágio and la caseta.

One last idiom for Monday evening: at a snail's pace. Brazil says a passo de tartaruga, with the tortoise. Mexico says a vuelta de rueda, one turn of the wheel at a time. Safe travels if you're heading out. Tomorrow the topic switches to food and cooking.
