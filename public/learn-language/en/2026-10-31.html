Halloween Kitchen Vocabulary: a abóbora, o doce, el pan de muerto

Saturday, October 31 — rotation topic Food, Cooking & Restaurant, episode ten. Today is Halloween, Monday is Finados, and in Mexico the Día de Muertos runs from Oct 31 to Nov 2. All three holidays put something on the plate: in Brazil sweets and doce de abóbora, in Mexico the pan de muerto, in the US the hollowed-out pumpkin. So we learn the kitchen words that go with them: to hollow out, to carve, to decorate, plus pumpkin, candy, Day of the Dead bread and a cake that means something quite different in Portuguese. 11 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, including 2 refreshers.

  • FALSE FRIEND o pastel ≠ el pastel — in Brazil o pastel is the filled pastry pocket from the market. In Mexico el pastel is the cake: according to the DEM a „Postre o golosina que se hace con pasta de harina de trigo horneada". priberam.org · dem.colmex.mx
  • FALSE FRIEND la calabacita ≠ small pumpkin — in Mexico the calabacita is, per the DEM, an elongated fruit 10 to 12 cm long (Cucurbita pepo), so in the kitchen usually zucchini. The big pumpkin is la calabaza. dem.colmex.mx · dem.colmex.mx
  • BR-PT a moranga — besides a abóbora, Brazilians often say a moranga. Dicio calls it „Certa variedade de abóbora": the round, flat squash, often used as a bowl for camarão na moranga. dicio.com.br
  • CONJUGATION ahuequé, que ahueque — the Mexican ahuecar („to hollow out") writes qu before e so the c stays hard. The PT counterpart escavar is completely regular. reverso.net · conjugacao.com.br
  • FALSE FRIEND decorar = to decorate — and to memorize — in Portuguese decorar has a second meaning: „aprender de cor". Decorar o vocabulário thus means: to cram the vocabulary. Spanish does not have this meaning. priberam.org
  • PHRASE ¿Me da mi calaverita? — this is how kids in Mexico ask for sweets. Traditionally on Nov 1, but because of Halloween often already on the evening of the 31st. Brazil says doces ou travessuras. nmas.com.mx

Three verbs for the pumpkin: hollow out, carve, decorate

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br and conjugator.reverso.net

Why „ahuequé" has a „qu": Imagine every letter has a costume. Before a, o and u, the c wears the costume „k": ahueco, ahuecamos. Before e and i, though, it switches to the costume „s" or „th". To keep the sound the same, the word puts on a different costume before e: qu. So ahuecé becomes the correct form ahuequé, and ahuece becomes ahueque. The sound does not change, only the spelling. Portuguese knows the same trick: ficar → fiquei. Today's two PT verbs, escavar and entalhar, have no c at the end of the stem and so need no costume change.

Hohl, oco, hueco — and the word from Wednesday The German and Spanish verbs build on the word for „hollow": DE hohl, ES hueco (hence a-hue-car). PT escavar is closer to „dig out": you dig the inside out. On Oct 28 you learned ausleeren („to empty out"), in PT esvaziar, in ES vaciar. That also works for a pumpkin: esvaziar a abóbora, vaciar la calabaza. The difference: when you hollow out, you leave a shell standing. When you empty, you only want nothing left inside. The seeds are called as sementes in PT and las pepitas in Mexico. verbformen.de

„tallar" in Mexico — first scrub, then carve The DEM, the dictionary of Mexican Spanish, gives as the first meaning of tallar: „Frotar con energía algún objeto para suavizarlo, lavarlo o limpiarlo" — with the examples tallar la ropa and tallar el piso. So in Mexico you scrub laundry and floors. Carving only comes as the fifth meaning: cutting small pieces from a piece of wood, „para formar figuras". In the pumpkin sentence the meaning is clear. But tállate bien in the bathroom means: „scrub yourself well". And chueco in the second sentence is Mexican for „crooked". dem.colmex.mx

„decorar a tabuada" — decorate or cram? Priberam lists decorar twice. Decorar¹ is „Guarnecer … com decorações", i.e. to decorate. Decorar² is a word of its own: „Aprender de maneira a guardar na memória; aprender de cor". The second comes from de cor, „by heart" — literally „from the heart". A Brazilian child who decorou a tabuada has learned the times table, not painted it. Spanish does not have this meaning; there it is aprenderse de memoria. In the ES sentence, note betún: that is the Mexican word for icing. And solitos is the typically Mexican diminutive of solos. The Day of the Dead bread in the third sentence carries, according to N+, „piezas de masa que simulan huesos". priberam.org · nmas.com.mx

Pumpkin, sweets, Day of the Dead bread — the table on three holidays

5 nouns and phrases · Halloween, Finados, Día de Muertos

Three holidays, one weekend: Picture three neighbors who celebrate on the same weekend, each in their own way. The first carves pumpkins and hands out candy: Halloween, today, October 31. The second goes quietly to the cemetery on Monday, November 2, and brings flowers: Finados in Brazil, a national holiday. The third sets a table for the dead, with bread, candles and orange flowers: the Día de Muertos in Mexico, which, according to the newspaper Crónica, falls on Saturday, Sunday and Monday this year. The neighbors borrow from each other, though: Mexican kids already go from house to house today, and Brazilian schools celebrate the Dia das Bruxas. The words below belong to all three tables.

abóbora, moranga, calabaza, calabacita Brazil has two everyday words. A abóbora is the umbrella term. A moranga is, per Dicio, „Certa variedade de abóbora" — the round, red squash you hollow out for camarão na moranga. In Mexico, la calabaza is, per the DEM, a fruit „de forma esférica u ovoidal, de gran tamaño, con cáscara dura". The diminutive is deceptive: la calabacita is not a small pumpkin. The DEM describes it as an elongated fruit 10 to 12 cm long; in a recipe that is usually zucchini. So if you buy „calabacitas" in Mexico, you will not come home with Halloween pumpkins. Also note anaranjado: in Mexico that is the usual word for „orange"; PT simply says laranja, with no ending. dicio.com.br · dem.colmex.mx · dem.colmex.mx

doce, bala, dulce — and a cake In Portuguese, o doce is anything sweet: candy, pudding, also the preserved doce de abóbora. For a single piece of candy Brazil says a bala — the same word as for a bullet. Portugal says rebuçado. Mexico calls almost everything dulce, even the spicy tamarind candies. Note two everyday verbs: PT pegar and ES-MX agarrar both simply mean „to take". In Spain coger sounds normal for this; in Mexico it is vulgar. So stick with agarrar.

Threaten or ask? German, English and Portuguese threaten: treat us, or else there is a trick — travessura is the prank. Mexico asks politely, with usted: „¿Me da mi calaverita?", literally „Will you give me my little skull?". What is meant is the gift for the kids. According to N+ (10/23/2026), the traditional day for this is November 1; „por influencia del Halloween" many families already set out on the evening of the 31st. In Brazil Halloween is called Dia das Bruxas and is mostly found in schools and English courses. And hoy en la noche is Mexican; Spain says esta noche. nmas.com.mx · opovo.com.br

A bread with bones According to N+, the pan de muerto is a sweet bread that is baked in Mexico „desde principios de octubre hasta mediados de noviembre", „decorado con piezas de masa que simulan huesos". It belongs on the ofrenda, the offering table for the dead. Brazil has no word of its own for it and adopts the Mexican name. The DE form „Totenbrot" is a loan translation. Remember: ES muerto and PT morto differ only in the vowel. On the ofrenda there is often also a calavera de azúcar, a skull made of sugar. nmas.com.mx

One flower, three images The DEM describes the cempasúchil as Tagetes erecta „de olor penetrante" and writes: „Es muy usada para adornar las tumbas, sobre todo el Día de muertos". It's Saturday, October thirty-first. Today's rotation topic is food, cooking and restaurants, episode ten. Today is Halloween, Monday is Finados, and in Mexico the Día de Muertos runs from October thirty-first through November second. All three holidays put something on the plate. Eleven entries, including three verbs with full conjugation and two refreshers.

The three verbs tell the story of a pumpkin night: to hollow out, to carve, to decorate. In Portuguese they are escavar, entalhar and decorar, and in Mexican Spanish ahuecar, tallar and decorar. With ahuecar, the c turns into qu before an e: yo ahuequé, que ahueque. The sound stays the same, only the spelling changes.

Two of the verbs have a flip side. In Mexico, according to the DEM dictionary, tallar first means to scrub, as in laundry or floors. And in Portuguese, decorar doesn't only mean to decorate, it also means to memorize. A kid who decorou a tabuada has learned the times table.

Then the pumpkin itself. In Brazil it's a abóbora or a moranga, in Mexico la calabaza. Watch out for la calabacita: that's not a small pumpkin, but, according to the DEM, an elongated fruit four to five inches long, usually zucchini in the kitchen.

At the front door, German kids shout Süßes oder Saures, American kids shout trick or treat, and Brazilian kids say doces ou travessuras. In Mexico, kids ask politely: ¿Me da mi calaverita? According to N Plus, the traditional day for that is November first, but because of Halloween many families head out tonight.

On the Mexican offering table you'll find pan de muerto, a sweet bread with bones made of dough, and the orange flower cempasúchil. In Portuguese the same flower is called cravo-de-defunto, and in English, the marigold.

Today's false friend is pastel. In Brazil, o pastel is the fried pastry pocket from the market; in Mexico, el pastel is the cake. The cake is called o bolo in Brazil.
