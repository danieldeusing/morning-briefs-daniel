Vocabulary after the election: apurar, reconhecer, a derrota

Sunday, November 1 — rotation topic Politics, Law & Health, episode nine. The occasion was a week ago: on Sunday, October 25, Lula was re-elected in the runoff with about 51.8% of the valid votes against Flávio Bolsonaro. The president of the electoral court TSE, Nunes Marques, called both candidates that same evening to confirm the result. Today we learn the words for the time after election day: to count (the votes), to acknowledge, to concede, plus result, defeat, re-election and inauguration. 10 entries, 3 of them verbs with full conjugation, including 3 refreshers from July.

  • FALSE FRIEND apurar os votos ≠ ¡apúrate! — in Brazil, apurar means "to count the votes." In Mexico, according to the DEM, apurarse means "Darse prisa": ¡Apúrate! = "Hurry up!" priberam.org · dem.colmex.mx
  • BR-PT o segundo turno — that is what the runoff is called in Brazil. Priberam marks it as [Brasil]; in Portugal people say a segunda volta. Mexico says la segunda vuelta. priberam.org
  • FALSE FRIEND rendir protesta ≠ to protest — in Mexico, a new office-holder takes the oath of office with rendir protesta. According to the DEM: "Comprometerse solemnemente alguien que recibe un cargo". dem.colmex.mx
  • CONJUGATION anerkannt, not "angeerkannt" — anerkennen is separable (ich erkenne an), but the participle gets no ge-, because er- already comes before the stem. verbformen.de
  • CONJUGATION reconheço / reconozco — the same verb, two spelling tricks: PT writes a ç before o, ES inserts a z. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net
  • PHRASE to concede defeat (eine Niederlage einräumen) — PT reconhecer a derrota, ES reconocer la derrota or admitir la derrota. The German einräumen has a second, very practical meaning: to put things away in a cupboard.

Three verbs for election night: to count, to acknowledge, to concede

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br and conjugator.reverso.net

What happens after election day: Imagine a game where everyone drops a slip of paper into a box. Then three things happen. First, someone counts all the slips: that is auszählen (to count), in PT apurar. Then everyone says: "Yes, that is the result, it stands." That is anerkennen (to acknowledge), in PT reconhecer. And in the end the loser says: "Okay, you won." That is eine Niederlage einräumen (to concede defeat), in PT admitir or reconhecer a derrota. On October 25, the head of Brazil's electoral court called both candidates to confirm the result. The three verbs below tell exactly that night.

erkennen or anerkennen? PT and ES make no distinction German separates two things. Erkennen means: "I see who that is." Anerkennen means: "I say that it counts." PT reconhecer and ES reconocer cover both. Reconheci você pela voz means "I recognized you by your voice." Reconheço o resultado means "I acknowledge the result." The sentence decides. English separates them like German: recognize for recognizing, acknowledge for acknowledging. Two senses of "einräumen" — and "a pesquisa" In German, einräumen has a very practical first meaning: putting dishes away in the cupboard. PT says guardar or arrumar for that, Mexico guardar or acomodar. Only the second meaning is "to admit": you grant the other person a bit of being right, like a shelf in the cupboard. The English to concede fits election night; to admit fits the mistake. PT and ES use admitir for both. For defeat, both languages often also say reconhecer / reconocer. Watch out for a pesquisa: in Brazil it also means the election poll. Mexico says la encuesta for that. verbformen.de

Result, defeat, re-election — the words that follow

4 nouns and 1 adjective · around the runoff of October 25

Why there are two rounds of voting: Imagine a class choosing its spokesperson, and five kids are running. Nobody gets more than half of the votes. So the class votes again, but only between the top two. Now one of them has to get over half. That is how the presidential election works in Brazil. The first round is called o primeiro turno, the second o segundo turno. In the first round, Flávio Bolsonaro was still narrowly ahead, 47.03% to 45.16%. In the second round on October 25, Lula turned the result around and got about 51.8%.

"votos válidos" — why two sources differ slightly Brazil calculates in votos válidos: blank and invalid ballots do not count. The Diário do Grande ABC reports 64,256,232 votes for Lula, which is 51.79%. Times Brasil reported 51.78% with 99.98% of the ballot boxes counted. The difference is one hundredth of a percentage point, so here we say "about 51.8%". In the PT sentence, note o Lula: in everyday Brazilian speech, names are often preceded by an article. Mexico writes the decimal with a point: 51.8%. dgabc.com.br · timesbrasil.com.br

"Sufragio efectivo, no reelección" In Mexico, reelección is a politically loaded word. The president governs exactly one term of six years, the sexenio. After that, he may never hold the office again, not even after a break. The principle goes back to the Revolution, with the slogan Sufragio efectivo, no reelección ("Effective suffrage, no re-election"). In Brazil, by contrast, re-election is allowed; Lula has just won one. A sentence about Lula's reelección therefore sounds very different to Mexicans than to Brazilians. en.wikipedia.org

Is 4.4 million "close"? In absolute numbers that sounds like a lot. According to the Diário do Grande ABC, Lula got 64,256,232 votes, Flávio 59,826,041. The difference is 4,430,191 votes, but only about 3.6 percentage points. That is why newspapers call such a result close. PT says apertado, literally "tight, squeezed" — like a shoe that is too tight. ES says cerrado or reñido, "hard-fought." In the second sentence the word is missing on purpose: for "just barely," PT says por pouco, Mexico de milagro or por poquito. And the bus is called el camión in Mexico. dgabc.com.br

It's Sunday, November first. Today's rotation topic is politics, law and health, episode nine. A week ago, on October twenty-fifth, Lula was re-elected in the runoff, with about fifty-one point eight percent of the valid votes against Flávio Bolsonaro. Today there are ten entries, three of them verbs with full conjugation, plus three refreshers.

The three verbs tell the story of election night: to count, to acknowledge, and to concede. In German they are auszählen, anerkennen and einräumen. In Portuguese they are apurar, reconhecer and admitir, and in Mexican Spanish escrutar, reconocer and admitir.

The false friend of the day is apurar. In Brazil, apurar os votos means to count the votes. But in Mexico, apúrate means: hurry up. And no te apures means: don't worry.

With anerkennen, the German participle gets no extra ge: it's anerkannt. With reconhecer and reconocer, the spelling changes: eu reconheço, yo reconozco.

Then the words that follow: the election result, defeat, re-election, and close. In Portuguese, a close result is apertado, in Spanish cerrado. The margin was about four point four million votes. In Mexico, re-election of the president is forbidden; he serves exactly one sexenio of six years.

A second false friend: in Mexico, rendir protesta doesn't mean to protest, it means to take the oath of office. In Portuguese, that's tomar posse.

Finally, two refreshers from July. The runoff is o segundo turno in Brazil, a segunda volta in Portugal, and la segunda vuelta in Mexico. And the term of office is o mandato. Tomorrow is Finados, and the topic switches to economy and finance.
