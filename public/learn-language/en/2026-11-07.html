Three Verbs at the Stove: köcheln, abgießen, pürieren

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br and conjugator.reverso.net

Why “köcheln” is a little “kochen”: Imagine German attaching a small tag -eln to a verb. The action then becomes smaller and gentler. kochen (to boil) turns into köcheln (to simmer): the water only bubbles quietly. tropfen becomes tröpfeln, husten becomes hüsteln. Often an umlaut joins in: o becomes ö. Portuguese and Spanish do not have this trick with verbs. They say instead how big the flame is: ferver em fogo baixo, hervir a fuego lento — “to boil on a low fire”. With nouns, though, you know the trick from Brazil: o cafezinho is the little coffee.
Verb DE weak, verb in -eln → ich köchle, köchelte, geköchelt, perfect with haben · EN to simmer, regular · PT-BR ferver (em fogo baixo), regular · ES-MX hervir (a fuego lento), irregular (e→ie, e→i)
köcheln

Die Bohnen für die Feijoada köcheln seit dem Morgen.

Lass die Soße noch zehn Minuten köcheln.

Gestern hat die Tomatensoße eine Stunde geköchelt.

to simmer

The beans for the feijoada have been simmering since the morning.

Let the sauce simmer for another ten minutes.

Yesterday the tomato sauce simmered for an hour.

ferver em fogo baixo

O feijão da feijoada está fervendo em fogo baixo desde cedo.

Deixa o molho ferver em fogo baixo mais dez minutos.

Ontem o molho de tomate ferveu uma hora em fogo baixo.

hervir a fuego lento

Los frijoles para la feijoada hierven a fuego lento desde la mañana.

Deja hervir la salsa a fuego lento otros diez minutos.

Ayer la salsa de jitomate hirvió una hora a fuego lento.

Beans, tomatoes — two countries, two words The sentences hold two word pairs you should know. The bean is o feijão in Brazil, el frijol in Mexico. The tomato is o tomate in Brazil. In Mexico people say el jitomate in everyday speech, a word from Nahuatl. Also note the imperative deixa in the second PT sentence: the formal form would be deixe, but in the kitchen in Brazil you almost always hear deixa. Mexico uses deja, the normal tú form.

Verb DE strong (ie–o–o), separable → goss ab, abgegossen, perfect with haben · EN to drain, regular · PT-BR escorrer, regular · ES-MX escurrir, regular
abgießen

Gieß die Nudeln ab, bevor sie zu weich werden.

Ich habe die Kartoffeln zu früh abgegossen.

Wer gießt das Wasser ab, du oder ich?

to drain

Drain the pasta before it gets too soft.

I drained the potatoes too early.

Who's draining the water, you or me?

escorrer

Escorre o macarrão antes que ele fique mole demais.

Eu escorri as batatas cedo demais.

Quem escorre a água, você ou eu?

Escurre la pasta antes de que quede demasiado blanda.

Escurrí las papas demasiado pronto.

¿Quién escurre el agua, tú o yo?

“escorrer” — the water runs off In German you pour the water off (ab), that is, away from the pot. Portuguese and Spanish see it from the water's side: escorrer and escurrir mean “to let run off, to let drip”. That is why the word also fits dishes: escorrer a louça is letting them drip dry after washing. In the first PT sentence, note o macarrão: in Brazil that means any kind of noodle, not just macaroni. And in the second sentence, the potato: Brazil says a batata, Mexico la papa — never patata as in Spain.

to puree

I puree the bean soup in the blender.

We puree the vegetables for the baby.

“bater” — beating instead of pureeing Brazil has no everyday kitchen verb of its own for to puree. You beat the food in the blender: bater no liquidificador. You use the same bater for cream and for egg whites. Mexico has licuar for it, matching the appliance la licuadora. Without licuar there is hardly any salsa there. Watch the words for vegetables: in Brazil os legumes are the cooked vegetables, in Mexico people say las verduras.

In the Kitchen: Wooden Spoon, Jam, Lunch Box

Three new words — and a false friend that came from Portugal

How the quince became jam: Imagine a word as a suitcase that goes on a journey. Along the way you unpack something and pack something new. The Portuguese marmelada was originally a paste made from marmelo, the quince. Duden traces the German word back to exactly this. In Germany it became any sweet fruit spread. In Portugal and Brazil, marmelada stayed the firm quince paste. English took a third path: marmalade there is only an orange or citrus spread. The EU even adopted this English view for the German label. For you that means: in a Brazilian supermarket you look for geleia.
Noun DE m, pl. unchanged · EN the wooden spoon · PT-BR a colher de pau, f · ES-MX la cuchara de madera, f
der Kochlöffel

Rühr die Bohnen ab und zu mit dem Kochlöffel um.

Der Kochlöffel liegt in der Schublade neben dem Herd.

the wooden spoon

Stir the beans now and then with the wooden spoon.

The wooden spoon is in the drawer next to the stove.

a colher de pau

Mexe o feijão de vez em quando com a colher de pau.

A colher de pau está na gaveta do lado do fogão.

la cuchara de madera

Mueve los frijoles de vez en cuando con la cuchara de madera.

La cuchara de madera está en el cajón junto a la estufa.

“mexer” is not “mezclar” The first PT sentence hides a trap for Spanish speakers. The Brazilian mexer means “to stir” and, in everyday speech, also “to touch”: não mexe nisso! means “Don't touch that!”. Mexico says mover or revolver for stirring. And the stove: in Brazil o fogão, in Mexico la estufa. Literally, the colher de pau is a “stick spoon”, just like the German Kochlöffel made of wood.

la mermelada

Order “marmelada” and you get quince paste Priberam explains marmelada briefly as “Doce de marmelos” — a firm paste made of quinces that you cut into slices. Brazil calls the bread spread a geleia. Dicio describes it as “Preparado de frutas”, which turns firm and yet soft and wobbly. Spanish is your friend here: la mermelada is the normal spread in Mexico. In English use jam. According to Duden, under an EU regulation even in German strictly only the citrus spread is called “Marmelade”. priberam.org · dicio.com.br · duden.de

marmita, Henkelmann, tóper Dicio describes the marmita as “Panela de lata ou de outro metal, com tampa, que serve para o transporte de comida”. That is almost exactly the German Henkelmann from the Ruhr region. In Brazil, though, marmita also stands for the food inside: whoever says “levo marmita” brings lunch from home. Mexico thinks of the plastic container. The DEM lists it as tupper and writes: “Se pronuncia tóper y también se escribe así” — a container with a tight-fitting lid for food. dicio.com.br · dem.colmex.mx

“sobremesa” — dessert in Brazil, chat in Mexico Here you stumble between your two languages. In Brazil a sobremesa is the dessert. Spanish has the word too, but it means something else. The DEM explains la sobremesa as “Conversación que tiene lugar después de haber comido” — the conversation when everyone stays at the table, full. A “café de sobremesa” is therefore the coffee afterwards, not a dessert. Mexico calls the dessert itself el postre. dem.colmex.mx

Quick refresher — one word, two body parts On Oct 31 the cinnamon came with the soul bread. Today a bonus: a canela is also the shin in Brazil. A Brazilian idiom from the kitchen — and its Mexican counterpart

IDIOM Idiom PT-BR and ES-MX colloquial · only PT has an image from the kitchen
etwas in die Länge ziehen

Der Vortrag hatte zehn Minuten Inhalt, den Rest hat er nur in die Länge gezogen.

Komm zum Punkt und zieh es nicht so in die Länge!

Filling sausage, throwing a roll The image is a sausage into which you stuff cheap filling so that it looks full. Dicio explains encher linguiça as “Preencher espaços vazios com coisas sem importância” and lists enrolar and embromar as synonyms. The Mexican counterpart needs no kitchen. The DEM calls rollo a long, heavy and boring topic and lists “echar rollo” as a fixed expression, with the example “Nos echó un rollo de dos horas y no dijo nada”. Careful with the German: um den heißen Brei herumreden sounds like the kitchen, but means dodging the topic. dicio.com.br · dem.colmex.mx

It's Saturday, November seventh. Today's rotating topic is food, cooking and restaurants, installment eleven. In Brazil, Saturday is feijoada day, and rain is in the forecast for the afternoon. A good day to be in the kitchen. Today there are ten entries: three verbs with full conjugation, and three reviews from last Saturday.

The first verb is köcheln, to simmer. That little ending, e-l-n, turns kochen, to boil, into a gentle boil in German. Portuguese and Spanish have no verb of their own for it. They say ferver em fogo baixo and hervir a fuego lento, which means boiling on a low flame. Watch out for the Spanish hervir: it's yo hiervo, but nosotros hervimos, and in the past, él hirvió.

The second verb is abgießen, to drain. It's a strong, separable verb: ich gieße ab, ich goss ab, ich habe abgegossen. In Portuguese it's escorrer, in Spanish escurrir. Both are regular. And remember the potato: in Brazil it's a batata, in Mexico la papa.

The third verb is pürieren, to puree, with the participle püriert, with no ge at all. In Brazil you beat the food in the blender: bater no liquidificador. In Mexico people say licuar, to match the licuadora. According to Reverso, both stress patterns are correct there, licúo and licuo.

Now, two false friends. The Portuguese marmelada is quince paste. Duden says our German word Marmelade comes from exactly that. Brazil calls the jam you spread on bread a geleia, and Mexico calls it la mermelada. And the cake: in Mexico, la torta is a sandwich roll. The cake there is el pastel, and in Brazil it's o bolo.

Typically Brazilian is a marmita, the lunch box with a lid for your midday meal. In Mexico you take el tóper, the plastic container. Add to that the wooden spoon: a colher de pau and la cuchara de madera.

As a refresher, three words come back from October thirty-first. Dessert is a sobremesa in Brazil and el postre in Mexico. Careful: the Spanish sobremesa is the conversation after the meal. Cinnamon is la canela in both languages, and in Brazil a canela is also your shin.

To finish, an idiom from Brazil: encher linguiça, literally filling sausage. It means padding something out with filler. In Mexico people say echar rollo. Enjoy your meal. Tomorrow the topic switches to politics, law and health.
