Feriadão Vocabulary: sair, correr, o sinistro

Friday, November 13 — rotation theme Travel & Traffic, installment twelve. In exactly one week, on Friday, November 20, Brazil has a public holiday: the Dia Nacional de Zumbi e da Consciência Negra. That makes a Feriadão from Friday through Sunday. Mexico is faster: there, the Revolution holiday has been moved to Monday, November 16, and the long weekend starts tomorrow. And on November 10, Paraná launched a campaign to halve the number of road deaths in ten years. So today is about setting off, speeding and crashes. 10 entries, including 2 verbs with full conjugation, among them 3 refreshers.

  • FALSE FRIEND o sinistro ≠ sinister — in Brazilian official texts, o sinistro is the crash, not “the creepy thing.” The adjective sinistro, on the other hand, really does mean “frightening.” dicio.com.br
  • BR-PT o feriadão — the long holiday weekend, with the augmentative ending -ão: the “big holiday.” Mexico says el puente. dicio.com.br
  • CONJUGATION ich bin gerast ≠ das Publikum hat gerast — for fast driving, German rasen forms the perfect with sein; in the sense of “to rage,” with haben. duden.de
  • CONJUGATION yo salgo, yo saldré — Mexican salir has a g in the first person and a d in the future. Brazilian sair has an i instead: eu saio. reverso.net · conjugacao.com.br
  • PHRASE enforcar o dia — that is what Brazilians call taking a bridge day: “hanging” a workday between a holiday and the weekend. This time you don't need it, because the 20th is itself a Friday. dicio.com.br
  • PHRASE a alta temporada ↔ la temporada alta — the same two words in reverse order: Brazil puts the adjective first, Mexico puts it last.

Two verbs for the road: aufbrechen, rasen

2 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, duden.de, conjugacao.com.br and conjugator.reverso.net

Why “sein” or “haben” depends on the meaning: Picture the German perfect tense as two doors. Through the sein door go verbs where you move from one place to another: Ich bin nach Curitiba gerast. Through the haben door go verbs where you stay in your spot and do something: Das Stadion hat vor Begeisterung gerast. So the same word can take a different door depending on its meaning. aufbrechen works the same way: Wir sind früh aufgebrochen (set off), but Jemand hat das Auto aufgebrochen (opened by force). Portuguese and Spanish have only one door: ter and haber. That's why this choice is easy for native speakers and hard for learners.
Verb DE strong (e–a–o), separable → brach auf, aufgebrochen, perfect with sein (in the sense “to set off”) · EN to set off, irregular (set–set–set) · PT-BR sair, irregular · ES-MX salir, irregular (salgo, saldré)
aufbrechen

Wir brechen um fünf Uhr auf, dann sind wir vor dem Verkehr durch Ourinhos.

Sie sind früh aufgebrochen, um das lange Wochenende am Meer zu verbringen.

Wann brichst du nächsten Freitag auf?

to set off

We set off at five, so we're through Ourinhos before the traffic.

They set off early to spend the long weekend at the beach.

When are you setting off next Friday?

A gente sai às cinco, aí passa por Ourinhos antes do trânsito.

salir

Salimos a las cinco y así pasamos Ourinhos antes del tráfico.

Salieron temprano para pasar el puente en la playa.

¿A qué hora sales el próximo viernes?

“aufbrechen” — to set off or to break open Verbformen explains aufbrechen as “sich auf den Weg machen, sich entfernen” (to head out, to leave) and gives the sentence “Sie sind früh aufgebrochen.” But the word has a second life: ein Auto aufbrechen means to break into it by force — and then it takes haben. PT and ES simply use “to go out” for setting off: sair and salir. That is also where the word for the exit further down comes from: a saída, la salida. If you like it formal, Brazilians say partir; in everyday speech you almost always hear sair. verbformen.de

“rasen” — to speed or to rage The Duden separates two meanings. The first: “mit hoher Geschwindigkeit [irgendwohin] fahren” (to drive [somewhere] at high speed), perfect with ist, as in “ein Auto kam um die Ecke gerast.” The second: “von Sinnen, außer sich sein; […] toben” (to be out of one's mind, beside oneself; […] to rage), perfect with hat, as in “das Publikum raste [vor Begeisterung].” Verbformen lists haben as the default and notes that forms with sein exist too. So for the road, it's: ich bin gerast. In the third PT sentence it says Não corre instead of Não corra: in conversation, Brazilians often use the indicative form for commands. Mexican words in the ES sentences: el tráiler for the semi truck and agarrar for “to catch.” duden.de · verbformen.de

The Feriadão: holiday, long weekend, exit

One new word and two refreshers — for the week leading up to November 20

Why Mexico moves the holiday and Brazil doesn't: Imagine a calendar where some holidays sit on wheels. In Mexico, labor law doesn't prescribe a fixed date for some holidays, but a weekday: for the Revolution, “el tercer lunes de noviembre,” the third Monday in November. So every year a long weekend comes out of it automatically — in 2026, November 14 to 16. In Brazil, holidays are fixed. If one falls on a Sunday, like the Proclamação da República on November 15, it's simply gone. November 20 is lucky in that respect: it's a Friday, and the weekend attaches itself.

This time the holiday weekend already starts on Friday.

feriadão and puente — two images for three days off The Dicio lists feriadão as a “substantivo masculino” and writes: “Feriadão é uma palavra derivada de feriar.” The ending -ão makes things big, as in o paredão (the big wall). A citation from the Folha reads: “Confira o que abre e fecha neste feriadão.” Mexico says el puente, the bridge. Expansión writes that the day of rest will be moved to Monday, November 16, in 2026; schools are additionally off on Friday, the 13th. We have this word today only from the press, not from a dictionary. According to Bloomberg Línea, the Puente coincides with the Buen Fin, Mexico's big discount weekend. dicio.com.br · expansion.mx · bloomberglinea.com

Quick refresher — and why nobody is “enforcing” this time On Nov 2, the holiday came up with the Finados weekend. According to Serasa Experian, November 20 is a national holiday under Lei 14.759/2023. On November 15, the page writes: “Ela não passa automaticamente para a segunda-feira.” For the bridge day, Brazil has a drastic verb: enforcar, literally “to hang.” The Dicio explains the meaning like this: “Deixar de comparecer às aulas, ao trabalho, em dia útil que caia entre dois feriados ou entre um feriado e um domingo.” The Duden defines the Brückentag (bridge day) as an “zwischen zwei arbeitsfreien Tagen […] liegender einzelner Arbeitstag” (a single workday lying between two days off). This time there is no such day, because the 20th is itself a Friday. serasaexperian.com.br · dicio.com.br · duden.de

Quick refresher — the word is hidden in the verb On Oct 30, the exit came up with the Finados return trip. Today you see where it comes from: a saída is what happens when you sair, la salida what happens when you salir. In German, the door for pedestrians is der Ausgang and the one for cars is die Ausfahrt. PT and ES need only one word for both. Mexican expression in the second sentence: pasarse la salida means “to drive past the exit.”

Arriving safely: crash, road fatality, average speed

Three new words from Paraná's new road-safety campaign

FALSE FRIEND Noun DE m, pl. Unfälle · EN the crash · PT-BR o sinistro (de trânsito), m — official language; in everyday speech o acidente · ES-MX el accidente, m

After a crash, the road is often closed for hours.

Luckily, nobody was hurt in the crash.

Por sorte, ninguém se feriu no acidente.

el accidente

Después de un accidente, la carretera suele quedar cerrada por horas.

“sinistro” in a headline is not a horror movie If you think in English, you read sinistro as sinister, “creepy.” As an adjective, that's right: the Dicio gives “Temível; que provoca medo; que é assustador.” As a noun, though, o sinistro is the misfortune itself: “Desastre; circunstância que provoca perda, dor ou morte,” and in legal terms the insurance loss. Brazilian authorities now like to write sinistro de trânsito instead of acidente. For example, Lieutenant Colonel Gustavo Zancan said at the campaign launch in Paraná, according to Detran-PR: “Precisamos reduzir o número de óbitos e sinistros.” In conversation you still say o acidente — that's why the second sentence uses the everyday word. dicio.com.br · detran.pr.gov.br

An adjective as a noun — and a woman for every man On November 10, Paraná launched the “Movimento Paraná pela Vida – Rodovias Mais Seguras.” According to Detran-PR, the PRF, the BPRv highway police, the ANTT agency and eight concessionaires are taking part; the goal is to cut the number of road fatalities in half within ten years. How badly that is needed was shown by Finados: Band reported “11 mortes e 81 acidentes” on the federal highways in Paraná. Grammar: Verkehrstote is an adjective used as a noun. That's why it's der Verkehrstote but ein Verkehrstoter — like der Deutsche, ein Deutscher. In PT and ES, a vítima and la víctima are always feminine, even when a man has died. detran.pr.gov.br · band.com.br

The speed camera that calculates between two points In September, the Portal do Trânsito reported on ANTT tests of radar de velocidade média in eight states. For Paraná, it names BR-163, BR-277, BR-376, PR-151, PR-444 and PR-862. The test is “exclusivamente técnico, educativo e experimental” and cannot trigger a penalty on its own: “não poderá, por si só, gerar autuação ou penalidade.” So the test was not meant to trigger any fines. On Sept 21, the Diário do Transporte reported: “ANTT suspende 15 testes de velocidade média em rodovias.” Which stretches that affected and where things stand today, we could not verify. Linguistically: Brazil says média, Mexico prefers promedio in everyday speech — both mean “average.” portaldotransito.com.br · diariodotransporte.com.br

To finish: peak travel season and a snail's pace

One new word and an idiom from Oct 30 to refresh

Noun DE f, pl. -en · EN peak travel season · PT-BR a alta temporada, f · ES-MX la temporada alta, f
die Hauptreisezeit

In der Hauptreisezeit sind die Busse nach Florianópolis schnell ausgebucht.

Wir buchen die Unterkunft lieber vor der Hauptreisezeit.

peak travel season

In peak travel season, the buses to Florianópolis sell out fast.

We'd rather book the place before peak travel season.

a alta temporada

Na alta temporada, os ônibus para Florianópolis lotam rápido.

É melhor a gente reservar a pousada antes da alta temporada.

la temporada alta

En temporada alta, los autobuses a Florianópolis se llenan rápido.

Mejor reservamos el hospedaje antes de la temporada alta.

Same words, different order PT and ES build the term from the same parts, but the other way around. Brazil says a alta temporada, with the adjective first, like a fixed brand. Mexico says la temporada alta, with the adjective last, as is usual in Spanish. The opposite is a baixa temporada and la temporada baja. German glues everything into one word: Haupt-reise-zeit. New in the second sentence: a pousada is the small guesthouse in Brazil; Mexico generally says el hospedaje.

Quick refresher — the opposite of speeding On Oct 30, the snail's pace came up behind the toll booth. Today it is the counterpart to rasen and correr from above. Brazil takes the tortoise, a tartaruga; Mexico takes the wheel that has just moved forward one turn: a vuelta de rueda. In the second sentence you see the “better … than” pattern: melhor … do que in PT, mejor … que in ES. Have a good trip into the Feriadão.

Today there are ten entries, including two verbs with full conjugation and three refreshers.

The first verb is aufbrechen, meaning to set off. It forms the perfect with sein: wir sind früh aufgebrochen, we set off early. In Portuguese you simply say sair, and in Mexican Spanish, salir. Watch the forms: eu saio in Portuguese, yo salgo and yo saldré in Spanish.

The second verb is rasen. For fast driving, it's ich bin gerast. In the sense of raging, though, it's das Publikum hat gerast. In both Brazil and Mexico, people say correr. And in English: to speed, with the past tense sped.

Now the long weekend. In Brazil it's o feriadão, the big holiday. In Mexico it's el puente, the bridge. There, the Revolution holiday has been moved to Monday, November sixteenth, and the long weekend starts tomorrow. In Brazil, by contrast, November fifteenth falls on a Sunday, with no Monday off. For a bridge day, Brazil has the verb enforcar. This time nobody needs it, because the twentieth is itself a Friday.

Today's false friend is o sinistro. In official texts it means a crash, not something creepy. On November tenth, Paraná launched a campaign to cut road deaths in half within ten years. A road fatality is a vítima fatal in Portuguese and a víctima mortal in Spanish. And average speed is a velocidade média in Brazil and la velocidad promedio in Mexico.
