Three Kitchen Verbs: to salt, to chop, to season

3 verbs with full conjugation · DE · EN · PT-BR · ES-MX · cross-checked against verbformen.de, conjugacao.com.br and conjugator.reverso.net

Why “salgar” suddenly becomes “salguei”: Imagine the letter g has two voices. Before a, o and u it sounds hard, like in “garden.” Before e and i it sounds soft in Portuguese, like the j in “journal.” With salgar, though, the g should always stay hard. So the spelling slips a silent u in between: salguei, que eu salgue. It is the same with c: before e it sounds like s. To keep picar hard, you write piquei and, in Spanish, piqué. The sound does not change, only the spelling. So these verbs are irregular only on paper.
Verb DE weak → salzte, but participle usually gesalzen (rarely gesalzt), perfect with haben · EN to salt, regular · PT-BR salgar, spelling change g→gu before e · ES-MX salar, regular

Only salt the water once it's boiling.

Salt in the price — gesalzen and salgado The old participle gesalzen lives on in an expression that fits the topic exactly. The Duden explains gesalzen as “(of prices, bills, etc.) very high” and gives “gesalzene Preise” as an example. Portuguese uses the same image: the Dicio lists the sense “Exagerado” for salgado, with the example “preço um tanto salgado.” So if you groan about the PF further down, you say: Tá salgado! In the first PT sentence, salgue appears with gu — that is the polite imperative for você, which is the same as the subjunctive. duden.de · dicio.com.br

“picar” — to cut or to bite The Dicio gives the kitchen sense of picar first: “Culinária. Cortar em pequenos pedaços.” A few lines further down it says: “Morder, quando se trata de insetos ou répteis.” That is why in Brazil, after a night outdoors, you say: O pernilongo me picou — the mosquito bit me. O pernilongo is the typical Brazilian word for the mosquito. In the third sentence you can already see the false friend of the day: parsley is a salsinha (or a salsa) in Brazil and el perejil in Mexico. More on that under the sauce. dicio.com.br

Verb DE weak, regular → würzte, gewürzt, perfect with haben · EN to season, regular · PT-BR temperar, regular · ES-MX sazonar, regular

“temperar” sounds like temperature — but means flavor If you think in German, you hear “temperature” in temperar. In Brazilian cooking it simply means “to season,” and the noun for it is o tempero (see below). Mexico says sazonar. In the third sentence, PT and ES put the object first: A picanha a gente tempera …, La picaña la sazonamos …. That stresses what you are talking about. In Spanish the object must then come back as la. In Portuguese it may be left out. Coarse salt is sal grosso in Brazil and sal de grano in Mexico. conjugacao.com.br · reverso.net

The Lunch Special for R$31.90

One new word and a review from 11/07 — eating out and the lunch box from home

What a “prato feito index” measures: Imagine you buy the same plate in many cities every month: rice, beans, a piece of meat, salad and a side dish. Then you write down what it costs. If the price rises, you notice that eating out is getting more expensive — with no complicated statistics. That is exactly what the index of the economic research unit of the Faculdade do Comércio (FAC-SP) in São Paulo does. According to Itatiaia, the plate cost R$31.90 on the national average in June 2026: 5.4% more than in March and 7.2% more than in January. That is the latest figure we found. Anyone who pays this price every day soon does the math and packs the marmita again.

PF and comida corrida — two fixed plates Itatiaia describes the PF as “a refeição composta por arroz, feijão, proteína, salada e guarnição.” Prato feito literally means “made plate”: you do not choose from the menu, the plate comes as it is. According to Itatiaia, prices differ by region; we did not check the individual regional figures ourselves. In Mexico, people eat the comida corrida at midday in the fonda. Chilango calls it “un menú de precio fijo y accesible.” According to the site, it often includes “sopa, arroz o pasta, un guisado a elegir, agua del día y a veces hasta postre.” The word is documented here from the press, not from a dictionary. The German Mittagstisch is a restaurant’s offer, not the table itself. itatiaia.com.br · chilango.com

From verb to noun o tempero comes directly from temperar, just as das Gewürz comes from würzen. In everyday Brazilian use, tempero is broader than “spice”: it means anything that gives flavor, including fresh herbs, garlic and onion. The market is a feira in Brazil and el tianguis in Mexico; the big sack there is el costal. These word pairs come from our own language knowledge and were not looked up in a dictionary today.

Order “salsa” in Brazil and you get greens With very good Spanish the mistake is easy to make: you want sauce and say salsa. In Brazil, though, a salsa is a plant. The Portuguese Wikipedia opens its article with “A salsa, salsinha ou perrexil” and gives the name Petroselinum crispum — parsley. In everyday speech you mostly hear the diminutive salsinha. The sauce is o molho; the tomato sauce for the pasta is o molho de tomate. And one more Mexican word in the first sentence: picoso means “spicy.” Brazil says apimentado, from a pimenta. pt.wikipedia.org

One word, three kitchens A massa and la masa look the same but live in different kitchens. In Brazil massa also means “noodles” in general: Hoje tem massa means “there is pasta today.” In Mexico, masa first brings to mind the corn dough for tortillas. Watch the gender: German says der Teig, PT and ES say a and la. Sticky is grudento in Brazil and pegajoso in Mexico.

Soft for pillows and meat PT and ES use the same word for tender meat as for a soft pillow: macio and suave. German and English separate the two: a pillow is weich (soft), meat is zart (tender). Because a carne is feminine, the PT sentence has macia. In Mexico you also hear tierno, and when raving, the diminutive suavecita. Today the weather suits it: outside at the grill, as long as it stays dry.
