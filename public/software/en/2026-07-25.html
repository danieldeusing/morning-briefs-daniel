€890 million: Brussels collects from DMA gatekeeper Google for the first time

On July 23, the EU Commission issued two fine decisions against Google: €460 million and €430 million, €890 million combined. The first part concerns self-preferencing in Search, the second the anti-steering lock in the Play Store. It's the highest penalty under the Digital Markets Act so far — and the counterpart to the specification decisions from July 16, which we covered on Wednesday: there it was the "how," here it's the price of not doing it.

Self-preferencing & anti-steering: Self-preferencing means: whoever owns the stage puts its own product front and center — Google's own comparison and specialized services rank higher in Search than competitors', regardless of who's actually better. Anti-steering is the other half: in a department store that takes a 30% cut, no stall is allowed to hang a sign saying "it's cheaper at my own shop." That's exactly the sign Google bans in the Play Store — and exactly what the Commission is objecting to.

For developers, the second part is the tangible one: according to the Commission, app developers distributing through Google Play must be allowed to tell customers about alternative, often cheaper offers free of charge and direct them there. The complaint is that Google prevents developers from "communicating freely, promoting offers, and concluding contracts through distribution channels of their choice." Anyone running an app with a subscription model is talking about the difference between a 15–30% store cut and running their own payment flow.

My take: the amount is pocket change for Alphabet, the behavioral remedy isn't. What matters isn't the wire transfer but the implementation — after a comparable requirement in the App Store, Apple initially introduced a link fee, effectively rescuing its cut through the back door. So the test case is the phrase "free of charge" in the decision. If Google's implementation this fall includes a "facilitation fee," this was only round one.

Sourcing On its decision page, the Commission only states that Google was ordered to "bring the non-compliance to an end" — without a specific deadline in days. Trade press has "60 days" and periodic penalty payments of up to 5% of worldwide daily turnover circulating; we couldn't verify either against the primary source, so we're leaving those figures out.

Certighost: the ordinary domain user who suddenly becomes a Domain Controller

The most interesting security finding of the week comes from a corner many consider settled: Active Directory Certificate Services. CVE-2026-54121 (CVSS 8.8, "Improper Authorization," CWE-285) lets a low-privileged domain user obtain a certificate issued to a Domain Controller's identity. The Kerberos credential gained from that lets an attacker pull the krbtgt secret via DCSync — and at that point, the domain is structurally lost.

AD CS, DCSync, krbtgt: AD CS is the company's own ID office: it issues digital IDs (certificates) for users and servers. DCSync is the trick of posing to that ID office as a different Domain Controller and saying "hand over your password database for a sync check" — servers routinely do this among themselves. And krbtgt is the master key: every login ticket in the domain is derived from it. Whoever holds it can impersonate anyone, at any time — and a password reset isn't enough; the key has to be rotated twice.

Timeline: Microsoft closed the hole on July 14 with the July updates; the full proof-of-concept was published ten days later, on July 24. Per The Hacker News, none of the primary sources reviewed had reported in-the-wild exploitation at that point. This sequence — patch first, exploit code after — is the normal case, and exactly the window attackers prefer to work in: the code is out there, but many companies' patch window is still open.

No direct impact on Daniel (no AD in my own stack), but very much on the German client side: anyone developing for a mid-sized company with a Windows domain should ask whether the AD CS role is even needed. Most domains run it half-forgotten, a byproduct of some old VPN or Wi-Fi rollout — and an unused service is the cheapest hardening there is.

Microsoft's explanation for Thursday: the automation cleared out too much

Regarding the major Microsoft 365 and Azure outage on Thursday (07/23), Microsoft has named the cause, per BleepingComputer: a bug in its automated network maintenance system. A maintenance request removed IP routes from more devices than intended — and for the affected paths, the way to the service simply disappeared.

That makes it, three days after the expired SSL certificate at GitHub Actions and the Frankfurt AZ that took CloudFront down globally with it, the third outage in a week with the same signature: not an attack, but operational automation. Withdrawing routes is the normal maintenance procedure — you cleanly pull a device out of traffic before working on it. The bug wasn't in the idea, but in its scope: the automation cast its filter too wide.

The uncomfortable lesson for anyone scripting infrastructure — even at small scale: the most dangerous part of a maintenance script isn't the action, it's selecting the target set. A WHERE clause that's a notch too broad is, in the cloud, the same as a DELETE with no condition. Anyone running such jobs should have them count what they'd touch first, and abort on an unexpected count.

Clop switches targets: now PTC Windchill and FlexPLM

The ransomware group Clop is running a new data-theft extortion campaign against internet-facing instances of PTC Windchill and FlexPLM, per BleepingComputer (07/24). No more encrypting, just stealing and threatening — Clop's pattern since MOVEit.

PLM system: "Product Lifecycle Management" is product development's filing cabinet: CAD models, bills of materials, supplier contracts, revision histories. For a machine builder, that's literally where the product lives before it exists — which makes it a better extortion target than accounting.

The pattern is boringly predictable by now, and still effective: find a widely deployed enterprise system that, first, holds masses of sensitive files, and second, is hanging directly off the internet out of convenience. MOVEit was file transfer, Cleo was file transfer, Oracle EBS was ERP, now it's PLM. Anyone running such an application can skip the expensive detection debate and instead answer the mundane question of why it's publicly reachable at all without a VPN or reverse-proxy gate.

RefluXFS follow-up: the 7.8 isn't an NVD number

Yesterday this brief carried CVSS 7.8 for CVE-2026-64600. That attribution deserves a correction: the NVD published the entry on July 23, last modified it on July 24 — and, checked directly this morning, still shows "NVD assessment not yet provided" for both CVSS v3.1 and v4.0. So the 7.8 comes from secondary coverage around the Qualys disclosure, not from the NVD.

In practice that doesn't change the assessment — local privilege escalation to root, not a remote attack, working PoC — but it does change how you should pass the number along. Anyone entering scores into a ticket system should note "score provisional, source secondary press" here, rather than claiming an NVD authority that doesn't exist yet. NVD re-scoring routinely shifts values by several tenths.

The recommended action remains Qualys's: apply the kernel patch and reboot fully — there's no live-patching-free mitigation. We had no knowledge of a CISA KEV entry as of this morning; the CISA site still returns 403 on direct access, so we can only say that with a caveat.

GitLab 19.2: scan schedules become policy, not a request

GitLab released version 19.2 on July 16. From the release post, for non-AI purposes, one change is the most relevant: Scheduled Pipeline Execution Policies are GA. You define a schedule once, in a security policy project, and enforce it across every project in scope.

Sounds like a minor detail, but it's the difference between "we have a policy" and "the policy actually runs." The classic alternative is a per-repo cron entry that dies at the first fork, and its absence goes unnoticed because not running doesn't throw an error. Anyone who has to prove to auditors that every repo gets scanned weekly — and that's increasingly landing on suppliers via NIS2 and CRA supply-chain questionnaires — finally has an answer here that isn't "trust us."

Also new: Dependency Scanning Auto-Remediation in beta — GitLab now proposes the upgrade MR itself for vulnerable dependencies it finds. I'd treat this as beta and not set it to automerge; a patch-level bump in a transitive dependency is exactly the spot where a compromised version can sneak in unnoticed. (The release's AI-facing parts — Duo CLI and Custom Flows, both GA — belong in ai-dev territory; mentioned here just for reference.)

Two deadlines in the window: today and Friday

Today (07/25) the CISA KEV deadline for the SharePoint deserialization flaw CVE-2026-50522 expires. It's directly binding only for US federal agencies, but it's the best early indicator available of what's actually being exploited right now — anyone running SharePoint on-prem has today as the last comfortable day.

Friday (07/31) marks the end of the grace period during which the BSI tolerates late NIS2 registrations. Not an obligation of my own as a solo dev in Brazil — but very much a supplier-side topic: registered entities pass their security requirements down the chain, and starting in August, questions about reporting channels and patch deadlines will be showing up in German clients' questionnaires.
