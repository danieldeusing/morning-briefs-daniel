Nextcloud's website went down — likely through the WordPress hole

On Sunday evening, July 19, the web server behind nextcloud.com was attacked. The company says it isolated the affected server immediately and restored it from a backup. The likely way in: nextcloud.com runs on WordPress, and Nextcloud itself couldn't rule out that wp2shell caused the incident — that chain of two core flaws CISA added to the KEV catalog on July 21 over active exploitation.

The most important part first, since it's the question every self-hoster asks: customer and user servers, downloads, and the App Store run on separate infrastructure and, per Nextcloud, were unaffected. What fell was the marketing site, not the software and not the delivery path. heise still calls the communication "clumsy" — the company took a while to say clearly what had actually been affected.[1]

Chained RCE: A single flaw often isn't enough to execute arbitrary code. So attackers combine them: one to get a foot in the door, a second to turn that into execution. Like a break-in where the first trick only cracks the window open and the second reaches an arm through — either one alone looks harmless, together they're standing in the living room. That's why, in a chain, the highest individual score is never the whole truth.

And this is where a correction to our own work belongs. Our July 22 brief listed CVE-2026-63030 at CVSS 9.8. That number is documented — but it isn't the only one in circulation: assessors disagree on the very same CVE. Rapid7 and CISA-ADP rate CVE-2026-63030 — a logic flaw in the REST API's batch processor under /wp-json/batch/v1 — at 7.5, while Tenable and the CVE's CNA rate it at 9.8.[2][3] For the second flaw in the chain, the SQL injection CVE-2026-60137 in the author__not_in parameter, the disagreement runs the other way: Tenable and the CNA list it at 5.9, CISA-ADP at 9.1 (Critical).[3] Per Picus, affected are WordPress versions 6.9.0–6.9.4 and 7.0.0–7.0.1; the fixes ship in 6.9.5 and 7.0.2.[4]

My read: the lesson here isn't in the CVE, it's in the org chart. In almost every organization, a marketing website is an accessory — it belongs to marketing, not to ops, it's in no runbook, and its WordPress gets updated whenever someone remembers. That's exactly why it's the softest reachable point of an otherwise security-conscious company. Anyone running their own product website should put it on the same inventory list as the production servers today — or serve it statically and remove the problem structurally.

Nextcloud's other front: 360,000 records left exposed

The website story isn't the only one this month. It was publicly reported on July 9 that a Nextcloud Elasticsearch database had been sitting on the internet without access controls — though researchers had actually found it back on May 18, and it was closed within two days of being reported to Nextcloud, meaning already by late May. The report, in other words, trailed the actual event by weeks. It held around 360,000 entries in 8 GB, including customer and partner information, employee data, and company contracts.

Nextcloud says regarding the incident that no case is known in which the data was misused. The same separation as above applies here too: "Other Nextcloud servers belonging to our customers, partners, or other users are not affected by this issue."

Two incidents, made public within three weeks of each other, both outside the product, both in the category of "internal systems nobody ever treated as an attack surface." A search index is convenient: dump data in, find it fast, worry about access control later. In practice, "later" often means "never" — because the thing sits on the internal network, until someone pushes it to the cloud and the internal network turns out to be the internet.

Langflow: on the KEV catalog twice in three weeks

Langflow is a builder where you assemble and run workflows visually. We don't care what's inside those workflows — we care about the server running them, and that server has landed in CISA's catalog of actively exploited flaws twice in three weeks.

Round one: CVE-2026-55255, an authorization flaw in the /api/v1/responses endpoint, CVSS 8.4, added July 7 with a three-day federal remediation deadline — by July 10. That's extreme even by KEV standards. Sysdig's threat research team observed exploitation as early as June 25; attackers ran other people's flows and harvested the API keys and credentials embedded inside them.[6][7]

IDOR: "Insecure Direct Object Reference" — the server checks whether you're logged in, but not whether you own the object whose ID you're sending. Like a hotel front desk that hands over a key to anyone who names a room number, without checking the guest book. Here, the ID of someone else's flow was enough to run it — secrets someone else had stashed inside included.

Round two: CVE-2026-0770, CVSS 9.8, unauthenticated code execution with root privileges via exec_globals — no login, no precondition. Added to the KEV on July 21, exploited according to KEVIntel as early as June 27, with over 220 exploitation attempts from 64 distinct source IPs before it even landed on the KEV.[8]

Sources disagree Our two sources give different fixed versions for CVE-2026-55255 — one says "1.9.1 or newer," the other "1.9.2 and newer." We couldn't resolve that. Anyone running Langflow should reasonably take the higher figure and check the project's own release notes.

The point for this brief is a software point, not an AI point: a flow runner, by design, executes code the user described — it's an eval() with a web UI, and it deserves to be treated like a CI runner, meaning isolated and without long-lived credentials sitting in plaintext. The more expensive damage is the harvested API keys, not the server: the flaw gets patched, a leaked key stays valid until someone rotates it.

€550 million for AliExpress — the DSA is aiming at the algorithm

On July 20, the EU Commission hit AliExpress with a fine of €550 million for Digital Services Act violations — about $630 million. That's the highest DSA fine so far; the previous record was held by Temu at €200 million on May 28, 2026. The proceeding has been running since March 2024.[9][10]

DSA risk assessment: The Digital Services Act doesn't just require very large platforms to remove illegal listings once someone reports them. It requires the platform to itself assess in advance which systemic risks its own mechanisms create — and document that. Like a sawmill: it's not enough to bandage the injured; you have to be able to prove you inspected the machine for hazards beforehand.

That's exactly where AliExpress fell short. The four findings: insufficient detection of illegal products, weak sanctions against flagged sellers, a categorization scheme that let sellers dodge compliance checks, and inadequate safeguards against counterfeits. The reasoning is notably technical — the Commission says the platform neither assessed whether it has enough staff for review, nor adequately examined how its recommendation and advertising systems amplify the spread of illegal products.[10]

For developers, that's the real news here: the recommendation algorithm is treated not as a product feature, but as a regulated artifact requiring documented risk analysis. Anyone working on ranking or feed code in Europe is building something subject to review — with a documentation obligation, not just metrics. The size of the fine, meanwhile, shrinks quickly in context: measured against Alibaba's group revenue of roughly €120 billion, the fine is under one percent.[11] The signal matters more than the amount.

Source situation The Commission's official notice at digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu returned only the page shell on fetch (JavaScript-rendered), no text. The figures here come from The Register's coverage, read directly, which draws on the Commission's announcement.

An extension with 314 million installs was reading WhatsApp Web

Adobe closed a flaw in its Acrobat extension for Chrome on July 22 — the add-on that opens PDFs in the browser and, per The Hacker News, sits at over 314 million installs. CVE-2026-48294, CVSS 7.4, classified as universal cross-site scripting with "cross-origin data disclosure." Affected are all versions through 26.5.2.2; it's fixed in 26.5.3.0.[12]

Same-origin policy and UXSS: The basic rule of the web: a site may only read its own data. bank.com can't touch what's sitting in the browser for whatsapp.com — like tenants who aren't allowed into each other's apartments. "Universal XSS" means someone found a master key that overrides this rule for every door. Browser extensions are the most popular place to find one, because they hold that master key by design.

Guardio Labs demonstrated the consequence: the flaw allowed "cross-origin read access to session-bound data" — specifically content from WhatsApp Web: contact names, chat lists, message previews, profile details. Not the whole account, but enough for a precise picture of who's talking to whom about what.[13]

My view: extensions are the worst-monitored privileged code on any developer machine. They update silently, appear in no dependency file, show up in no SBOM, and their permissions get confirmed once at install time and never again. The 314 million isn't trivia, it's the blast radius. Practical step: go through chrome://extensions, drop anything you haven't consciously used in three months, and question "on all sites" access for the rest.

Numbers diverge The install count varies by outlet — 300, 314, or 329 million — and the version numbers differ too (26.5.2.3 instead of 26.5.3.0). We're citing the figures from the one source we read directly — the order of magnitude is solid, the exact number isn't.

Cloud gaming: both giants capitulate on the same day

On July 23, Microsoft and Amazon admitted the same thing — game streaming doesn't stand on its own as a premium product. Microsoft is testing ad-supported free streaming for Xbox Insiders: an ad plays before the session, sessions are capped at one hour, and it only works with games you already own. Amazon is folding Luna into the Prime Video app — as a "Games" tab next to movies, series, and sports, available to Prime members with Fire TV in the US and UK.[14][15]

Both are retreats, even when sold as expansions: what was once a subscription with its own revenue line becomes, in one case, an ad-funded snack, and in the other, a retention feature bolted onto someone else's subscription. The technical reason is unromantic. A movie gets encoded once and delivered to millions; a game needs a dedicated machine per player, running for the entire session. Anything that ties up dedicated hardware per session scales linearly with users — and linearly scaling costs only survive at a correspondingly high price per user. At entertainment-app prices, that math doesn't work, and nothing has changed on that front since Stadia.

The common thread: systems with no owner

Three incidents, one pattern. The website of a data-sovereignty company. A browser extension from a corporation with a mature product security process. A workflow builder that teams spin up to experiment faster. None of these systems is "the product," and that's precisely why none of them has a patch window, a named owner, or a line in the inventory.

no owner→ no inventory→ no patch trigger→ standing-open window

Yesterday this space carried the thesis that the discovery machine has gotten faster than the fix machine. Today, the less comfortable half: even a ready-made fix never reaches the adjacent systems, because nobody has a list to check it against.

So the proposal for this week isn't a patch, it's a list. Fifteen minutes, three columns: system, who notices if it hangs, when it was last updated. Marketing website, status page, wiki, CI runner, browser extensions, that demo tool someone spun up quickly on a VPS. Whatever has no line there has no defense — attackers work through the list either way, whether it exists or not.

Over two hundred twenty attack attempts from sixty-four different addresses were counted before the flaw even made the catalog. The lesson is simple: a workflow runner is, at its core, an eval with a web interface, and it belongs in isolation, like a build runner.
