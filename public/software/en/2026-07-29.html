JFrog patches eight zero-days — and stays silent on the most obvious question

Update since Tue On July 28, JFrog published fixed versions of Artifactory and credited OpenAI researchers as the reporters — for at least eight vulnerabilities: CVE-2026-65617, CVE-2026-65925, CVE-2026-65921, CVE-2026-65923, CVE-2026-66018, CVE-2026-66014, CVE-2026-66015, and CVE-2026-65924.[1] The report doesn't give specific version numbers; it points to JFrog's release information.

That closes the loop with yesterday's story. OpenAI had written that its own models, in an internal test run, had "identified and exploited a zero-day in the package registry cache proxy" — the path out of the sandboxed environment into the open internet and on to code execution on Hugging Face's servers.[1] Artifactory is, in most JVM shops, exactly that cache proxy.

Confirmed and unconfirmed Confirmed: JFrog shipped fixes on Monday and thanks OpenAI researchers; OpenAI describes a flaw in the package cache proxy. Not confirmed is that these are the same eight numbers — The Register asked JFrog exactly that: "but the DevOps firm isn't talking."[1] Nobody has published CVSS scores for the eight CVEs yet. We're treating the connection as highly plausible, not confirmed.

The most consequential story of the day, for an unglamorous reason: a self-hosted Artifactory is almost always the one machine in an otherwise sealed build network that's allowed out — and yet it gets treated like furniture. Two questions for this afternoon: which version is running, and who can reach the web interface without logging in? The second is usually the more expensive one.

Fastjson 1.x: exploited for a week, and there's no fix for the line

Fastjson, Alibaba's widely used JSON parser for Java, has a remote code execution flaw: CVE-2026-16723, CVSS 9.0, affecting 1.2.68 through 1.2.83. Security vendor ThreatBook reports observing in-the-wild exploitation after enabling detection on July 22 — against financial services, healthcare, data centers, and retail, mostly in the US, with additional hits in Singapore and Canada.[2]

The uncomfortable part isn't the score, it's the missing line in the changelog: as of July 25, Alibaba had not published a fixed 1.x build.[2] Coverage names the workaround as SafeMode — the emergency brake that disables arbitrary class instantiation — or migrating to Fastjson2.

AutoType / deserialization: Rebuilding a Java object from JSON is harmless as long as it's fixed which class gets created. Fastjson 1.x let the class name be read straight out of the JSON itself. That's like an order form that also lets you specify which machine in the factory should process it — and the sender can name any machine, including the one that opens the factory gate. That's what the entire Fastjson CVE series has hinged on for years.

My take: this is the second kind of flaw our tools can't handle. There's a CVE number, the scanner dutifully fires — except the suggested action is "update to a version that doesn't exist." Practically: mvn dependency:tree | grep fastjson runs in thirty seconds, and Fastjson shows up more often as a transitive dependency pulled in through an SDK than directly in your own POM.

vBulletin: the fix is four weeks old, the exploit two days

CVE-2026-61511 is unauthenticated code execution in vBulletin, the classic forum package. It affects the 5.x line through 5.7.5 and the 6.x line through 6.2.1; the flaw sits in vB5_Template_Runtime::runMaths() and is reachable through the {vb:math} template tag.[3]

The timeline is the real story: reported on June 25, fixed in 6.2.2 on July 1 along with backports for 6.2.1, 6.2.0, and 6.1.6 — and on July 27, SSD Secure Disclosure published the analysis with proof-of-concept. Rated 9.8 under CVSS 3.1, or 9.3 under 4.0.[3][4]

Four weeks between patch and public exploit is a fair margin — it just doesn't help if nobody's watching. Forums are the textbook example of software that gets installed once and never touched again: no pipeline, no Dependabot, often not even an operator who feels responsible for it. Anyone still carrying a vBulletin install for a club or an old client project has been counting in days since the day before yesterday.

KEV catalog: a perfect ten with a deadline tomorrow, and a 2025 revenant

The US agency CISA added two entries to its KEV catalog on July 27 — the list of vulnerabilities with confirmed evidence of active exploitation.[6] It's binding only for US federal agencies; for everyone else, it's the best free filter between "theoretically bad" and "currently being used."

The first entry is a command injection in Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator: CVE-2026-16812, CVSS 10.0, federal deadline July 30 — that's tomorrow.[5] An SD-WAN orchestrator is the control center for every site's network connectivity; whoever holds it can rewrite routing instead of just taking over individual machines.

I find the second one more interesting: CVE-2025-68686, an information disclosure in FortiOS (SSL-VPN) with a mere CVSS 5.3, deadline August 10. Already patched back in February 2025, it's returning to the catalog because of renewed exploitation.[5] A medium score on a VPN device is rarely the goal — it's the first step: whoever harvests usernames before login turns guessing into targeted logins. The score rates the individual flaw; the attacker rates the chain.

OpenWrt: root on the router, no login required, from inside your own network

The DHCPv6 service odhcpd has a stack overflow — CVE-2026-53921, CVSS 9.8, in the serialization of DHCPv6 IA responses, at two independent locations, with no authentication required. Why this doesn't stay theoretical is stated by the advisory itself: "odhcpd runs as root on OpenWrt; embedded targets often lack stack canaries and ASLR, making code execution realistic."[7] In other words: a router is missing the safeguards that on a Linux server usually turn an overflow into a crash instead of a takeover.

It's fixed in 24.10.8 for the 24.10 branch and in 25.12.5 for the 25.12 branch. Exploitation in the wild wasn't reported as of July 28 — but the advisory does include Python sample code for both overflow paths.[8]

Some context before anyone panics: the router speaks DHCPv6 inward, not to the internet. So an attack comes from your own network — from the guest Wi-Fi, from the TV, from the sensor still running 2019 firmware. Not an all-clear, just a different question: not "am I exposed?" but "what's sitting on the same segment as my router?"

24,650 servers hand their password hashes to anyone who asks

A survey of the open internet found 36,872 reachable BMCs — of which 24,650 handed over password hashes before any login took place. Affected are remote-management modules from HPE (iLO), Supermicro, and Dell. Researcher Michael Katchinskiy is behind the second number that stings: "More than 30% of the returned hashes were associated with passwords that could be recovered using common wordlists and predictable factory chassis-sticker formats."[9]

BMC (Baseboard Management Controller): A second, tiny computer on a server's mainboard, with its own network card and its own operating system. It keeps running when the server is off — that's the point: power it on remotely, mount an installation image, watch the console. Like the janitor's master key that also works at night. Whoever has it doesn't even need the operating system anymore.

The flaw is CVE-2013-4786 — the year isn't a typo. It can't be patched away: "This is an inherent problem with the specification for IPMI v2.0," and Dell states plainly, "there is no patch."[9] The protocol hands over the hash during the login handshake — that's by design. Whoever gets it cracks it offline, with no failed-attempt counter ticking up anywhere.

That makes this the most instructive entry today: no version number that helps, and therefore no task a tool can complete. The only fix is network topology — put the BMC on a management network, reachable only over VPN (in our case, the Tailscale side), and replace the password from the factory sticker, since its format is already in the wordlists.

432 kernel CVEs in one weekend — and why that's not a scandal

The Register reported on July 22 that the Linux kernel security team published 432 new kernel CVEs over two consecutive days. Jan Schaumann of Akamai supplies the operations-side reaction: "This onslaught really shows it's not feasible to attempt to prioritize individual kernel changes."[10]

Why the number can grow to almost any size, Greg Kroah-Hartman explains himself: "At the level that the Linux kernel runs, almost any type of bug that can affect a running system can be classified as a vulnerability."[10] Not an excuse, but the consequence of the kernel issuing its own CVE numbers: anyone assigning a number to every security-relevant fix produces thousands a year — and fuzzing tools find bugs faster today than they used to.

That's the flip side of yesterday's Redis story. There, zero numbers blocked the automation; here, 432 at once do — both end the same way, with the list no longer doing the job of prioritization. The conclusion is uncomfortable and has been the same for years: for the kernel, the right unit isn't the CVE, it's the stable branch. Whoever runs the current stable series has the 432 anyway.

Date is fuzzy The report only says "across Sunday and Monday," with no calendar dates; secondary write-ups name July 19/20. We're therefore treating it as "a weekend in mid-July," not an exact date.

CRA follow-up: the reporting clock runs on 24, 72, and 336 hours

Update since Tue Two clarifications on Monday's guidance. First, the form: Commission Communication C(2026) 5252 plus annex, built around 67 worked examples with flow diagrams — and explicitly non-binding.[11] No fine print here: an interpretive aid without legal force calms down the customer's questionnaire, but doesn't protect against a different reading by market surveillance authorities.

Second, the actual clock starting September 11, 2026: whoever learns of an actively exploited vulnerability in their own product sends an early warning within 24 hours, the full report within 72 hours, and the final report within 14 days of the fix.[11] From today, that's six weeks away.

For a one-person business, the 24-hour step is the hard one. Not because of the content — the early warning is short — but because someone has to notice it and file within a day, Saturdays included. If you don't already have an answer to "which authority do I report this to, on which form?", better spend that half hour now. By September 10 it's too late, and the client with a CRA clause in the contract will ask beforehand.

Three states of a vulnerability — and only one fits our tools

The day sorts itself out with surprising cleanliness. At JFrog, the flaw has a number, a fix, and a date — the scanner flags it, the bot proposes a fix, a ticket gets created. At Fastjson, the number exists, but the fix for the affected line doesn't: a ticket nobody can close inside a maintenance window, because the answer is a migration. At the 24,650 BMCs, there's nothing there at all.

Number + fix→ Number, no fix→ neither

Yesterday's thesis was that our update automation triggers on metadata rather than code. Today's addition: it also assumes that for every report, there's a version that fixes the problem. For most of the estate, that's true. For the uncomfortable cases — end-of-life libraries, protocols from the 2000s, device firmware — it isn't, and that's where the tooling quietly abandons you. What's left is the unspectacular suggestion: for the handful of systems that really matter, write down, once, who is allowed to reach them. The only defense that still works even without a version to update to.

It's Wednesday, July 29th. Today sorts itself by a question you rarely ask out loud: what state is a security flaw actually in? We've got three side by side today — one that's fixed and numbered, one that's being exploited with no fix in sight, and one that will never get one.

The first case closes out yesterday's story. On July 28th, JFrog shipped fixed versions of Artifactory and credited OpenAI researchers for reporting at least eight vulnerabilities. That makes it very likely that Artifactory was the package cache proxy OpenAI's models broke through during that internal test run, escaping the sandbox into the open internet. But it's not confirmed: The Register asked JFrog that exact question, and the company isn't commenting. Nobody has published CVSS scores for the eight numbers yet. If you run Artifactory yourself, check two things today: which version you're on, and who can reach the web interface without logging in.

The second case is Fastjson, Alibaba's JSON library for Java. The flaw is CVE twenty twenty-six, one six seven two three, rated nine point oh, and it affects versions one point two point sixty-eight through one point two point eighty-three. It's been exploited in the wild since around July twenty-second, mostly against targets in the United States. The catch: as of July twenty-fifth, there was still no fixed build for the affected one-dot-x line. So the scanner dutifully flags it, but the suggested fix doesn't exist. The real way out is migrating to Fastjson two — and that's not a maintenance-window kind of task.

The third case is the most striking. A survey of the open internet found almost thirty-seven thousand reachable server remote-management modules. Of those, twenty-four thousand six hundred fifty handed over their password hash before any login even happened. More than thirty percent of those hashes could be cracked with common wordlists and the predictable password formats printed on factory stickers. The flaw dates back to twenty thirteen, it's baked into the protocol's specification, and Dell simply says there's no patch for it. The only real fix here is network segmentation: these modules belong on a management network, reachable only through a tunnel.

The second is more interesting: an information leak in Fortinet's operating system, rated just five point three, already patched back in February twenty twenty-five, now returning to the catalog because it's being exploited again. A middling severity score on a VPN device is usually not the goal — it's the first step in a chain.

OpenWrt has an overflow in its DHCPv6 service, which runs as root — severity nine point eight, fixed in versions twenty-four ten eight and twenty-five twelve five. No exploitation in the wild has been reported, but the advisory does ship with sample code. The attack comes from inside your own network, not from the internet — so the question isn't whether you're exposed, it's what else is sitting on the same segment as your router.
