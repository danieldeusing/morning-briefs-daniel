One Key That Unlocked Every Cosmos DB in the World

Wiz Research published an attack chain in Azure Cosmos DB on Jul 30 that they're calling "CosmosEscape." The entry point was the Gremlin endpoint — the query interface for Cosmos DB's graph mode, where you send the service small code snippets that it executes server-side. That's exactly what the sandbox around it is for. The researchers broke out of it via .NET reflection and ended up holding a signing key that belonged not to them, but to the platform: "It was a platform-wide key that could retrieve the primary key for any Cosmos DB account on the service, all through publicly accessible endpoints."[1]

Multi-tenancy: A cloud service doesn't spin up a separate machine per customer — it runs one big shared facility where each customer gets a partitioned-off area. Like an apartment building: your own unit, your own key — but one building, one boiler, one superintendent. If the superintendent's master key goes missing, your own best door lock is worthless.

The timeline is the real story. The chain was reported on 2025-11-20. The Gremlin entry point was, per Wiz, "blocked within 48 hours" — and Wiz dates the complete fix across all regions to July 2026.[1] Eight months between "the door is shut" and "the lock has been changed." That's not a criticism: rotating a platform-wide key without tearing apart millions of live connections is exactly the kind of work that takes time. But it's the most honest number of the day.

Microsoft's statement: "We have fully addressed the issue and found no evidence of customer impact based on our investigations."[2] That's plausible and simultaneously unverifiable — only Microsoft holds the telemetry that could confirm it. No CVE number was assigned.

Heads up Without a CVE there's no NVD entry, no scanner hit, no KEV entry, no ticket, no line in the SBOM. For eight months, every tool in your chain would have answered: "no findings." That's not a failure of the tools — it's the edge of their jurisdiction, and it runs exactly where your responsibility for a managed service ends.

My take: the shared-responsibility model gets explained as a division of labor; in practice it's a division of visibility. Above the line, you have instruments. Below the line, a press release. Anyone keeping production-critical data in a managed store should keep that in mind the next time they decide on customer-managed keys — one of the few measures that still works below the line.

CodeIgniter: is_image Doesn't Mean It's an Image

On Jul 31, the NVD published CVE-2026-63223: CVSS 9.8 (Critical), vector AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H, affecting the PHP framework CodeIgniter 4 before version 4.7.4.[3] The core of it, in the NVD's words: "the is_image and mime_in upload validation rules do not independently enforce a safe client filename extension, allowing a remote attacker to upload executable content when an application preserves the client filename and stores uploads in a web-accessible script-enabled directory."[3]

MIME type vs. file extension: The MIME type describes what's actually inside ("this is a PNG"); the file extension is just the label on the package (".png"). Like a package marked "books": the inspector looks inside and nods — but the sorting machine behind them just reads the label. If it says .php, the web server executes the content, no matter how diligently the inspector nodded.

Two conditions have to line up: the application accepts the client-supplied filename, and the upload directory sits inside the web root with active script execution. Anyone who renames uploads or stores them outside the web root is off the hook. The 9.8 describes the worst case, not the average one — a good occasion to stop reading the base score as a probability.

PHP isn't Daniel's stack, but the trap crosses frameworks. Spring ships the same raw material with MultipartFile, FastAPI with UploadFile: the framework validates the content, and the web server makes the execution decision based on the name. The stable rule stays boring: reassign filenames server-side, store uploads outside the web root, serve them through a handler instead of a filesystem path.

Today, the Deadline Expires for a Hardcoded Password

The KEV entry for CVE-2026-20316 ran here yesterday — today is the day US federal agencies must have dealt with it. Cisco published an advisory on Jul 29 about static credentials for a low-privileged account in Secure Firewall Management Center; an unauthenticated attacker can use them to log in remotely and access that account's data.[5] Cisco PSIRT became aware of active exploitation in July. CISA added the flaw to the KEV catalog on Jul 29 and gave federal agencies a deadline of 2026-08-01.[4]

The interesting number is the base score: 5.3. Cisco still rates the impact as "High" because the access can be chained with other FMC flaws into a privilege escalation.[5] That's exactly why the KEV catalog is the better triage signal than CVSS: it sorts by "is being used," not "could be bad." A 5.3 with a deadline beats a 9.8 with no observed exploitation — every time.

Not affected, per reporting: Cloud-Delivered FMC, Firewall Device Manager, ASA, Threat Defense, and Security Cloud Control. Trade press cites the fixed versions as 7.0.9.1, 7.2.11.1, 7.4.7.1, 7.6.5.1, 7.7.12.1, and 10.0.1.1.[5]

The CVE ID, listing date, and deadline therefore come from several independent secondary sources, not from our own pull of the agency page. They agree in substance; formally, that's one step below a primary source.

Antitrust Authority on SAP: For Now, No Abuse in Data Access

Germany's Federal Cartel Office (Bundeskartellamt) closed its preliminary inquiry into SAP SE on Jul 30 and is not opening an abuse proceeding. The trigger was complaints from software companies, including Celonis, that SAP was making it harder for customers and third-party vendors to access data from its ERP systems and thereby favoring its own offerings — particularly in process mining.[6]

Process mining: Software reads the logs of an operating system like SAP and reconstructs from them how a process actually ran — not how the manual says it should. Like the desire paths worn into a lawn: the master plan shows the paved walkway, the paths show where people actually walk. That reconstruction depends on raw access to the log data — hence the dispute.

The authority's reasoning is terse: "Nach den Vorermittlungen bestehen derzeit ausreichende Möglichkeiten für die Datenextraktion und es liegen keine ausreichenden Hinweise auf kartellrechtsrelevante Behinderungspraktiken vor."[6] The decisive word is derzeit — "currently." This isn't a structural clean bill of health, just a snapshot of today's interface situation. If SAP narrows the extraction paths later, the file can reopen — heise, consistently, calls the outcome a "setback for Celonis," not a landmark ruling.[7]

Practical takeaway for anyone building integrations on SAP data: today's extraction paths count as sufficient in the authority's eyes. That's not a blank check for the next five years — dependence on a single extraction method remains a risk worth keeping small.

Database Corner: A Postgres in Rust, a Beta 2, a Point Release

Turso wants to rebuild Postgres — announced back on Jul 16

Turso, known for its Rust reimplementation of SQLite, announced on its own blog that it's building a Postgres-compatible engine on the same Rust core. The claim, in their own words: "Turso is becoming the LLVM of databases. One modern, reliable core; many database frontends compiled down onto it."[10] Important for context: the post is dated 2026-07-16. That it circulated through the trade press this week doesn't make it today's news.

My skepticism isn't aimed at Rust. The core is never the hard part of a database — the hard part is the compatibility surface: the wire protocol, extensions, planner behavior under real statistics, pg_dump round trips, logical replication. SQLite is a small, sharply bounded target to reimplement; Postgres is the opposite. The claim only cashes out once an existing schema, extensions included, migrates over without changes.

PostgreSQL 19 Beta 2 and SQLite 3.53.4

From the same corner, without any fuss: PostgreSQL 19 Beta 2 came out on Jul 16, with work on vacuum, logical decoding, temporal tables, property graphs, and pg_dumpall. SQLite 3.53.4 landed on Jul 24.

Three Flaws, Three Zones of Responsibility — Our Tools Know One

This weekend's three security stories don't sit side by side by chance. They mark three completely different relationships to accountability — and only one of them is mapped in our tool chain.

CVE number→ NVD entry→ Scanner hit→ Ticket

CodeIgniter runs the full chain: version, score, vector, fix version — from discovery to ticket with no human involvement. That's the case the industry has spent fifteen years building its entire infrastructure for, and it works.

Cisco enters at a different point. A base score of 5.3 would have gotten lost in any severity-based prioritization. Only the KEV catalog flips the sort order from "how bad would it be" to "is it being used." Here, it wasn't the tool that prioritized — it was a government agency.

CosmosEscape doesn't show up anywhere in the chain. For eight months, every dashboard's answer was: clean. And it wasn't wrong — it just wasn't its job.

The takeaway isn't alarm, it's calibration. A green vulnerability dashboard means: "in the part of the system I can see, nothing is known." The more state lives in managed services, the smaller that part gets — and the more weight falls on the few measures that also work below the visibility line: customer-managed keys, network boundaries around the data paths, and the question of what data even needs to sit there in the first place.
