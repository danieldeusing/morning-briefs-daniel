The Deadline That Isn't Happening Today

In the EU AI Act's original timeline, 2026-08-02 was the big date: general applicability of most requirements except Article 6(1), plus operational national AI sandboxes.[3] Anyone who spent twelve months on compliance documentation worked toward this date. It's here today — and nothing happens.

High-risk AI system (Annex III): The AI Act sorts systems by potential for harm, not by technology. Annex III lists the particularly sensitive use cases — candidate screening, creditworthiness, access to education, critical infrastructure. Like a vehicle inspection: a toaster doesn't need a safety sticker, an elevator does. What counts isn't complexity, but what happens if the thing fails.

The reason is the "Digital Omnibus", which took effect on 2026-07-27 — three days after publication in the Official Journal.[1] It pushes standalone high-risk systems under Annex III from today to 2027-12-02; for AI embedded in products already covered by sector rules under Annex I, the deadline slides to 2028-08-02.[1][2] Two independent law-firm analyses cite both dates word for word.

My read: not an all-clear, but a cost deferred in installments. Anyone who stops work on risk management and technical documentation now will do it again in 2027 — under time pressure and alongside the Cyber Resilience Act obligations that were not postponed. And a deadline that can move by sixteen months once is worth less as a planning anchor than it was on the day it was announced.

Brussels' Other Clock — Still Running

While one calendar got cleared, the other ticks on unchanged. The Cyber Resilience Act requires conformity for horizontal Type A products from 2026-08-30, and for Type B and vertical Type C products from Oct 30, 2026.[5] From 2026-09-11, the reporting duty kicks in: anyone who learns of an actively exploited vulnerability or a serious incident in their own product must send an early warning to ENISA and the national authority within 24 hours, an interim report within 72 hours, and the final report within 14 days.[4]

Those 24 hours aren't really a legal requirement so much as a technical one: someone has to be reachable on the weekend, there needs to be a decision path, and someone must be authorized to notify an authority before the root-cause analysis is done. You don't retrofit that in six weeks.

This week itself is the yardstick: Adform discovered the poisoned ad code on Jul 27, and it went public on Aug 1.[11] Five days. Whether a service like that even falls under the CRA's product definition is its own discussion — for calibration, the comparison still holds: today the reflex is "understand first, then talk"; from Sep 11 on, it's "report, then understand."

B3: Three Hours Without a Stock Exchange, Over a File Transfer

Brazil's stock exchange B3 delayed its trading open on Friday, Jul 31. Official wording: "em decorrência do atraso no envio dos arquivos e na abertura da Câmara B3 … postergaremos a abertura da negociação dos contratos Mini Índice (WIN) e Mini Dólar (WDO)" — because of a delayed file transfer and the delayed opening of the Câmara B3, trading in the Mini Index (WIN) and Mini Dollar (WDO) contracts started later, while other securities stayed in the auction phase.[6] Operations normalized around 12:30 pm local time, roughly three hours after the usual open.[7]

Câmara / clearing house: Between buyer and seller, an exchange interposes a third party that guarantees to each side that the other will deliver or pay. Like an escrow account when buying a house: the money doesn't go straight to the seller, but to a party both sides trust. Until that party has opened and reconciled its books, nobody may trade — otherwise nobody would know by evening who owes what.

For developers, the cause is more interesting than the outage. It wasn't the matching engine that stalled, not the network, not the database — it was a file transfer, the quiet overnight batch job that has to finish before the day can start. Every serious system has one of these, and it's almost always the part with no load testing, no chaos engineering, no SLO of its own — because it "just" moves data from A to B. On Friday, that's what brought the entire visible operation to a halt for three hours.

CVSS 10.0 with a Three-Day Deadline: Arista VeloCloud

On 2026-07-27, the NVD published CVE-2026-16812: an OS command injection (CWE-78) in Arista Networks' VeloCloud Orchestrator On-Prem, base score 10.0 (Critical) on both scales — CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:C/C:H/I:H/A:H, matched by CVSS 4.0.[8] It was exploited before the patch existed. CISA added it to the KEV catalog the same day and gave US federal agencies three days — the shortest deadline this process allows.[9][10] In the same advisory: CVE-2025-68686 (Fortinet FortiOS, sensitive information disclosure).[10]

SD-WAN orchestrator: Instead of connecting thirty sites through thirty individually configured lines, a central piece of software tells every site's box which traffic takes which path. Like a transit agency's control center: the buses drive themselves, but the routing comes from above. The orchestrator sits above the network it controls.

That's exactly where the 10.0 comes from. A clean ten requires network reachability, no privileges needed, no user interaction, and a scope change all lining up — meaning the attacker breaks out of the component they compromised. For a control center, that's the normal case: whoever runs commands there doesn't get one device, they get configuration authority over everything behind it. According to the report, it's fixed in VCO 5.2.3.14, 6.1.3.4, 6.4.2.4, and 7.0.0.1 and above; only the self-hosted on-prem variant is affected.[9]

The lesson sits in the comparison with yesterday's Cisco FMC password: base score 5.3, same treatment — KEV entry, deadline, obligation. The score says how high the ceiling is; the KEV catalog says whether someone's climbing it right now.

Sourcing Scores and publication date come from a direct NVD pull — a primary source. The KEV alert pages on cisa.gov again rejected direct access with HTTP 403; their content was read via a text-reader proxy and matches trade coverage. The listing date and deadline are solid, but formally one step below a direct pull.

Adform: The Foreign Code Running on Your Site

At Danish ad-tech vendor Adform, the shipped file trackpoint-async.js was tampered with. The trojanized script continuously watched the clipboard and swapped detected Bitcoin, Ethereum, and TRON addresses for the attackers' own — and it also rewrote addresses typed directly into form fields.[11] Adform discovered the incident on 2026-07-27, removed the code, and notified customers and authorities; it went public yesterday.[11][12]

Ad tag embedded→ Vendor CDN poisoned→ Script reads clipboard→ Foreign wallet

For scale: Adform cites around 1,800 customers for 2025, 1.5 billion ad impressions a day, business in more than 180 countries.[11] That's the vendor's reach, not the number of affected sites — nobody publicly knows that number. That uncertainty is part of the problem.

Heads up This class of incident is so persistent because the script is loaded at runtime from a foreign domain: no lockfile, no SBOM, no Dependabot report ever sees it. Only measures from a different layer help — a CSP with a tight script-src, Subresource Integrity where the vendor allows fixed hashes (ad tags rarely do), and the most boring one: no third-party tags at all on pages that handle money or credentials.

My view: the ad tag is the last big supply chain with no lockfile. We've spent fifteen years pinning, signing, and scanning npm and Maven dependencies — and on the same page we load a file whose contents can change at any time without triggering anything. That's not an Adform problem, that's the design.

Half a Click: OWAReaper in Exchange OWA

CVE-2026-42897 is a cross-site scripting flaw in Outlook Web Access, CVSS 8.1 ("high"), affecting Exchange Server 2016, 2019, and the Subscription Edition; the attacker doesn't need to be logged in. heise sums up the core in one sentence: "Displaying an email in OWA leads to the execution of embedded JavaScript."[13] Just displaying it is enough — the plain preview in the reading pane.

It has been exploited since 2026-07-22 by a Russian state-linked actor, known variously as "Laundry Bear" or "Void Blizzard" depending on the naming scheme. It loads a previously unknown JavaScript implant called OWAReaper for persistent mailbox access; it then rewrites the attack email server-side through the Outlook APIs and strips out the exploit content.[14] Targets: government, telecom, finance, hospitality, and aviation in the US and Europe.

The self-erasure is the real story. Anyone searching the mailbox for the incident — full-text search, rules for suspicious attachments — finds nothing, because the evidence has redacted itself. Detection needs to happen one layer earlier: at the reverse proxy in front of OWA, not in the mail store behind it. And for anyone still running Exchange on-prem: the reading pane is long past being a passive display — it's an execution environment.

  2. theregister.com — actor, timeline from Jul 22, 2026, OWAReaper and the server-side email rewrite

Two Ways to Get at Your Token

vault-secrets-webhook: CVSS 9.6

CVE-2026-54725, published 2026-07-31: the Kubernetes webhook that injects secrets from HashiCorp Vault into pods can be tricked into "sending the associated Kubernetes ServiceAccount JWT directly to an attacker-controlled Vault address."[15] Base score 9.6, fixed in 1.23.1.

Mutating admission webhook: Before Kubernetes starts a container, it sends its description to a small service that's allowed to modify it — for example, to inject passwords that shouldn't sit in the config file. Like a company mailroom that opens and stamps every letter before it goes out. Convenient — as long as nobody slips the mailroom a fake destination address.

The attack flips the direction of trust: what's stolen isn't the secret, but the credential that gets you to every secret. If a cluster-wide webhook sends the ServiceAccount token to an arbitrary URL, neither network policy on the pod nor encryption in etcd helps.

npm @joyfill: Two Beta Packages with Remote Control

Compromised: @joyfill/layouts@0.1.2-2773.beta.0 and @joyfill/components@4.0.0-rc24-2773-beta.4, reported on 2026-07-29. The malicious code "can collect host information, establish a Socket.IO remote-control channel, execute passed JavaScript or shell commands, upload files, read clipboard data, and modify developer tool files."[16]

Two things stand out. First, the clipboard — the same monetization as Adform, just a different channel. Second, it hit beta and RC tags: a @beta in the install command bypasses the mental bookkeeping people automatically do with pinned versions, and it still lands in the same node_modules.

1,442 Chrome Bugs in Three Releases

By Google's own count, 1,072 security bugs were fixed in Chrome 149 and 150 alone; with 151, that's 1,442 across three releases — more than the 23 releases before that combined. Chrome 151 contributes 370 findings, 349 of them found internally.[17] A separate, older case from the same category: CVE-2026-3545, a critical sandbox escape in navigation that, according to Google, sat undiscovered in the source for 13 years and was already fixed back in March in an earlier release — found via an agentic review framework built on Gemini.[17][18]

The tooling angle belongs in the ai-dev brief; what matters here is what this means for the browser as software — two points.

First: a jump in bug count doesn't mean Chrome got worse, it means the find rate changed — and that rate is rising for everyone searching the same codebase. What's critical here isn't Chrome itself, which updates silently, but everything that drags Chromium along with it: Electron apps, CEF embeds, in-app browsers, kiosk systems. Those run on their own release cycles and accumulate these findings as backlog.

Second: thirteen years should finally bury the heuristic that "old code is vetted code." Age is exposure time, not proof of quality. What nobody found in 2013 wasn't safe — just unobserved.

Deadlines Postponed, Attackers Not

Three deadlines shaped the week: one canceled (today), one three days long (Arista), and one that starts in six weeks (CRA). The real damage came from none of them.

CVE number→ Deadline→ Ticket→ Patch

The chain works — for Arista it ran its course in three days, for the Kubernetes webhook it's sitting there as a version bump. But only for things with a number. The Adform code had none: no package, no version, nothing a scanner could see. OWAReaper has one, but it only helps up to the patch — the implant in the mailbox carries no version string, and the email that delivered it redacted itself.

The useful takeaway costs nothing this weekend: go through the list of what gets loaded at runtime from foreign domains, and for each line answer who's allowed to change it and how you'd notice. That's where both of this week's big incidents happened — and there's no KEV catalog or Brussels deadline for that.

It's Sunday, August second. Today was supposed to be a major deadline in the European Union — and it no longer is.

Under the original timeline for Europe's AI law, August second of this year was the day most requirements would kick in, including obligations for high-risk systems and the launch of national AI sandboxes. Instead, on July twenty-seventh, the so-called Digital Omnibus took effect — three days after it was published in the Official Journal. It pushes standalone high-risk systems out to December second, twenty twenty-seven, and systems embedded in other products all the way to August second, twenty twenty-eight. Two independent law firm analyses cite the same two dates.

My take: this isn't an all-clear. Anyone who stops working on documentation and risk management now will just be doing it again in a year and a half, under pressure. Because Brussels' other clock keeps running: the Cyber Resilience Act requires conformity for the first product category starting August thirtieth, and starting September eleventh, the reporting duty kicks in — twenty-four hours for the early warning, seventy-two hours for the follow-up, fourteen days for the final report. Those aren't legal deadlines, they're technical ones. Somebody has to be reachable on a weekend.

The toughest security story of the week comes from Arista. The US vulnerability database published a flaw in the VeloCloud Orchestrator on July twenty-seventh with the highest possible score, ten point zero, on both rating scales. It was already being exploited before the patch existed. The US cybersecurity agency gave federal agencies just three days to fix it, the same day it was listed — the shortest deadline this process allows. An orchestrator sits above the network it controls; whoever can run commands there doesn't just get one device, they get control of everything behind it.

The second big story doesn't have a number at all. At the Danish ad company Adform, a shipped script was tampered with. It watched the clipboard and swapped any crypto address it recognized for the attacker's own, and it even rewrote addresses typed straight into a form. Adform caught it on July twenty-seventh and pulled it, but the incident only went public yesterday. The company reports roughly eighteen hundred customers last year and business in more than a hundred eighty countries — that's its reach, not the number of sites actually affected.

And that's the sore spot. An ad script shows up in no lockfile, no bill of materials, no dependency report. We've spent fifteen years pinning and signing package dependencies, and on the very same page we load a file whose contents can change at any moment. What actually helps comes from a different layer entirely: a tight content security policy, fixed checksums where possible, and the most boring advice there is — don't load third-party scripts at all on pages that touch money.

Out of Microsoft's world comes an attack that takes half a click. A cross-site scripting flaw in Outlook Web Access, rated eight point one, runs embedded JavaScript the moment an email is displayed. A Russian state-linked group has been exploiting it since July twenty-second, planting an implant called OWAReaper for lasting access to the mailbox. The remarkable part: it then rewrites the attack email itself through Outlook's own interfaces and strips out the exploit. The evidence erases itself — so detection has to happen before the mailbox, not inside it.

A few more numbers, briefly. Google fixed one thousand four hundred forty-two security bugs across three Chrome releases, more than the twenty-three releases before that combined; separately, back in March, it had already fixed a sandbox escape that sat undiscovered in the code for thirteen years. In the Kubernetes world, a widely used Vault webhook can be tricked into sending its ServiceAccount token to a stranger's address, rated nine point six, fixed in version one twenty-three point one. And Brazil's stock exchange, B3, opened almost three hours late on Friday — not because the trading engine failed, but because an overnight file transfer to the clearing house ran late. Every serious system has a transfer like that, and almost none of them test it.

Next week the industry heads to Las Vegas. Black Hat talks run Wednesday and Thursday, DEF CON from Thursday through Sunday. It's the busiest disclosure window of the year — expect this brief to get a lot fuller by midweek.
