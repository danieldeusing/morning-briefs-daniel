The Deadline Was Real After All — Just Not for Us

Update since yesterday Here it said the Aug 2 deadline would pass without effect, because the Digital Omnibus pushed the Annex III high-risk obligations to 2027-12-02. That holds for Annex III. It doesn't for Chapter V: as of yesterday, the Commission has its oversight and enforcement powers over providers of GPAI models — it can request documentation, run its own technical model evaluations, demand risk mitigation, and restrict or withdraw a model from the EU market (Art. 91–93).[1]

GPAI model: A model built not for one purpose but for many — the same thing writes copy, answers support tickets, and sorts images. Think of a general-purpose engine that ends up in a lawnmower one day and a boat the next: the engine maker doesn't know where the product will end up working. That's exactly why Brussels targets the engine maker here, not the boat builder.

The fine ceiling for this sits in Art. 101 and is uncomfortably specific: up to 3% of the prior year's global annual revenue or €15 million, whichever is higher.[1] That's no longer a minor infraction — it's a balance-sheet line item.

What interests me about this date is the asymmetry. The part of the AI Act that would have hit thousands of ordinary software teams — applicant scoring, creditworthiness, access systems — is now on ice for sixteen months. The part that hits a good handful of very large model providers arrived on schedule. For those of us further down the chain, that changes not a single line of code today, but it changes something structural: for the first time, there's someone upstream in the supply chain with a named duty to disclose. Anyone who used to have to write "the model is a black box, ask the provider" now at least has a body that's allowed to ask the provider.

Sourcing The AI Act tracker states verbatim, for Aug 2, 2026, the start of the Commission's powers over GPAI providers.[1] The same provider's implementation timeline states for the same date only, in general terms, "The remainder of the AI Act starts to apply, except Article 6(1)" and hasn't yet been updated for the Digital Omnibus.[2] Both need to be read together; an official consolidated text showing both the postponement and the Chapter V start date in one document wasn't available to me today.

The Software Bill of Materials Gets New Mandatory Fields

On Jul 29, CISA and roughly seventeen international partner agencies published the "2026 Minimum Elements for a Software Bill of Materials." New among the minimum elements, per reporting, are "component hash algorithms, component licenses, the name of the SBOM generation tool, and generation context"; several existing fields were renamed, and a field for a digital signature was added.[3] The paper thereby replaces the 2021 NTIA minimum elements — the reference practically every SBOM tool of the past five years is calibrated against.

SBOM (Software Bill of Materials): A machine-readable ingredient list: which libraries, in which versions, sit inside a program. Like the ingredient list on a frozen pizza — not so you read it, but so you can figure out in seconds which boxes need to be pulled if a batch of mozzarella goes bad. In software, the spoiled batch is a specific Log4j version.

The most substantial new field is the hash. A bill of materials without checksums claims "we used lodash 4.17.21"; one with checksums proves "we used exactly this artifact." Only with that does the ingredient list become a piece of evidence — and only with that can a swapped-in package be told apart from the real one. The name of the generating tool is the second underrated point: it tells the reader how much to trust the rest of the file, because different scanners see with different depth into container layers and transitive dependencies.

My read on where this actually lands: not with the agencies that wrote it, but in your pipeline. Once the generators catch up, the output format of a step nobody has touched in years will change — and the license field, as a side effect, turns the file into a compliance artifact that legal will suddenly start reading too. If you generate SBOMs, take a look at the output in the next few weeks rather than waiting for a customer to do it for you.

Sourcing cisa.gov again rejected direct access with HTTP 403; content and field details therefore come from trade coverage.[3] One secondary source lists Germany's BSI among the co-signing agencies.[4] I couldn't confirm that against a primary source — plausible as German participation, but not verified.

Eclipse and OWASP Sign On Against CRA Anxiety

The Eclipse Foundation and OWASP signed a letter of intent on Jul 29 (distributed via newswire on Jul 30, picked up by the German trade press on Aug 2). Five work streams: CRA readiness for the open-source ecosystem, alignment across the various security frameworks, joint training, support for stewards and maintainers, and policy and regulatory education. Announced as first outputs are practical guides for stewards, joint CRA webinars, and SBOM-adoption material.[5] Eclipse executive director Mike Milinkovich states the core reason: "Open source is critical digital infrastructure, but responsibility for securing it is distributed across a complex global ecosystem."[5]

Steward (open source): The role for someone who doesn't sell a project but carries it long-term — foundations, corporate OSPOs, paid maintainer teams. Like the superintendent of an apartment building: he doesn't own the building and doesn't rent it out, but when the hallway light goes out, he's the person to call. The CRA needs exactly this middle figure, because otherwise nobody would be addressable at all.

Two associations sign a paper — at first glance that's a press release, and the newswire text reads like one too. What matters anyway: the CRA reporting duty starting 2026-09-11[5] hits projects that have no legal department, and around the Open Regulatory Compliance working group is exactly where the one place practical answers for those projects is taking shape. In addition, the free ORC Learning Hub has been running role-based courses for maintainers, product, security, and legal teams since Jun 30.[6]

My reservation stands: training doesn't settle liability. A maintainer who knows, after the webinar, what an incident report looks like still doesn't know whether they're required to write one. But it's the right order of operations — tools first, the legal fight after.

An Uploaded Image Reads Out the Rails Secret Key

The Rails security team published CVE-2026-66066 on Jul 29: "arbitrary file read and remote code execution in Active Storage variant processing." Affected are activestorage < 7.2.3.2, >= 8.0, < 8.0.5.1 and >= 8.1, < 8.1.3.1. The impact, in the announcement's own words: "A Rails application that displays image variants may allow an unauthenticated attacker to read arbitrary files from the server, including the process environment" — meaning including secret_key_base.[7][8]

The root cause sits one layer down, in libvips: the library reads and writes formats through "loaders" and "savers," many of which build on third-party libraries — and it marks some of them itself as unfuzzed, "meaning they are unsafe for untrusted content."[7] Active Storage simply never turned those operations off.

unfuzzed: Fuzzing means pelting a program with millions of deliberately broken inputs and watching where it falls over. Whatever never ran through that test counts as "unfuzzed" — not proven unsafe, but untested. Like a spare part with no crash test: it might hold up. Nobody just knows.
Upload image→ Generate variant→ unfuzzed loader→ Process environment→ Secret key

It's fixed in the Active Storage patch releases; libvips also needs to be at least 8.13. If you can't move to a new Rails version right away and already run libvips ≥ 8.13, the announcement says you can also disable the risky operations via the VIPS_BLOCK_UNTRUSTED environment variable. Full details will be made public "no later than 2026-08-28."[7]

Heads up Patching alone isn't enough here. If a publicly reachable instance displays image variants, treat everything the process could read as read: secret_key_base, master key, database passwords, object-storage credentials, API tokens. This is a rotation job, not a deploy. And Aug 28 is a clock — from then on, the path there is documented in public.

The uncomfortable part isn't the bug, it's the label. The dangerous operations had been flagged upstream as unsafe for foreign content for years — the information sat right there in the API, it just never got passed down. That's the most common shape of supply-chain risk: not a hidden hole, but a warning that no layer forwards. As a side note, it's exactly the case a bill of materials wouldn't catch — the version was fine, the configuration wasn't.

  2. thehackernews.com — independent assessment: unauthenticated file access via image uploads

GitHub Now Holds Back Workflows It Doesn't Like

Since Jul 28, GitHub Actions holds workflow runs it classifies as "potentially malicious" before they execute. Someone with write access has to approve them, specifically via an authenticated web session. For now it only applies to public repositories, it's active with no configuration required, and GitHub Enterprise Server isn't covered yet.[9] GitHub states the reasoning itself: "Recent supply chain attacks use compromised GitHub credentials to push malicious GitHub Actions workflows that steal CI/CD credentials and carry out additional attacks."[9]

Token stolen→ Workflow pushed→ Runner executes→ CI secrets exfiltrated

The real trick is in the fine print: approval only works in the browser, not via API token. A stolen personal access token can therefore still push — but it can't confirm. That puts a step in the chain that an attacker's script can't play along with unless some human with a valid session clicks a button somewhere. That's worth considerably more than one more heuristic in a scanner.

The backstory here is the npm wave from May: "Mini Shai-Hulud" hit 1,048 package versions across 498 packages via hijacked maintainer accounts, including libraries with more than a million weekly downloads.[10] It's exactly this pattern — stolen credentials, then automated propagation through build infrastructure — that the new approval step is trying to break.

What's still missing is a lot, though: private repositories, GitHub Enterprise Server, and any statement of what "potentially malicious" means heuristically. Heuristics mean false positives, and a CI run stuck waiting for approval on a Friday night is also a disruption. For Daniel's setup, that means, very practically: a run in a public repo can now get stuck waiting — that's not a defect, that's the feature. And it's the only structural answer to stolen tokens that I know of: execution no longer follows automatically from a push.

Four Answers to the Same Problem

Three of today's four stories are administrative answers to the supply chain: new mandatory fields in the bill of materials, an approval step before execution, a training alliance for the CRA. All three either document or interrupt trust. None of them would have caught the fourth case.

Warning sits upstream→ nobody passes it down→ hole→ CVE number

In the Rails case, the version was correct, the checksum would have matched, the license too — the bug sat in a default setting. In the end, the most boring procedure imaginable found it: someone read what the library says about itself and asked what the layer above does with that. There's no mandatory field for that.

This week's task is still an administrative one, though, and it costs half an hour: check once which tool generates SBOMs in your own pipeline, and which version — because that's exactly where the new field list will land, quietly, with the next update. And starting Wednesday, keep an eye on Las Vegas.

It's Monday, August third. Yesterday this newsletter said the big EU AI Act deadline would pass without effect. That was too broad a claim, and the correction is today's most important story.

Here's what's actually true: the obligations for high-risk systems have been pushed back by sixteen months. What hasn't been pushed back is Chapter Five. As of yesterday, the European Commission holds oversight and enforcement powers over the providers of general-purpose AI models. It can demand documentation, run its own technical evaluations of their models, require risk mitigation, and restrict or pull a model from the European market entirely. The fine ceiling is three percent of global annual revenue or fifteen million euros, whichever is higher.

What interests me here is the imbalance. The part of the law that would have hit thousands of ordinary software teams is on ice. The part that hits a handful of very large model providers arrived right on schedule. For everyone further down the chain, that changes no code today. But there's now someone upstream with a named duty to answer questions, and that's new.

The second story comes out of Washington, and it will land in every pipeline eventually. The US cyber agency, working with roughly seventeen partner agencies, published a new version of the minimum elements for software bills of materials on July twenty-ninth. New additions include checksum methods, licenses, the name of the generating tool, and a field for a digital signature. The paper replaces the twenty-twenty-one baseline that practically every tool built in the last five years is calibrated against. The checksum is the heaviest addition: only with a checksum does an ingredient list become actual evidence.

The third story is the only one with real urgency. The Rails team disclosed a critical flaw in Active Storage on July twenty-ninth. Any application that displays image variants can let an unauthenticated attacker read arbitrary files off the server, including the process environment — and with it, the application's secret key. The root cause sits in the image library libvips, which flags some of its own format loaders as untested and unsafe for outside content. Active Storage simply never turned those loaders off. It's fixed in versions seven-two-three-two, eight-oh-five-one, and eight-one-three-one, and libvips needs to be at least eight-thirteen.

One thing matters here: patching alone isn't enough. Anything the process could read counts as compromised — so rotate the secret key, database passwords, and storage credentials. And August twenty-eighth is a deadline of a different kind: that's when the full technical details go public.

Over at GitHub, there's a new approval step as of July twenty-eighth. Runs flagged as potentially malicious now wait for someone with write access to approve them — and only through a logged-in browser session, not through a token. That's the actual trick: a stolen token can still push code, but it can't click approve. For now this only applies to public repositories. The trigger was May's package wave, which used hijacked maintainer accounts to compromise more than a thousand package versions.

Three quick items. Python three-fourteen-seven ships tomorrow, a routine maintenance release — anyone without a pinned patch version in their Dockerfile gets it automatically on the next rebuild. The Eclipse Foundation and OWASP signed a letter of intent to help open-source projects get ready for the Cyber Resilience Act, whose reporting duty starts September eleventh. And four Spring advisories from June are still outstanding, worth a maintenance window of their own.
