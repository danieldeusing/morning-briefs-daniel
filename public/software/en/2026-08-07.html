Twelve Years of Bad Randomness

CryptoJS is one of those libraries you never consciously install — it just sits in the dependency tree, has for years, and handles hashing and encryption in the browser. Blockchain security firm Coinspect disclosed yesterday that its CryptoJS.lib.WordArray.random() function has never produced cryptographically sound randomness since its introduction twelve years ago: a multiply-with-carry generator fed from Math.random().[1]

The number that shows the severity: "Coinspect analyzed 128- and 256-bit entropy. Those should yield search spaces of 2^128 and 2^256, but the vulnerable generator reduced them to roughly 2^39 and 2^47."[1] A search space that would outlast the universe becomes one a laptop can exhaust in an afternoon.

Entropy / Search Space: A key is only as secure as it is unpredictable. "128 bits of entropy" means there are 2128 equally likely possibilities an attacker would have to try. Picture a combination lock advertised with ten digits — but the mechanism actually only turns on four of them. From the outside it looks the same. Brute-forcing it takes a few hours instead of billions of years.

This was fixed in version 4.0.0 in February 2020. Everything below that is affected (exceptions: 3.2.0 and 3.2.1).[1] And still: the first cash-out wave on May 27 pulled $3.14 million from 431 accounts, the second between May 30 and July 13 another $2.55 million from 522 seeds — roughly $5.7 million combined.[1] Affected wallet apps: RRWallet and Milo (both discontinued, no fix), Bexo (fixed in v20.1.0), NanChat (v1.3.0), and Bitcoin Libre (v4, July 2024).[1]

Math.random()→ 239 instead of 2128→ Enumerate seeds→ BIP39 phrase→ Drain address

What I find important here, and it's the counter-thesis to the whole week: no one was in the supply chain. No compromised maintainer, no stolen signature, no worm. The bug had been fixed and public for six years. It still works today only because software that's shipped once doesn't update itself — a 2018 mobile wallet carries its 2018 dependencies around forever.

The practical part Run npm ls crypto-js against everything you maintain — including the projects that are "done." Anything below 4.0.0 is suspect. And the real question isn't the version, it's: was anything secret ever generated with this library — a token, a salt, a session key, a password suggestion? If so, an update alone won't help: whatever was generated with broken randomness stays broken and has to be regenerated.

TONTOU: the Defense Runs, the Interrupt Comes After

Daniël Trujillo and Mengjia Yan of MIT CSAIL have demonstrated a way to bypass modern processors' Spectre v2 defenses — not through a new vulnerability, but through timing. The name explains it: TONTOU, "Time-of-Neutralization to Time-of-Use," also referred to as "Interrupt Injection."[2]

The mechanism in the researchers' own words: "An unprivileged Linux program can time a hardware interrupt to land in the gap between a processor sanitizing its branch predictor and the kernel using it, re-poisoning the predictor after the defense has run."[2]

Branch Predictor / Spectre: Processors guess which way an if will go and start computing ahead of time. If the chip guesses wrong, it discards the result — but traces remain in the cache, and secrets can be reconstructed from those. Spectre v2 means the attacker deliberately makes the chip guess wrong. The defense wipes the guess memory clean before every kernel entry. TONTOU just waits until the wiping is done, then smears it dirty again quickly before the kernel looks.

Affected are AMD Zen 1 through 4 (bypassing Safe-RET there), and on Intel it partially works on Arrow Lake and Cascade Lake Refresh against eIBRS/BHI_DIS_S.[2] The measurements on Zen 2: 5.47 bytes per second at 91.97% accuracy — slow, but plenty for pulling password hashes out of kernel memory. There's no CVE number; AMD published bulletin AMD-SB-7061 on Aug 6, and the Linux kernel patch has been in flight since a commit on June 2. Intel opted against its own countermeasure and points to existing recommendations.[2]

My read: for Daniel's day-to-day — Docker on his own hardware, a couple of VPS — this isn't an alarm, it's an argument for not letting kernel updates sit for months. What's interesting is a different reason. This has been going on for eight years now: defense, bypass, defense. Every round costs compute, and nobody ever adds up how much. The suspicion remains that we're now permanently paying a double-digit percentage of performance for Spectre — for a problem that never quite closes.

Zapscape: From the Guest to the Host

Hyunwoo Kim found a bug in KVM that undermines the core assumption of virtualization. CVE-2026-64561, dubbed Zapscape, sits in KVM/x86's shadow MMU — the bookkeeping that translates guest memory addresses into real host addresses. The description: "Zapscape is a stale-root check ordering flaw in KVM's shadow-MMU bookkeeping that can lead to a use-after-free."[3]

In practice: anyone with kernel privileges in the guest can break out of isolation and execute code as root on the host.[3] That's exactly the chain virtualization as a whole exists to prevent. Affected is Linux from 5.9 onward — essentially every hypervisor in the field —, fixed in the stable releases 6.6.148, 6.12.101, 6.18.42, 7.1.6, and 7.2-rc5; the upstream fix has been merged (commit 2abd5287f083).[3]

Context, not alarm Red Hat provisionally rates this CVSS 7.0 — lower than "VM escape" sounds, because the prerequisite is steep: you already need kernel privileges in the guest, and the race condition is delicate. Anyone running their own VMs on their own hardware has no new problem. Anyone running other people's workloads on shared hardware — every hosting provider, every CI-runner fleet handling customer jobs — made exactly this promise their security guarantee.

Cisco: Triple 9.9 — and a Transposed Digit in the Trade Press

Cisco published a security hardening round for Catalyst SD-WAN and IOS XE on Aug 5, 2026. From the primary source, the advisory itself: CVE-2026-20303, CVE-2026-20304, and CVE-2026-20310 sit at CVSS 9.9 (critical), plus CVE-2026-20312 at 8.8 and CVE-2026-20313 at 7.7.[4] On exploitation, Cisco writes: "The Cisco PSIRT is not aware of any public announcements or malicious use of the vulnerabilities that are described in this advisory."[4] They were found internally.

A small cross-check finding on the side, and I'm noting it because it shows why you read primary sources: the trade press headlines "Including Three 9.8 CVSS Score Bugs" and then writes 9.9 itself in the body text.[5] It's 9.9. The difference barely matters in practice, but the lesson does: if you're prioritizing by CVSS, get the number from wherever it's assigned.

All deployment modes are affected (on-prem, Cloud-Pro, Cloud Managed, FedRAMP); fixed depending on branch in 20.9.10, 20.12.8.1, 20.15.6, 20.18.4, and 26.1.2. There are no workarounds.[4] Not directly relevant for Daniel — no Cisco in his stack. What's relevant is the pattern: it's the second case within a week where the network's control plane would be the target, not the endpoints on it.

ENDLESSDOORS: the Backdoor Ships From the Factory

VulnCheck found a factory-installed implant process in 21 firmware images across more than 20 router models from Chinese manufacturer Zbtlink — ENDLESSDOORS. It disguises itself as a Linux kernel thread but runs as root in userland and phones home to Chinese control infrastructure every 35 seconds.[7]

The detail from VulnCheck CTO Jacob Baines's analysis: "One reserved string, rctlbash, tells the implant to open a second connection to port 7001, allocate a pseudo-terminal, spawn /bin/sh, and bridge it. That is a live interactive root shell."[7] Zbtlink says the function was "solely intended" for after-sales support on sample units, and points out that OEM customers install their own firmware anyway.[7]

That explanation is notable because it doesn't make things better. A maintenance function that disguises itself as a kernel thread and phones home every 35 seconds isn't a maintenance function — maintenance access doesn't need to lie about what it is. Zbtlink builds a lot of hardware for OEM customers whose names end up on the case. The router in the next room is rarely the one whose name is printed on it.

Switzerland's Federal Government, and the Two Weeks After

Switzerland's Federal Office of Information Technology, Systems and Telecommunication (BIT) confirmed that attackers compromised roughly 200 accounts via SharePoint flaws. Suspicious activity on the SharePoint servers was spotted on July 28; by July 31 it was confirmed that credentials from multiple accounts had been exfiltrated. BIT locked down external access, patched, and reset passwords. So far there's no indication of data exfiltrated beyond the credentials.[8]

The office suspects the SharePoint flaws closed in July's Patch Tuesday as the entry point — under discussion are CVE-2026-56164 (actively exploited privilege escalation) and CVE-2026-50522 (critical code execution, later used to steal SharePoint machine keys).[8]

The second part is the actual point. Anyone who got at the machine keys via -50522 keeps access across the patch: the key is still the same one, forged session tokens keep working. "We patched" isn't the all-clear after a key theft — it's only half the job; the other half is key rotation. That it hit a federal agency with its own cyber office should reassure no one and surprise no one.

Angular Takes Its Foot Off the Gas — Finally

Barely noticed among all the CVEs, but the most relevant news of the week for anyone with Angular in their stack: the twice-yearly major-release rhythm is history. The official release docs now say "A major release every 12 months" with "4-6 minor releases for each major release," and explicitly: "Until Angular v22, Angular had a 6-month major release cycle, with 1-3 minor releases for each major release."[9]

The second part matters more: "All major releases are typically supported for 24 months" — twelve months active, twelve months LTS with security and critical fixes.[9] It used to be 18. The table shows v22.0.0 active until June 2027 and in LTS until June 2028; v23 accordingly won't land until around June 2027.[9]

This is an honest course correction. The six-month cadence forced teams into two migrations a year, most of which delivered little in substance; skip one version and you'd suddenly be two majors behind the support window. A twelve-month cadence plus 24 months of support means you can skip a major and still be in support. That was exactly impossible before — and exactly where Angular's reputation for being high-maintenance came from.
