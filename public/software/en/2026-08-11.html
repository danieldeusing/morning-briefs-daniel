The Ten With No Number

Metabase sits between the database and the people who want to see numbers at plenty of companies: a container, a port, dashboards on top. That's exactly why the flaw the project disclosed on August 6 is so uncomfortable. The publicly reachable endpoint POST /api/session/reset_password accepted arbitrary SQL until the fix — no login, no token. CVSS 10.0; the advisory is titled „SQL injection using an unauthenticated endpoint leading to admin access".[1][2]

Anyone who reaches the endpoint becomes admin — and a Metabase admin is the keyring: they can „steal stored credentials for the connected databases, read any data accessible through those connections, and export data".[3] That's why a Metabase incident never stops at the BI tool. Every version from 1.58 across the 0.58 through 0.63 branches is affected; fixed in x.58.24, x.59.21, x.60.17, x.61.11, x.62.9, and x.63.5.[2] Attacks have been running since around August 3.[1]

CVE vs. GHSA: A CVE number is the worldwide case-file standard for security vulnerabilities; it lands in the US NVD database, which practically every scanner pulls its facts from. A GHSA is GitHub's own case-file system — fully valid, but a different filing cabinet. Like a wanted notice posted only at city hall and never broadcast over police radio: public, accurate, complete — it just never reaches the patrol cars. „Scanners relying solely on NVD feeds will not flag exposed instances."[1]

I consider that more important than the bug itself. Our entire routine sits on top of a number: scanners, SBOM tools, and compliance reports match CVE IDs against package versions. Without the number, the chain doesn't report „unknown" — it reports green. A 10.0 under active attack, and the dashboard says: all clear.

Who's already been hit

PC maker Framework and accounting provider Tally admitted to data theft.[3] LexisNexis also took three services offline on 08/10 — Diligence, Metabase API, and Newsdesk — after „suspicious activity on third-party vendor servers"[4], but clarified that its similarly-named „Nexis Metabase API" product is not connected to Metabase Cloud or the flaw described here, despite the shared name. Belgium's Centre for Cybersecurity is warning too.[5]

For self-hosters Running a Metabase instance: check the version, upgrade to the fixed point release on your own branch. Anyone who can't update immediately should block /api/session/reset_password at the reverse proxy — that's the interim workaround the advisory names.[2][5] After that comes the work that actually matters: the stored database credentials count as compromised the moment the instance was exposed. Rotate them, don't hope.

Progress Kemp LoadMaster

CVE-2026-8037, CVSS 9.6: unauthenticated command injection via the API, in the KEV catalog since August 7 — after 792 reported exploit attempts.[8] Affected are GA versions through 7.2.63.1 and LTSF through 7.2.54.17.[9] heise reported on 08/10: „Schadcode-Attacken auf Progress LoadMaster im Gange"; patches are available.[11] The bug chain is a textbook case in C: escape_quotes() grabs a heap buffer and forgets the trailing null byte. Whatever sits in memory after that becomes part of the string.[10]

uninitialized heap buffer→no null terminator→out-of-bounds read→system()
KEV & the three-day deadline: The KEV catalog ("Known Exploited Vulnerabilities") is CISA's list of flaws with observed attacks — the difference between "someone could break in here" and "your three neighbors already got broken into." For US federal agencies, directive BOD 26-04 ties a three-day deadline to it.[8] For everyone else, it's not an order, but it's the most honest prioritization signal you'll get for free.

Django Goes Annual

The best news of the day comes from the Django project, and at first glance it looks like administrative housekeeping: DEP 20 has been adopted, and Django is moving to an annual release cycle.[16] Starting in January 2028 there's one feature release per year, the version number is the year — the first one is simply called Django 2028 — and every release gets three years of support: one year of bugfixes, then two years of security and data-loss fixes.[17] Nothing changes before 2028; the commitments for 5.2 LTS and 6.2 LTS stand as made.[18]

That's what makes the label „LTS" disappear — which sounds like a loss and is the opposite. The eight-month rhythm forced every team into a standing decision: jump on every release, or hop from LTS to LTS and make one giant leap every two years? When every release lives for three years, that question goes away — one upgrade per year, plannable in the second week of January, with a two-year buffer. This is the version of "release trains" that actually works: Java showed that predictability is worth more than feature density, and Django is drawing the consequence into an annual rhythm — right where its audience already thinks anyway.

And last week's security releases

On August 4, Django 6.0.8 and 5.2.17 came out with four CVEs.[19] The most severe, CVE-2026-15307 (High): spatial lookups let str and dict values through to GDALRaster — server-side write access and request forgery included. Affects only GeoDjango users, but hits them hard. Plus an XSS via URLField in the admin (CVE-2026-15920) and two DoS fixes (CVE-2026-15830, CVE-2026-15337).[19]

A Worm With a Valid Signature

On August 4, the broadest compromise of the year began in the npm namespaces keyv and cacheable: „at least 434 packages across 1,381 versions have been confirmed to be compromised, with the combined monthly installation count of these packages exceeding 2 billion".[20][21] Caching building blocks — nothing you install on purpose, but something that hangs in almost every Node dependency tree.

The sequence: a malicious preinstall hook (setup.mjs) fetches a Bun runtime, the second stage harvests cloud and CI credentials and uses them to publish the next trojanized packages — a worm in the literal sense, attributed by Wiz to the „Mini" Shai-Hulud family.[20][22] The part that stings: the poisoned versions shipped with valid provenance, signed by GitHub Actions.[22] Because the attacker pushed straight to main and immediately triggered a release, the build ran through the real pipeline. Everything checked out — except the content.

Provenance: A cryptographic proof of origin: „This package was built from this Git commit, in this CI pipeline." Like the stamp on an egg — tamper-proof, names the farm, says nothing about the health of the chicken. Provenance proves the build path, not the intent of the code.

In parallel, two more waves at the same layer: around 800 malicious npm packages carrying a cross-platform remote-access trojan and infostealer,[23] and two trojanized VS Code extensions named "Solidity Pro" (helper-beeps.solidity-pro, web3devtoolsx.solidity-pro) that grab wallets, API keys, and credentials.[24] And from the WordPress corner: attackers compromised BdThemes' infrastructure and tampered with a JSON feed so that admin accounts silently appeared on customer sites.[25]

Four incidents, one target: the layer that runs on every developer machine with full privileges and no oversight. What actually helps is unspectacular: commit lockfiles, use npm ci instead of npm install, --ignore-scripts wherever you can, a few days' grace period for new versions — and treat extensions with the same skepticism as dependencies, because that's what they are.

CRA: Exactly One Month Left

On September 11, 2026 — one month from today — Article 14 of the EU Cyber Resilience Act kicks in. Anyone placing a „product with digital elements" on the EU market will then have to report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents: 24-hour early warning to ENISA and the national CSIRT, 72-hour triage report, 14-day final report.[29][30] Full application doesn't follow until 12/11/2027 — but the operationally most demanding obligation comes first.[30]

Reporting duty (Art. 14 CRA): Until now, it was your call whether and when you made an incident public. Starting in September, it's a deadline — like reporting a car accident to your insurer: first the call within 24 hours, then the report, then the record. This doesn't just cover people who build devices: software alone counts too.[30]

For a one-person company like twiceD, the groundwork isn't legal, it's organizational: one defined channel for reports, someone who reads it, a template for day one. That fits with the BSI figure from last week: 1.8% of German website operators have a security.txt.

Passkeys Are Strong, Not Magic

On August 3, Unit 42 published three attack paths against the Google Password Manager in Chrome on Windows devices with a TPM, under the name „Pass-ta-key". The most far-reaching one extracts the 32-byte Security Domain Secret — the key that decrypts the synced private passkey keys.[34][35]

The context matters just as much: „every path begins with malware already running on the victim's device", and as of August 7 there's no evidence of exploitation in the wild.[34] This doesn't devalue passkeys — they still work against phishing exactly as before. It corrects a misconception: a synced passkey is only as secure as the device it's unlocked on. Anyone with malware on the box has a device problem, not an authentication problem.

Separately, Dirk-jan Mollema showed that malware inside a logged-in session can use the hardware-bound Windows Hello for Business key without re-unlocking it; Microsoft's logging flaw CVE-2026-34348 (vendor CVSS 6.5) has been patched.[34]

Fixed as of PCC release 5E290.3.[36][37] It was found exclusively in Apple's Virtual Research Environment — the sandboxed replica built for researchers.

It's Tuesday, August eleventh. Tonight brings Microsoft's Patch Tuesday release, but the most important vulnerability of the day doesn't show up there at all — and it doesn't show up in a lot of vulnerability scanners either.

Metabase has an unauthenticated SQL injection with the maximum score, ten point zero. Metabase is the analytics tool that runs as a container next to the database at countless companies. The endpoint for resetting a password is publicly reachable, and until the fix, it accepted arbitrary SQL with no login required. Anyone who reaches it becomes an administrator, and a Metabase administrator can access the stored credentials of every connected database. So a Metabase incident never stops at the analytics tool.

But the real story here isn't the bug, it's the reporting pathway. The flaw has a GitHub advisory number, but at the time of disclosure, no CVE number. Almost every scanner pulls its facts from the American vulnerability database, and that database only knows CVE numbers. So without a number, the chain doesn't report unknown, it reports green. A perfect ten under active attack, and the dashboard says everything's fine. That's a design flaw in how we measure risk.

It's fixed in the point releases fifty-eight twenty-four, fifty-nine twenty-one, sixty seventeen, sixty-one eleven, sixty-two nine, and sixty-three five. Anyone who can't update right away should block the endpoint at their reverse proxy. And then comes the work that actually matters: rotating the database credentials stored in Metabase.

It sounds like a loss, and it's the opposite: when every version lives for three years, the standing decision between jumping on every release or making a giant leap every two years just goes away. Nothing changes before twenty twenty-eight.

From the supply chain comes a worm with a valid signature. On August fourth, at least four hundred thirty-four packages across two npm namespaces were compromised, in one thousand three hundred eighty-one versions combined, adding up to more than two billion installs a month. A malicious pre-install step fetches a runtime environment, gathers cloud and build credentials, and uses them to publish the next poisoned packages. The part that stings: the poisoned versions came with valid provenance, signed by GitHub Actions. Because the attacker pushed straight to the main branch and immediately triggered a release, everything ran through the real pipeline. Provenance proves the build path, not the intent of the code. Anyone who reads it as verified-equals-safe has confused a signature with an audit.

Alongside that, around eight hundred more malicious npm packages carrying a remote-access trojan, two trojanized extensions for Visual Studio Code, and a tampered data feed at a WordPress vendor that secretly created administrator accounts. Four incidents, one target: the layer that runs on every developer machine with full privileges and no oversight.

On the networking-appliance side, this week's pattern continues. Progress's LoadMaster load balancer has been on CISA's list of actively exploited vulnerabilities since August seventh, after seven hundred ninety-two reported attack attempts. The bug is a textbook case in C: a function grabs memory, writes into it, and forgets the trailing null byte — whatever's in memory after that becomes part of the string and eventually lands inside a system call. At N-able N-central, China-linked actors are deploying a new piece of ransomware called StormEncryptor. And at SonicWall, the group INC chains two flaws together to steal credentials and two-factor seed values. Remember that second part: with the seed, the attacker generates your one-time codes themselves. A password change alone won't cut it.

From Europe, a look at the calendar: one month from today, on September eleventh, the reporting duty of the European Cyber Resilience Act kicks in. Twenty-four hours for an early warning, seventy-two hours for a triage report, fourteen days for a final report. That covers anyone placing a product with digital elements on the EU market — software alone counts too. The groundwork isn't legal, it's organizational: one defined channel for reports, someone who reads it, and a template for day one.
