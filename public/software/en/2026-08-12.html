One of the Biggest Patch Tuesdays — Depending on Who's Counting

Rapid7 writes: "Microsoft is publishing 421 vulnerabilities on August 2026 Patch Tuesday, including 236 vulnerabilities in Windows."[1] SecurityWeek arrives at the same 421, split into 62 critical and 357 important.[2] BleepingComputer counts "a massive 400 flaws," including 42 critical — not counting the flaws in Mariner, Teams, Azure, Entra, Office, and Power Apps that Microsoft quietly fixed earlier in the month.[3]

It's not a record: both outlets explicitly rank August below July. Rapid7 calls it "lower volume than last month's record-breaking behemoth, but still one of the largest Patch Tuesday totals ever"[1], and BleepingComputer puts the prior month at 570 flaws.[3] August, then, is one of the biggest Patch Tuesdays ever — the record has stood since July.

More interesting than the volume is the spread: a 21-CVE and 20-"critical"-rating gap between two competent observers of the same Tuesday. The reason is banal, and that's exactly what makes it annoying — "a Patch Tuesday" isn't a defined quantity. Third-party CVEs that Microsoft merely passes along, out-of-band fixes from earlier in the month, cloud-side corrections that need no action: every tracker cuts it differently. Anyone who steers their risk by "how many CVEs came this month" is steering by a number with more than one value.

The breakdown says more than the sum. By BleepingComputer's count, 176 of the flaws are privilege escalations, 110 are remote code execution, 86 are information disclosures, plus 21 spoofing, 12 DoS, and 11 security feature bypasses.[3] The largest group, then, assumes someone is already on the machine.

EoP vs. RCE: Remote Code Execution means: someone from outside gets your machine to run their code — the burglar picks the front-door lock. Elevation of Privilege means: someone who's already inside makes themselves the building superintendent with a master key. RCE is the entry, EoP the escalation afterward — which is why it rarely makes the headline, and yet it's the building block most real incidents can't work without.

Three With Names [1][3]

Exactly one is actively exploited: CVE-2026-68820, a privilege escalation in the Ancillary Function Driver for WinSock. Microsoft's own status for it reads "Exploitation Detected".[1] AFD is the kernel driver that practically every network socket on Windows runs through — every process touches it, even the least privileged one. That's exactly why it's been a favorite target for an attack's second step for years.

Add to that two publicly known but so far unexploited flaws: CVE-2026-62832 (privilege escalation in the User Profile Service) and CVE-2026-72971 (tampering with the Container Isolation FS Filter Driver).[1][3] Both outlets cover all three numbers; they just label them differently. BleepingComputer sums it up: "This month's Patch Tuesday fixes three zero-day vulnerabilities, with one exploited in attacks and two publicly disclosed."[3] Rapid7 reserves the alarm bell for the one confirmed-exploited flaw and lists the other two as publicly disclosed.[1] So if you read "three zero-days," check which definition is meant — exactly one needs urgent action.

The Real Story Is Ten Weeks Old

While everyone's watching the fresh chain, a different SharePoint flaw is being used for extortion right now: CVE-2026-45659 (CVSS 8.8, risk "high") "is now being actively exploited for ransomware campaigns," reports heise.[4] The patch has been available since the end of May. Shadowserver Foundation scans still found over 200 unprotected instances on Monday — "about half of them are attributed to the US, roughly a quarter to Europe." For Germany, the statistics show "around a dozen," and low single digits for Austria and Switzerland. In early June it was still a good 1,100.[4]

Going from 1,100 to 200 in ten weeks is a good rate. It just doesn't help the 200 — and it describes the pattern of the day: hundreds of patches come out month after month, and incidents still happen where ten-week-old updates are missing. More volume doesn't fix that; it just enlarges the list that has to be prioritized.

ShieldBreak: The July 9 Patch Doesn't Hold

A few hours after the August release, a researcher going by the name Nightmare Eclipse published a PoC called ShieldBreak. It fully bypasses the patch Microsoft shipped on July 9 for CVE-2026-50656 ("RoguePlanet"). The claimed success rate is 100%, compared with the unreliable original exploit. Tested on Windows Server 2025 as well as Windows 11 25H2, including the Canary channel; Windows 10 remains vulnerable but isn't covered by the current code. At the time of publication, Microsoft had not commented, and no fix exists.[1][5]

Proof of Concept: A PoC is working proof code: not the claim "this works," but the demonstration. Like a locksmith uploading a video showing the new security lock opening with the same trick as the old one — the manufacturer had said that was fixed. A published PoC drastically lowers the barrier to entry for everyone else: what was research yesterday is a script tomorrow.

I consider that the more important story of the day — more important than 421 new numbers. The entire monthly rhythm rests on a silent assumption: patched = done. A fully reliable bypass a month later cancels that assumption. The compliance report that marks July "fixed" describes a state that no longer exists.

On sourcing Rapid7 and Cyberkendra both report it; Rapid7 describes ShieldBreak as "a full patch bypass for RoguePlanet, a previous entry in the series which Microsoft patched as CVE-2026-50656 during July."[1] The 100% figure still comes from the researcher himself, not from independent testing. No MSRC advisory. So: a well-corroborated finding, but an unverified effectiveness number — attention, yes; an emergency plan, no.

The npm Worm Has a Counting Problem

The attack on the npm namespaces keyv and cacheable began on August 4 at 09:00 UTC.[8] Eight days later, it's unclear how big it is. Aikido: "At least 444 packages (across 1381 versions)".[6] SafeDep: "An npm worm published 2,234 poisoned versions across 444 package names".[7] The package names match; the number of poisoned versions differs by 853.

We're not resolving that here, because we can't: both sources are credible, both are dated August 4–5, and neither names its counting method. Explanations — different snapshot timestamps, withdrawn versions counted in or subtracted out — would be speculation. Both agree, though, on the order of magnitude that actually matters: over 2 billion installs per month across all 444 packages, and, most recently, "very active community spread of this supply chain worm to other maintainers and packages".[6]

The part that concerns me most is where it lives. Wiz describes the persistence like this: "Persistence is attempted via Claude Code hooks and VS Code tasks.json. Data is exfiltrated via GitHub repositories created under compromised identities."[8] The malicious preinstall hook fetches a Bun runtime and drops an obfuscated second stage — but it doesn't live in node_modules, it lives in your tools' configuration.

tasks.json & agent hooks: Both are configuration files that sit in the project and contain commands your editor or your coding agent runs automatically. Like a note taped inside your front door: "Before you do anything, call this number first." Everyone who comes in does it — and nobody thinks the note is a program. That's exactly why these files survive an rm -rf node_modules, a clean npm ci, and usually code review too.

The consequence is unspectacular but concrete: .vscode/tasks.json, .devcontainer/, and the hook files of agent tools belong in review like source code — because they are source code. Add to that: commit lockfiles, npm ci --ignore-scripts wherever possible, a few days' grace period for fresh versions. The dev-environment configuration layer has quietly become an execution layer, without our review routines keeping up.

SAP: A Perfect 10.0 in Commerce Cloud

Same Tuesday, with less noise: SAP published, per Onapsis, 33 new and updated security notes, including five HotNews and nine High Priority notices — SecurityBridge counts a different 29 notes and four HotNews.[9] The top one is a clean 10.0 — Note 3771065, SAP Commerce Cloud (Data Hub Adapter): missing authorization check plus inadequate input validation, resulting in code execution. heise: "SAP Commerce Cloud can be completely compromised".[10]

Behind that, three more severe ones: Note 3765948 (9.9) — code injection in Manufacturing Integration and Intelligence, "execution of arbitrary commands on the underlying host"; Note 3714806 (9.8) — a memory corruption bug in NetWeaver ABAP's DIAG protocol parser, triggerable unauthenticated; Note 3758900 (9.1) — template injection and SSRF, again in MII.[9]

Two notes. On sourcing: SAP's notes sit behind a customer login, so the public state of play is what analyst firms like Onapsis distill from them — citable, but a secondary tier. For context: DIAG is the proprietary protocol between SAP GUI and the application server, ancient and reachable in almost every landscape. An unauthenticated memory violation right there is the most unpleasant of the four numbers, even though the 10.0 looks worse next to it.

AI Act: Live for Ten Days — and It Hits Contract Developers

Not a story from today, and not covered here yet: since August 2, 2026, the Commission's AI Office and national authorities have been enforcing the AI Act's transparency obligations — "From 2 August 2026, the European Commission's AI Office, together with national authorities, will begin enforcing the Artificial Intelligence (AI) Act."[15]

Specifically, three things apply: chatbots and other interactive AI systems must disclose that you're talking to a machine ("tell users they are dealing with AI, not a human"). Deepfakes must be labeled. And AI-generated or -altered content must carry machine-readable markings.[15]

Machine-readable marking: Not a visible "AI" sticker, but a feature inside the file — metadata or a watermark that a program can read. Like the watermark on a banknote: you don't see it in passing, but the checking machine at the register does. The point is downstream processing: platforms, archives, and search engines should be able to recognize generated content without a human looking at it.

For a one-person company like twiceD, what matters most is this: these obligations don't land only on model providers. Anyone shipping a support chatbot in the EU for a client builds the disclosure in as a feature and lists it as a line item in the quote. Concretely: a fixed disclosure line in the bot's first response, a paragraph in the handover documentation, a sentence in the contract about who's responsible for labeling generated media. Half a day now against an uncomfortable conversation later.

What we're not saying here The Commission's announcement doesn't name a figure for possible fines, and we haven't checked a primary source for that today — so none appears here. Anyone who needs the reliable framework should check the regulation's text.
