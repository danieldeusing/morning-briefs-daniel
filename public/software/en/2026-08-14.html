Postgres Closes 28 Holes — and the Worst Ones Aren't in the Server

PostgreSQL released 18.6, 17.11, 16.15, 15.19, 14.24 and 19 Beta 3 on August 13. The release note is blunt about the size: "This release fixes 28 security vulnerabilities and over 110 bugs reported over the last several months."[1] Fourteen of the listed CVEs sit at CVSS v3.1 8.8 — the tier where an authenticated-but-ordinary user gets arbitrary code execution. Three give the flavour: CVE-2026-14664 in the regexp engine, CVE-2026-14669 in to_char, CVE-2026-19385 in pg_dump, each described as a "heap buffer overflow executes arbitrary code."[1]

That last one is where I'd stop and think. pg_dump isn't the database — it's the backup job: cron-scheduled, usually more privileged than the app, often pointed at data you don't fully control. A memory bug reachable from the rows being dumped inverts the trust direction, and the backup tool becomes the way into the backup host.

The upgrade is the boring kind: "users are not required to dump and reload their database or use pg_upgrade … you may simply stop PostgreSQL and update its binaries."[1] That "simply" hides two chores, though.

Heads up The note asks for follow-up on two index types. Tables with GIN indexes: "we recommend checking their reltuples values after updating." And ltree btree indexes over values with "more than about 14,653" labels "may be corrupt" and want a REINDEX.[1] Neither is a five-second job on a large table — budget the window before the restart, not after.

The two that follow the data out of the server

CVE-2026-6464 (CVSS 8.1) is in psql: a COPY FROM STDIN that fails early causes the remaining data lines to be "processed as psql commands."[1] Pipe generated CSV into psql from a shell script — seed jobs, migrations, CI, everyone does it — and a badly-timed failure turns your data into instructions. Patching the server does nothing here; the client binary on the runner is what has to move.

The other one is quieter and worse. CVE-2026-14663 (6.5): pgcrypto, "for OpenSSL-disabled ciphers, silently encrypts to and decrypts from cleartext."[1]

"OpenSSL-disabled cipher": Modern OpenSSL builds can be configured to switch off algorithms considered obsolete — on RHEL-style systems the platform-wide crypto policy does it for you, without your application asking. Think of a hotel quietly removing the locks from its cheapest rooms: the door still closes, the guest still hears a click, nothing is actually locked. Here pgcrypto asked for a cipher, OpenSSL declined, and the function returned successfully anyway — with your plaintext in the column.

A 6.5 badly undersells that. No exception, no log line, no failing test; the only way to notice is to read the bytes. If you use pgcrypto anywhere, this deserves an audit query, not a patch and a shrug.

November 12: Postgres 14 is done

Also in the note: "PostgreSQL 14 will stop receiving fixes on November 12, 2026."[1] Thirteen weeks. A major-version upgrade is a project with a rehearsal, not a Friday afternoon — and 14.24 shipping in this round is the reminder that fixes keep coming right up until they don't.

The KEV Clock Runs Out Today — For Two of the Three

Three vulnerabilities went into CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on August 11. The catalog's own JSON feed gives two of them a federal remediation deadline of August 14 — today: Metabase CVE-2026-72898 and Cisco CVE-2026-20349. The third, Windows AFD CVE-2026-68820, carries dueDate 2026-08-25 — eleven more days. cisa.gov KEV JSON feed (accessed 2026-08-14) We covered the Cisco and Metabase mechanics yesterday; what's useful now is the fixed-version list and the third entry, which got lost inside Patch Tuesday.

KEV catalog: The US cyber agency's list of flaws confirmed to be exploited right now, not theoretically; federal agencies get a binding fix-by date per entry. Nobody outside that government is bound by it — but it's the cleanest free signal for "this one is being used on real people this week," which is exactly the filter you need when 400 CVEs land in a single Tuesday.

Metabase — CVE-2026-72898, CVSS 10.0. Unauthenticated SQL injection through the password-reset endpoint, found the hard way: the vendor writes that "Metabase Cloud was attacked by someone utilizing an unknown ('0-day') security vulnerability in versions 1.58 and above."[2] Fixed in 1.58.24, 1.59.21, 1.60.17, 1.61.11, 1.62.9 and 1.63.5 plus the matching 0.x open-source builds; self-hosters who can't patch today are told to temporarily block the /api/session/reset_password endpoint.[2] That's a reverse-proxy rule — a fifteen-minute stopgap, not a fix.

Cisco ASA/FTD — CVE-2026-20349, CVSS 8.6. An attack can "cause the affected device to reload, resulting in a DoS condition," and — the part that decides your evening — "there are no workarounds that address the flaw." Hotfixes exist for ASA 9.16 through 9.24 and FTD 7.0 through 10.0.[3]

Windows AFD for WinSock — CVE-2026-68820, CVSS 7.0. ZDI flags it as the one Microsoft flaw this month "under active exploitation": a local elevation of privilege.[4] Unglamorous, and that's the point — it's the second stage after a phishing click, turning "code as the user" into "code as SYSTEM." It shipped in Tuesday's rollup, so auto-updating machines are already done — which is presumably why CISA gave it until August 25 rather than today. Don't read the longer clock as a lower priority: a privilege-escalation bug is only boring until it's the second half of somebody's chain.

On the sourcing The deadlines above come from CISA's machine-readable KEV feed (known_exploited_vulnerabilities.json), which is fetchable even when the human-facing alert pages 403 — worth knowing if you script this. The per-CVE mechanics and fixed-version lists come from the vendors and from independent reporting: The Hacker News for Metabase and Cisco, ZDI for the Windows flaw.

LiteLLM: Forty Minutes on PyPI, 153 GB of Consequences

CloudSEK published its reconstruction of the March 2026 LiteLLM compromise on August 11, and the numbers are the story. Two poisoned releases — 1.82.7 and 1.82.8 — went up on PyPI during a "short exposure window," pinned at "40 min approximate period the affected PyPI packages were live." From that window CloudSEK reconstructs an exposure dataset of "2,500+ companies" and "434,000 CI/CD pipelines potentially exposed," attributing the operation to a group it calls TeamPCP.[5] On August 13, Help Net Security reported that a 153 GB archive of the raw exfiltrated secrets had reached multiple researchers.[6]

Forty minutes is the number to sit with. No monitoring posture, no advisory feed, no "watch the security list" discipline catches a malicious version that lives for forty minutes in a registry your build pulls from automatically — every human control loop in this industry is slower than that. What would have helped is mechanical and unglamorous: a lockfile with hashes, a build that refuses anything not in it, an internal mirror that lags upstream on purpose.

CloudSEK's remediation guidance is the half teams get wrong: isolate "affected runners, hosts, images, and caches," then "rotate every credential accessible to the affected process, including cloud, repository, registry, Kubernetes, SaaS, database, and AI keys" — because "rotating only the LiteLLM or model-provider key is insufficient."[5] A compromised process in a CI runner doesn't steal the key that names it; it steals the environment it happens to be standing in.

How they got in CloudSEK is explicit that LiteLLM was never attacked directly: "Its CI pipeline installed the Trivy scanner unpinned from the system package manager (apt), so the compromised scanner flowed into the build automatically."[5] A bleak joke — the tool you add to find vulnerabilities was the way in — and a pointed one, because almost nobody pins the version of their security tooling. The list of named affected organisations circulating alongside this story is a separate matter: it comes from Hudson Rock's analysis of the dumped data, and appearing in a credential dump is not the same as a confirmed breach. Treat those names as unconfirmed.

The npm Worm Shipped With Valid Provenance

We covered this campaign's command-and-control move yesterday; the detail that deserves a second look sits upstream of it. Wiz's write-up of the keyv/cacheable compromise names dozens of poisoned packages — keyv 6.0.0, cache-manager 7.2.10 and cacheable-request 13.0.20 among them; the exact tally differs between write-ups — and records that "this worm has since propagated to over 400 distinct npm packages," hunting "cloud credentials, infrastructure secrets, developer credentials, AI-related configuration files, and cryptocurrency wallets."[7] Secondary analyses add the part that should bother us most: the poisoned versions were published carrying valid provenance signed by GitHub Actions.[8]

Build provenance: A signed receipt on a published package saying "this artifact was built by this workflow, from this commit, on this CI system" — so you can tell a package built by the project's own pipeline from one uploaded by a stranger with a stolen token. The catch is what the receipt certifies: it's a delivery note proving the parcel came from the right warehouse. It says nothing about what someone put in the box while it sat on the shelf.

That's the lesson. Provenance is an origin attestation, not an integrity-of-intent one. When the attacker owns the workflow the receipt is genuine and the contents are poison — and the badge meant to make you relax does so at exactly the wrong moment. Anyone running "auto-merge dependency bumps that carry provenance" has a policy that was defeated by design.

Wiz's advice reads like the LiteLLM advice, which is itself the tell: remove the affected versions, "treat affected systems as potentially compromised and rebuild them," "rotate exposed credentials, including cloud credentials, GitHub tokens, SSH keys."[7] Keep the units straight: Wiz counts 400+ affected packages; Singapore's CSA advisory counts "over 1,300 package versions … representing a combined 2 billion monthly downloads."[9] Different denominators, not a contradiction.

BSI Puts TR-03183-1 at 1.0.0, Five Weeks Before the CRA Clock Starts

Germany's BSI published TR-03183-1 v1.0.0 — its concrete reading of the Cyber Resilience Act — in early August: the BSI's own press release is dated 5 August and headlined "Technische Richtlinie TR-03183-1 in Version 1.0.0 veröffentlicht."[10] heise's security ticker picked it up eight days later, on 13 August, which is why it reads as fresh news this week. heise.de/security (Aug 13, 14:52 CEST) The "1.0" is Part 1 specifically, not the guideline as a whole: the BSI's overview page lists Part 1: General requirements at 1.0.0, Part 2: Software Bill of Materials at 2.1.0, and Part 3: Vulnerability Reports and Notifications at 1.0.0.[11]

SBOM (Software Bill of Materials): A machine-readable ingredient list for a piece of software: every library it contains, with version and origin. The point isn't paperwork — it's that when the next Log4j-shaped thing lands, "which of our products ship this?" becomes a query instead of an archaeology project.

Why it matters more than a normal standards release: the CRA's first hard date is close. The BSI's CRA page states that from 11 September 2026, "manufacturers of connected products are subject to mandatory reporting of vulnerabilities and incidents via the single reporting platform," with the essential cybersecurity requirements applying from 11 December 2027.[12] A guideline landing five weeks before a reporting duty starts is Germany answering the question every Mittelstand supplier has been asking: what do you actually want in the file?

Secondary write-ups say TR-03183 pins SBOM formats hard — CycloneDX 1.6 or newer, or SPDX 3.0.1 or newer, everything else non-compliant. We didn't confirm that against the BSI PDF, so treat it as a strong hint, not a spec to build against today. If you ship anything with a network interface into the EU, September 11 is the calendar entry; the widely-circulated "August 30" date we picked apart yesterday hasn't gotten truer overnight.

Docker Builds Its Own Hypervisor

Docker Desktop v4.86 ships Docker VMM as a public beta, "now available on both Mac and Windows starting with Docker Desktop v4.86," with a note that "Linux support will be available at GA." GA is "targeted for the end of October 2026, when Docker VMM becomes the default engine for new Docker Desktop installs across Mac, Windows, and Linux."[13]

VMM (virtual machine monitor): Linux containers need a Linux kernel and your Mac doesn't have one, so Docker Desktop quietly runs a small Linux VM in the background and puts your containers inside it. The VMM is the piece that runs that VM. Docker has always rented it from someone else; now it's building its own — roughly the difference between renting the engine in your car and machining it yourself.

The blog says container startup is "measurably faster across the board" and that "Docker VMM returns memory to the host when containers are idle."[13] Note what's missing: no benchmark figures at all, not one number. "Faster" is a vendor claim here, not a measurement — time your own compose up before and after rather than quote a percentage nobody published.

The memory behaviour is what I'd actually care about daily. The classic Docker-Desktop-on-a-laptop complaint isn't startup latency, it's that the VM claims several gigabytes and never gives them back until you restart it. If the beta really returns idle memory, that's the difference between "Docker plus IDE plus browser" being comfortable and being a swap-file negotiation. Beta means beta, though: side-project material, not a "move the client build over" release, and there's a full quarter before the default flips.

.NET 11 Preview 7: The Toolchain Compiles Itself Away

Microsoft published .NET 11 Preview 7 on August 11. The headline item is in the SDK, not the framework: "NativeAOT dotnet CLI is now enabled by default" — the CLI you type into is itself an ahead-of-time compiled binary now. C# 15 picks up union patterns, labeled break/continue and exhaustiveness checking for constrained type parameters; dotnet test gains run-level --timeout and --maximum-failed-tests.[14]

NativeAOT: A .NET program normally ships as intermediate code that gets compiled to machine code while it runs. Ahead-of-time means doing that at build time and shipping a plain native executable — no warm-up, no runtime to install. The difference between handing someone a recipe plus a kitchen and handing them the finished meal.

Applying it to the CLI itself only pays off in aggregate — but in aggregate it pays off a lot: every dotnet build in every CI job on earth stops paying JIT warm-up. Preview 7 is also where the feature list historically stops moving; secondary reporting puts GA at November 10, 2026, though Microsoft's preview post names no GA date.[15] Servicing releases 10.0.11, 9.0.19 and 8.0.30 shipped the same day — secondary coverage counts ten CVEs fixed across them, which we could not confirm primarily.

It's Friday, August fourteenth. Yesterday's brief was about other people's appliances — a Cisco firewall, a Metabase container, a WordPress login page. Today the story lands on the thing that's actually in your own compose file. PostgreSQL shipped an unusually large security round overnight, and the worst pieces of it aren't even in the database server.

The project released versions eighteen point six, seventeen point eleven, sixteen point fifteen, fifteen point nineteen and fourteen point twenty-four, plus a third beta of nineteen, on Thursday. Fourteen of those carry a severity of eight point eight — the tier where an ordinary logged-in user ends up executing arbitrary code.

That last one inverts the usual direction of trust. Your backup job runs on a schedule, often with more privileges than the application, and often against data you don't fully control. A memory bug reachable from the data being dumped turns the backup tool into the way into the backup host.

That's thirteen weeks, and a major version upgrade is a real project with a rehearsal, not a Friday afternoon. Now to the deadline. Three actively exploited flaws went into the American catalog of known exploited vulnerabilities on Tuesday, and for two of them the federal fix-by date is today. The Metabase one is a perfect ten and is fixed in a list of new point releases across every supported branch; anyone who genuinely can't patch today can block the password reset endpoint at the reverse proxy as a stopgap. The Cisco firewall flaw is an eight point six with no workaround at all, only hotfixes. The third one got lost inside Patch Tuesday and carries a longer clock — the catalog gives it until August twenty-fifth: a local privilege escalation in the Windows networking driver, rated seven point zero and under active exploitation. It's unglamorous, and that's the point — it's the second stage after a phishing click, the step that turns code running as the user into code running as system.

Two supply chain stories, and they rhyme. CloudSEK published its reconstruction of the March LiteLLM compromise: two poisoned Python packages that were live on the index for about forty minutes, from which it reconstructs an exposure set of more than two thousand five hundred organisations and four hundred thirty-four thousand build pipelines. On Thursday a hundred and fifty-three gigabyte archive of the raw stolen secrets reached multiple researchers. Forty minutes is the number to sit with, because no human monitoring loop in our industry is that fast. The only defences that would have worked are mechanical: a lockfile with hashes, a build that refuses anything not in it, and an internal mirror that lags upstream on purpose. And the remediation advice is the part teams get wrong — rotating only the key belonging to the compromised library is not enough. A compromised process in a build runner doesn't steal the credential that names it, it steals the whole environment it happens to be standing in.

The second one is the npm worm, and the detail worth revisiting isn't the malware, it's the receipt. The poisoned releases were published carrying valid build provenance signed by GitHub's own build system. Provenance is a signed note saying this artifact was built by this workflow from this commit. It's a delivery note proving the parcel came from the right warehouse — it says nothing about what someone put in the box while it sat on the shelf. When the attacker owns the workflow, the receipt is genuine and the contents are poison. Anyone who built a policy of auto-merging dependency updates that carry provenance has a policy that was defeated by design.

Two lighter items to close. Germany's federal security office published version one point zero of part one of its technical guideline for the Cyber Resilience Act — the concrete answer to what regulators actually want in the file — back on the fifth of August, five weeks before the reporting duty starts on September eleventh; the German tech press only picked it up on Thursday. And Docker shipped its own hypervisor as a public beta in Desktop version four point eighty-six, with general availability targeted for the end of October. Docker claims faster container startup and, more interestingly, that it returns memory to the host when containers go idle — but it published no benchmark numbers at all, so time your own build rather than quoting a figure nobody measured.

Beyond that the week is genuinely quiet — the next Patch Tuesday isn't until September eighth.
