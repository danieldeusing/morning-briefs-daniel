Someone has been living inside vCenter for two weeks

CVE-2026-59310 is a directory traversal flaw in the syslog service of VMware vCenter, rated CVSS 9.8. An attacker who can reach vCenter over the network needs no credentials at all to execute code.[1] Broadcom published the advisory VMSA-2026-0006 on July 29. The first connections to attacker infrastructure were seen on August 3; by August 5, 343 of the total 361 observed victim addresses were already there — roughly 95% of the campaign fell within two days. In the end, the analysis counted 361 victim IPs across 47 countries; it was reported publicly on August 13.[2]

That sequence of numbers is the real story. Five days passed between "patch available" and "mass exploitation complete"; another eight between mass exploitation and the public report. Anyone who learned from the press on August 13 that they should act had already been compromised for a week — in the bad sense. That's not an argument against patching, but against the defense model of "I react once it's in the media."

For persistence, the attackers used reverse_ssh, an open-source tool written in Go.[3] And that's where the problem sits in the most uncomfortable spot in the stack: vCenter isn't one machine — it's the console that creates, clones, moves, and snapshots all of them. Whoever controls it no longer needs a flaw in any guest system; they just pull the snapshot and read the memory contents offline. For Daniel's day-to-day, that means less "I run vCenter" and more: the host where the client VMs live does. Monday's question isn't whether your own deployment is patched, but whether the provider underneath it is — and whether they say so in writing.

Reverse Shell: Normally an attacker knocks on the firewall from outside — and gets turned away. A reverse shell flips the direction: the compromised system calls out to the attacker from the inside and keeps the line open. Like a burglar who doesn't break down the door, but instead gets someone inside the house to call him and leave the receiver off the hook. Almost every firewall allows outbound connections — which is exactly why it works.

N-able: the fix for the fix, and the backdoor was built in

One layer below the hypervisor, the same picture. CVE-2026-18577 (CVSS 8.2) is an authentication bypass in N-able N-central, discovered because it was being exploited — after the fix for the previous bypass, CVE-2026-18556, turned out to be incomplete.[4] Hotfix 2, build 2026.3.1.10, has been out since August 6 and, per the vendor, is mandatory even if Hotfix 1 (2026.3.1.7) is already installed.[5]

What's more interesting than the flaw is what the attackers did afterward: they used the built-in Take Control feature to reach the managed endpoints, and set up a Cloudflare Tunnel (cloudflared) for persistent access.[6] Neither one is malware — both are legitimate tools; one is part of the product, the other sits on every developer's machine. An antivirus scanner sees nothing unusual here, because nothing unusual is happening; it looks like remote support, and formally, it is.

That's the sore spot of any management software: its normal operation can't be told apart from an attack by the tool itself, only by the intent behind it. Anyone running such systems therefore has to not just patch, but log usage and notice outliers — which session, from which IP, at what time. Without that, the question "were we touched?" is simply unanswerable after an incident like this.

RMM: "Remote Monitoring & Management" is the software IT providers use to manage hundreds of client machines from afar: installing updates, taking over the screen, running scripts. A master key to entire apartment blocks — handy for the building manager, and exactly why it's the most rewarding target in the building.

When the agency stays silent, the attacker's number wins

The French tax authority DGFiP admitted yesterday that a breach allowed "the consultation and extraction of data concerning individuals and professionals." Access came via stolen credentials and an MFA bypass and, according to the agency's own account, had already been cut off at the end of June as part of an audit. The attacker is advertising more than 2 million taxpayer records on a criminal forum; a specialized tracker puts the confirmed figure at around 678,000. The DGFiP itself gives no number.[7]

That's exactly where the mistake lies, and it's self-inflicted. The only party that knows precisely how many records were in that access is the agency. As long as it stays silent, the number circling the globe is the extortionist's marketing figure — and that one is naturally rounded up. Victims can't tell whether they're affected, journalists have to quote the attacker, and if a smaller, real number shows up later, it sounds like downplaying. Six weeks between "access cut off" and "we're saying something" add up to six weeks' head start for the perpetrator's version.

What's practically relevant is the entry route: valid credentials plus an MFA bypass. In 2026 that's the standard path, not the exception — the second factor isn't a fence, it's a hurdle whose height depends on the implementation. Anyone still using SMS one-time codes or app push confirmations ("just tap yes") has a factor that can be handed off. Only methods bound to the domain — passkeys, FIDO2 keys — are phishing-resistant.

MFA Bypass: Multi-factor login means a password and a second proof. In a bypass, the attacker sidesteps the second proof instead of cracking it — inserting himself as a middleman in the real login, letting the victim confirm it themselves, and walking away with the valid session cookie at the end. Like someone who politely asks you to swipe your card at the entrance, then walks through the open door.

RingCentral: 1.6 million, and the dump came three weeks after the no

Have I Been Pwned confirmed that the leaked data contains records for 1.6 million accounts — names, email addresses, phone numbers, and physical addresses.[8] The breach happened in July via a "sophisticated social engineering campaign"; RingCentral disclosed it on July 28. Yesterday the group ShinyHunters posted a roughly 280 GB compressed archive on its darknet leak site, after the company reportedly refused to pay.[9]

The three weeks between disclosure and dump are the real lesson: that's what the extortion clock looks like. Don't pay, and you get the dump; pay, and you get a promise. That RingCentral apparently said no is the right call with an unpleasant price tag, and it shouldn't be read as a failure.

What makes the combination volatile: RingCentral sells telephony. Names plus phone numbers plus addresses plus the information "this company uses exactly this phone system" add up to the perfect toolkit for the next call to the help desk — and the entry point for this incident was itself social engineering. The circle closes, and round two gets cheaper than round one.

The npm worm now waits for you to open the folder

A detail has surfaced about the ongoing keyv/cacheable campaign, which began on August 4 around 09:00 UTC, and it shifts the attack surface. The poisoned repository contained a .vscode/tasks.json with an "Environment Setup" task marked runOn: folderOpen, and a .claude/settings.json with a SessionStart hook — both built to run the payload as soon as a developer opens the directory. In both tools, that requires the user to grant trust first.[12]

That's the interesting shift: the classic supply-chain payload hangs off npm install, a step you can lock inside a container. This variant hangs off a dialog box — "Do you trust the authors of the files in this folder?" — that most of us have been reflexively dismissing for years because it was harmless a thousand times before. That makes the editor's trust prompt a real security boundary, and one that falls with a single click.

In practice: open unfamiliar repos in a devcontainer or a VM, not in your main editor; before the first open, run a quick ls -a and glance into .vscode/ and .claude/; turn off automatic task execution on folder open. That costs ten seconds per clone and replaces trust with a reflex check.

On the scope This campaign's package counts disagree between trackers, and that's not a measurement error — it's the state of an incident still in progress: Socket counted 442 versions across 353 package names, Aikido reports "at least 868 packages across 1,381 versions," and SafeDep's own tracker today (Aug 15) shows 2,234 poisoned versions across 444 package names, using credentials from twelve organizations — as reported on Aug 4, that figure was still 1,684 versions across 420 names from nine organizations.[12] Only the order of magnitude is reliable: hundreds of packages, upward of a thousand versions. Anyone citing a single number is citing a moment in time, not a truth.

Postgres follow-up: the server isn't the problem

In case the week got too full: on August 13, 18.6, 17.11, 16.15, 15.19, and 14.24 shipped, along with 19 Beta 3 — with 28 security flaws fixed and over 110 bug fixes. The version series jumps from 18.4 straight to 18.6, because 18.5 was never shipped due to a regression.[13]

The distribution is notable. Several of the 8.8-rated ones are server-side heap overflows (Regexp, to_char, plperl) plus a SQL injection via the EXTRACT argument — unpleasant, but only exploitable if someone can already issue queries. The two that hit the real blind spot sit in the client: CVE-2026-18408 allows code execution in psql when a pg_dump from a malicious source server is restored, and CVE-2026-6464 (8.1) makes psql process the following data as its own commands after an early failure of COPY FROM STDIN.[13]

Anyone who's ever restored a dump from an unfamiliar environment locally — a customer migration, debugging, a backup pulled from a hosting panel — has used exactly this trust model: "it's just text." It isn't. A dump is a script that runs with your privileges. Second calendar entry from the same announcement: PostgreSQL 14 gets its last fixes on November 12, 2026. Anyone still on 14 today has three months for a major upgrade — doable, but not a side project.

  1. postgresql.org (release dated Aug 13, 2026, accessed Aug 15, 2026) — "28 security vulnerabilities fixed"; "over 110 bugs reported over the last several months"; "This release skips PostgreSQL 18 versions from PostgreSQL 18.4 to 18.6. 18.5 was not shipped due to a regression."; "Regexp heap buffer overflow executes arbitrary code" / "to_char heap buffer overflow executes arbitrary code" / "plperl tied object heap buffer overflow executes arbitrary code" / "Expression deparse allows SQL injection via EXTRACT argument"; "PostgreSQL 14 will stop receiving fixes on November 12, 2026."
