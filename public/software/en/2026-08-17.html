E-Evidence: as of tomorrow, the small hosting provider is a recipient

Regulation (EU) 2023/1543 has been in force since August 18, 2023, but it came with a three-year transition period. That period ends tomorrow.[1][2] It doesn't change the whether, but the how: until now, a French prosecutor's office that wanted data from a German provider had to go through German authorities first. Going forward, the order goes straight to the provider.

European Production Order: An official form that lets a judicial authority in an EU country request user data directly from you as a service provider — without an authority in your country having to sign off first. Like a certified letter from a court that used to always pass through your local agency first and now drops straight into your mailbox. The catch: the letter comes with a built-in clock.

The clock is what matters operationally. The recipient must transmit the requested data within ten days at the latest after receipt, directly to the issuing authority — that's the binding rule under Article 10(2) of the regulation, restated in Recital 56.[1] In emergencies — an immediate danger to a person's life, physical integrity, or safety — it's eight hours.[2][3] A preservation order ("freeze the data, we'll decide later") binds you for 60 days, extendable once by another 30 days.[1]

Eight hours means: not "the lawyer will look at it Monday." It means an address someone actually reads, a designated representative, and a path to the data that doesn't depend on one specific person being awake. For a one-person operation, that's not a legal question — it's an on-call question.

The real point is who's covered. That includes electronic communications service providers (messaging, mail, video conferencing), domain and IP allocation services, and information society services through which users communicate or store data — cloud, hosting, platforms. The deciding factor is the market-location principle: what matters is whether people in the EU can use the service — not company size, not where it's headquartered.[2] A niche provider with three hundred users is just as much a recipient as a hyperscaler.

Flagged honestly The 10-day deadline is a binding rule under Article 10(2); Recital 56 only restates it, and today we could likewise verify the 60/30-day preservation deadline verbatim only against Recital 60. Recitals are interpretive aids, not a legal basis — anyone relying on them should cite the article.[1] Same goes for the 2% of worldwide annual turnover ceiling: Article 15 (Penalties) requires member states to allow for fines of up to 2% of the service provider's total worldwide turnover from the preceding financial year.[1] The often-cited €500,000 figure next to it is not in there — it comes from a law firm's write-up on national implementation.[2] The regulation doesn't name a euro amount; it requires "effective, proportionate and dissuasive" penalties: anyone who needs the exact euro figure should check the implementing law, not a blog post.

Linux 7.2 is final — the cut has happened

Update since Sun Yesterday this space said kernel.org still listed 7.2-rc7 from Aug 9 unchanged as of Sunday morning, with the cut still pending. It has happened: Mainline 7.2, released Aug 16, 2026.[4] That puts the cycle back on its usual schedule, and the rc8 question is moot.

On substance, LWN highlights four points: cache-aware CPU scheduling, large-folio support in Btrfs, shared attributes in the bpf() syscall, and work on the swap subsystem.[5] The first is the most interesting one for anything running on modern many-core servers.

Cache-aware scheduling: Every CPU core keeps a small stash of already-fetched data sitting right next to it — the cache. If the scheduler moves a running process to a different core, that stash is gone and has to be fetched again. Like sending a cook to a different counter mid-dish: they have to gather all the ingredients again. The scheduler is now learning to keep tasks closer to their warm cache.

The rest of the table: stable 7.1.8 (Aug 9), longterm 6.18.44, 6.12.103, 6.6.151 (all Aug 9), 6.1.182, 5.15.215, 5.10.264 (Aug 7).[4] If you're running a distro LTS, nothing changes today — 7.2 arrives at the earliest with the next distro jump. If you build your own, you now have a target.

rsync 3.5.0: 33 bugs, and that's the good news

rsync 3.5.0 was released on Aug 13, 2026 and closes 33 security vulnerabilities found during a focused audit of path handling and the daemon protocol — backed by a fuzzing run against the daemon protocol and reports from external researchers.[6] That's what the project's own release notes say.[7] The CVE identifiers were assigned by VulnCheck as CNA. New is a hardened path resolution (secure_relative_open()) that resolves paths component by component and only follows symlinks that are owned by uid 0 or the running user; every fix ships with a regression test that fails on the unpatched tree.[7]

The number sounds like a disaster and is the opposite. 33 findings in a tool that's been sitting in practically every backup script for nearly three decades doesn't mean "rsync is broken" — it means someone finally shone a systematic light into the corner where this stuff accumulates. Tools nobody audits don't have zero vulnerabilities. They have uncounted ones.

What matters operationally is the mode: anyone running rsync only over SSH against their own machines — the normal case — is far less exposed than someone exposing an rsyncd to the network. The identifier the release notes rate critical, CVE-2026-53791, affects exactly the daemon configuration with proxy protocol = true: there, a directly connected client could spoof its source address and thereby bypass host-based access control.[7] Order of operations today: first check whether some forgotten rsync daemon is running anywhere, then update.

Sourcing Everything here is stated in the project's own NEWS file at download.samba.org — the finding count, the audit scope, secure_relative_open(), the CNA assignment to VulnCheck, and the "CRITICAL" rating for CVE-2026-53791.[7] Anyone who needs the full list of all 33 identifiers for their inventory should pull it directly from there, not from a news report.

GeoServer: a zero-day that still doesn't have a CVE number

GeoServer — the open-source server that agencies and utilities use to serve their geodata — has a SQL injection reachable without authentication that can lead to code execution. Rated CVSS 9.8. It was disclosed publicly on Aug 12 on X, and exploitation attempts began, per reporting, within hours — hundreds of attempts from a small pool of IP addresses. Confirmed compromises had not been reported as of Aug 13.[8] Fixed in 3.0.1, 2.28.5, and 2.27.6.

GHSA instead of CVE: A CVE number is a vulnerability's global ID card — scanners, inventory lists, and compliance reports all hang off it. A GHSA identifier is GitHub's own case number, which can be assigned instantly. Like a hospital case number before your insurer has generated its own claim number: same case, but your billing system doesn't know it yet. The practical consequence — here GHSA-mqjf-5f49-2fjh — your vulnerability scanner reports clean, and the hole is still there.

That's why this story is here: it shows a gap in the process, not just in the product. Anyone relying on "no CVE hit" meaning "not affected" has a blind spot for a few days. For a 9.8 that's being scraped within hours, that's whole days.

macOS Screen Sharing: 9.8, and a miner at the end

CVE-2026-65400 is an authentication bypass in the Screen Sharing component of macOS, CVSS 9.8, in Apple's phrasing "an authentication issue was addressed with improved state management." It's being actively exploited against Macs that are reachable directly from the internet; what gets installed afterward is a Monero miner.[9] The warning came from the Dutch NCSC, after public exploit code surfaced.[10] A patch has been available since Aug 6, 2026: Apple closed the flaw in an emergency update for macOS Tahoe 26.6.1, macOS Sequoia 15.7.9, and macOS Sonoma 14.8.9.[9]

What's notable is how modest the goal is: no spy implant, no extortion operation — someone's turning other people's Macs into a compute farm. It's about volume, not selection, and you get selected the moment your Mac is reachable via port forwarding for some historical reason. The test takes a minute: Screen Sharing on? Port 5900 open to the outside? Two no's — then this is a news item, not an incident. One yes — then the order of operations is: update to 26.6.1 / 15.7.9 / 14.8.9, then remove the port forwarding.

Microsoft delays Exchange CU1 — and points at the machines

Cumulative Update 1 for Exchange Server Subscription Edition was originally announced for the first half of 2026, got pushed to "second half of 2026" in May, and now has no date at all.[15] The Exchange team cites the backlog of vulnerability findings as the reason — and the machine generating that backlog is Microsoft's own: the team says it's "using a variety of AI tools to find vulnerabilities in our products." Every finding then has to be validated, reproduced, fixed, tested, and shipped — and that's exactly what's tying up the team.[16]

This belongs here because the hook isn't a model announcement, it's a delivery capability. What's notable is the direction: nobody here is complaining about submissions from outside. Microsoft turned the finder on itself, and the bill is arriving internally — landing on the very team that's supposed to be building the update. That's the more honest version of the story, and the more uncomfortable one: searching can be scaled up arbitrarily with AI, validating and fixing cannot. Point an AI tool at your own codebase and you buy yourself a backlog that people still have to work through — and, in the case of the first vendor this size to do so, that means delaying a paid subscription product.

It's still fair to read this with an eyebrow raised. "Our own AI findings are tying us up" is a convenient justification for a delay running past a year, and Microsoft doesn't cite a number — no finding volume, no false-positive rate, no headcount cost. Without that, it stays an explanation, not a measurement. Fittingly, Germany's BSI and domestic intelligence agency jointly warned on Aug 3 that AI is making cyber operations not just faster, but above all more accessible.[17]

It's Monday, August seventeenth. Today comes with a real deadline, and it isn't far off — it's tomorrow. Starting August eighteenth, the European Union's E-Evidence Regulation becomes fully applicable.

Here's what that means in short. Until now, a prosecutor's office in one EU country that wanted user data from a provider in another had to go through that country's authorities first. Starting tomorrow, the order goes straight to the provider. The regulation's own text sets ten days as the standard deadline. In emergencies — meaning immediate danger to someone's life or physical safety — it's eight hours. A preservation order locks the data down for sixty days, extendable once by another thirty.

The key question is who this applies to, and the answer is: almost everyone. It comes down to the market-location principle. All that matters is whether people in the EU can use the service — not company size, not where the company is based. A hosting provider with three hundred users is just as much a target as a giant cloud company. In practice, eight hours means an address someone actually reads, a designated representative, and a way to get at the data that doesn't depend on one specific person being awake. On the penalty side, we sharpened the numbers: the two percent of worldwide annual turnover ceiling is written into Article fifteen of the regulation itself. Last, a story worth remembering. Microsoft is delaying the first cumulative update for Exchange Server Subscription Edition, and cites the backlog of vulnerability findings from its own AI tools as the reason. The Exchange team writes that Microsoft uses a variety of AI tools to find flaws in its own products — so this isn't about outside submissions, it's about self-generated findings that the same team building the update also has to validate and fix. Searching can be scaled up arbitrarily with AI; fixing can't. Microsoft hasn't put a number on it, though, so for now it's an explanation, not a measurement.
