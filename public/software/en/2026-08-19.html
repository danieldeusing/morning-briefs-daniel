Seven Billion for the Switchboard

On Sunday, Bloomberg reported that Stripe had completed the purchase of OpenRouter — for more than $7B.[1] OpenRouter had last been valued at $1.3B in May 2026; the reported price is thus roughly 5.4 times that in three months.[2] According to the reports, the platform routes more than 400 models from over 60 providers and serves around 8M developers.[1][2] The company had previously raised just over $150M from CapitalG, Andreessen Horowitz, and Menlo Ventures.[3]

Model Router: An intermediary layer between your application and the AI providers. Instead of separate code, keys, and bills for every provider, you talk to one address, and the layer decides which provider actually answers the request. Think of a flight-comparison site: one login, one booking, one bill — which airline actually flies you is a line of fine print. The side effect is that the site sees every booking and sets the price.

The sourcing here is explicitly messy, and that's part of the story. A month ago the figure floating around was "around $10B"; another reporting thread cites "up to $8B in cash and stock"; the final report says $7B.[4] Neither company has confirmed the deal; Stripe generally doesn't comment on rumors, and every number — the purchase price, the 8M developers, the 400+ models — comes from that same reporting wave on Aug 16/17.[1][2] As additional color, it's been reported that Stripe is simultaneously pursuing a PayPal acquisition with Advent International, and that its unsolicited $53B offer was rejected — that's secondary reporting I could not independently verify.[4]

Why this belongs here and not in the AI brief: this isn't a model story, it's an infrastructure consolidation. A payments processor is buying the layer that measures and bills usage — just in tokens instead of euros. The purchase price doesn't buy models; it buys the position in between.

For your own architecture, something concrete follows from this. The reason a router is convenient — one API, one key, one bill — is the same reason it's a chokepoint: pricing, availability ordering, and data sharing are now decided by someone whose business model is transaction fees. The cheap countermeasure costs an afternoon: stick to the OpenAI-compatible interface, keep provider-specific extras out of your business logic, and track your own usage yourself. Then switching providers is a config line, not a quarter-long project.

Four at Once, Deadline Friday

On Aug 18, CISA added four vulnerabilities to its known-exploited-vulnerabilities catalog. I checked three of them individually in the NVD, and each carries the same pair of dates: Date Added 08/18/2026, Due Date 08/21/2026.[5][6][7] Three days — that's short, and the wording of the deadline requirement is new: it calls for remediation "in accordance with vendor instructions, ensuring compliance with CISA's BOD 26-04 Prioritizing Security Updates Based on Risk guidance and CISA's 'Forensics Triage Requirements.'"[5]

The four, individually:

CVE-2026-33824, CVSS 9.8 (assigned by Microsoft) — "Double free in Windows IKE Extension allows an unauthorized attacker to execute code over a network."[5] The entry was published on April 14, 2026. Four months of a patch being available, and now exploitation.

Double Free: A program frees the same block of memory twice. Like handing the same locker key to the front desk twice — by the second time, that locker already belongs to someone else, and the desk opens it for you anyway. Whoever times it right controls what's sitting in that locker; for a network service, that's enough to run someone else's code inside the process.

CVE-2026-55040, CVSS 9.1 — "Weak authentication in Microsoft Office SharePoint allows an unauthorized attacker to bypass a security feature over a network," published on July 14, 2026.[6] Germany's BSI, for its part, is separately warning about an actively exploited SharePoint flaw; whether it's the same identifier could not be cross-checked today.

CVE-2026-65400 — the macOS Screen Sharing flaw, covered in its own section alongside this one.[7] And CVE-2026-59310, the vCenter flaw already covered here on the 15th and the 18th. The fourth identifier comes from the search summary of CISA's announcement — cisa.gov returned a 403 again on direct access.[8]

The pattern behind the list is the real point: two of the four are Microsoft entries from April and July — the patch was available for months. What changed yesterday isn't the vulnerability, it's the knowledge that someone is using it. The deadline is only binding for US federal agencies — for everyone else, the KEV catalog is still the cheapest exploit-intelligence feed there is: a short list you can automate against your asset inventory without having to argue over CVSS thresholds.

Screen Sharing Without a Password

The most notable of the four new KEV additions is Apple's CVE-2026-65400. The wording in the NVD entry is Apple's own and leaves little room for interpretation: "An authentication issue was addressed with improved state management … An attacker on the network may be able to authenticate to Screen Sharing without valid credentials."[7] In other words: anyone on the same network can log into Screen Sharing — without valid credentials. This has been fixed since Aug 6 in macOS Tahoe 26.6.1, Sequoia 15.7.9, and Sonoma 14.8.9.[7] heise independently reported active exploitation yesterday at 12:33.[9]

Two details are worth a closer look. First, the 9.8: it doesn't come from Apple, but from CISA's own enrichment (listed in the entry as "CISA-ADP").[7] Apple doesn't assign CVSS scores — anyone using scores as an automated threshold will rank Apple vulnerabilities differently, or not at all, depending on the data source. Second, "improved state management": not a memory-safety bug, but a state confusion in the login flow — the service treats a half-finished session as a completed one.

In practice, Screen Sharing on macOS is off by default. But it gets switched on for a single remote-support session — and then stays on. That leftover is exactly the attack surface: not a server, but a developer laptop on café Wi-Fi that someone helped with three months ago. Checking "General → Sharing" in System Settings takes ten seconds. Separately, Apple shipped another update round yesterday — iOS 26.6.1, macOS 26.6.2, and others — covering, per heise, roughly 30 flaws across six operating systems.[9]

The Scanners Beat the Asset Inventory

Two vulnerabilities reported together yesterday say less about their products than about pace. MLflow — the tracking server where teams log experiments, metrics, and artifacts — has an unauthenticated SSRF in versions before 3.15.0, CVE-2026-64849, CVSS 9.3. It allows "HTTP requests to arbitrary internal cloud metadata endpoints" and lets an attacker harvest credentials there.[10]

SSRF Against the Metadata Endpoint: Every cloud VM has an internal address reachable only from the machine itself, which hands out the machine's own credentials on request — convenient, since no key needs to live in the code. SSRF means tricking a service into making a request for you. Like asking the concierge to fetch the key rack from the staff room: you'd never be allowed back there yourself, but he can go, and he won't ask why. Result: the server fetches its own cloud credentials and hands them out the front door.

The number that matters here isn't the 9.3, it's the time window. According to watchTowr, attackers were "indiscriminately … scanning for exposed MLflow instances online within hours of the CVE being assigned on August 17, 2026."[10] Not hours after an exploit dropped — hours after the identifier was assigned. If you plan your patch window in weeks, you're planning against a clock that runs in hours.

The same picture shows up with FUXA, a web-based SCADA interface: CVE-2026-25895, CVSS 9.8, through 1.2.9 inclusive — missing authentication plus path traversal, enabling attackers to "write arbitrary files to the server file system and achieve remote code execution." VulnCheck observed a single IP scanning broadly starting Aug 18; roughly 60 installations are publicly reachable, and no RCE payloads have been dropped yet.[10]

Both belong to the same species: an internal tool built without its own authentication — and then given a public IP anyway, because someone wanted to check on it from the road. The effective fix isn't the patch, it's asking why the thing is reachable from the internet at all. Sixty FUXA instances is a small number; sixty instances controlling a plant is a big one.

Catching Up: docker cp Also Copies Where It Shouldn't

This one slipped through here until now, and it hits Daniel's own toolbox directly. CVE-2026-17106, dubbed "CopyEscape": a malicious container can break out of the specified target directory during docker cp and overwrite files on the host — in some configurations, up to and including code execution as root. It's fixed in Docker Engine and CLI 29.7.2, Docker Desktop 4.86.0, and Docker Sandboxes 0.38.0; the disclosure process ran since April 2026 and had to be extended multiple times because an earlier fix caused regressions.[11]

The flawed assumption is baked into the command's shape. docker cp looks like cp: two paths, done. In reality, it's an instruction to the daemon, and the daemon runs as root. Whatever paths, symlinks, and intermediate states the container supplies aren't just a filename — they're input to a privileged service. Anyone copying artifacts from untrusted images to the host in CI should check their version.

For context: the fix is about two weeks old (the Sandboxes release landed on Aug 6), and the details come from the reporting vendor's own blog post, not from Docker's own advisory page. Not a fresh incident — a version you need to catch up on.

  1. imperva.com (August 2026) — "CVE-2026-17106 is a Docker vulnerability that allows a malicious container to break out of the destination specified by docker cp and overwrite files on the host … nicknamed 'CopyEscape'"; "Docker has patched the issue in Docker Engine and CLI 29.7.2, Docker Desktop 4.86.0, and Docker Sandboxes 0.38.0, following a disclosure process that began in April 2026 and required multiple extensions to resolve regressions from an earlier fix." Supply Chain: Doubled, and Now in Ruby Too

npm: 2,225 Versions

Update since Sun The keyv/cacheable campaign from August 4 keeps growing. On Sunday, this brief reported 434 packages across 1,381 versions; the current tally (Sonatype, via Security Boulevard) puts it at 2,225 affected component versions, totaling over 2B downloads per month, spreading at "50 to 100 newly infected packages every few minutes."[12] Affected downstream users named include Deliveroo, ServiceTitan, Picsart, and Qlik.

The mechanism is unchanged: a preinstall hook pulls in a standalone Bun runtime, harvests npm, GitHub, AWS, Vault, and Kubernetes credentials, and republishes the next wave using the stolen tokens.[13] Every victim becomes the next distributor — this thing doesn't scale with the attacker's own effort, it scales with the number of installs.

RubyGems: 16 Typos

In parallel, the trade press is reporting 16 typosquatted RubyGems packages under the name StubMaker, which harvest browser credentials, crypto wallets, seed phrases, and Telegram data.[14] A small incident with a bigger point: this playbook has become ecosystem-agnostic. Whatever helps against npm helps here too — take lockfiles seriously, don't let install hooks run unvetted in CI, keep publish tokens separate from build tokens. None of this is new, and that's exactly the point.

The Assistant Layer Is Now an Attack Surface

Two reports from yesterday belong here rather than in the AI brief — because they're about file and protocol design, not about models. First, CoSnitch: three flaws in Microsoft Copilot Personal, grouped under CVE-2026-24301, where a single crafted link is enough to quietly exfiltrate data from connected applications; at its core is an undocumented URL parameter.[15] An undocumented parameter that changes behavior isn't an AI problem — it's an interface with no contract.

Second, a study on self-propagating payloads that spread between agents via editable, persistent prompt files, demonstrated across chains of six collaborating coding agents.[15] Translated into filesystem terms: a file in the project folder that a tool reads on every startup and that the same process is also allowed to write is executable surface. We already know this from .envrc and .vscode/tasks.json — the npm worm took exactly this route two weeks ago. The takeaway applies to any tool with project-level config: files like this belong in code review just like source code does, not in the "it's just config" drawer.
