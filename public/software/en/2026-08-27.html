AWS Buys the People Who Build DuckDB

Amazon Web Services announced yesterday that it is acquiring DuckLabs — the company founded by DuckDB's authors that carries the bulk of the project's development and support. Financial terms were not disclosed. AWS has committed that DuckDB and its related projects will remain "free and open source under their MIT licenses" and that the nonprofit DuckDB Foundation will continue to govern them. DuckLabs chief Hannes Mühleisen wants to expand the Foundation's role instead, into a place "where the stakeholders or the people that are building things with DuckDB are coming together."

In-process OLAP database: DuckDB is to analytics what SQLite is to regular application data — it doesn't run as a server you connect to, it runs directly inside your program. Instead of driving to the library, the shelf is in your room. "OLAP" means optimized for crunching large columns, not for storing individual rows.

The most telling line comes from AWS engineer Andy Warfield: DuckDB is "broadly used and very much loved by S3 customers today." That's what Amazon is paying for — not a database product, but the standard engine people use to crunch Parquet files in object storage.

My take: Fixating on the license is the comfortable mistake. MIT can't be revoked for code already shipped, so "stays MIT" costs AWS nothing — and changing the license would also be the dumbest thing you could do to a community you just bought. What's actually shifting is the attention of paid full-time maintainers. The roadmap now has an owner, and that owner has a very concrete interest in how well DuckDB talks to S3. That doesn't have to end badly — core developers paid by a corporation instead of consulting gigs have more time for the database. But the honest question isn't "am I still allowed to use this" (yes), it's "whose priorities sit at the top of the issue tracker in eighteen months." Nothing to do today; if you've built DuckDB deep into your stack, watch the first release after the acquisition closely.

One Cease-and-Desist Letter, and the Open X Frontends Are Gone

XCancel wrote: "On Monday 24th August at 8 PM EST, we received at [sic] letter from X Corp. asking to cease and desist the service XCancel." Nitter's maintainer followed up: "nitter.net is offline and development has stopped for the time being. I'm seeking legal advice and won't be commenting further on the specifics for now." The GitHub repository is archived and read-only. Both services were open frontends that let people read X content without an account and without tracking.

My take: A cease-and-desist letter is not a ruling. Nobody determined that Nitter was illegal — it's just that nobody has the means left to find out whether it was. That's exactly the mechanism, and it's cheaper than any lawsuit: you don't have to win, the other side just has to be unable to afford the question. In practice, that means any read layer that sits on someone else's goodwill — a frontend, an unofficial API, a scraper — is a dependency with zero notice period. In this newsletter, the fallback route has long run through fxtwitter.com; that's goodwill too, not a contract.

Self-Hosted Email: From 44.6 to 22.4 Percent

Artem Berezin of Live Direct Marketing analyzed DNS records — MX, SPF, DMARC — from the OpenINTEL project against the Tranco top-1-million domain list for the Internet Society's blog, spanning ten years. Result: the share of self-operated mail servers fell from 44.6% (2016) to 22.4% (2026). Google Workspace accounts for 21.8% of identifiable MX records, Microsoft 365 for 16.8% — 38.6% combined.

MX record: the signpost in DNS that says which server accepts mail for a domain. Like the sign on the street that tells you which post office handles that address — from the outside, it shows you who's actually doing the delivering.

Berezin's argument isn't "Big Tech is evil," it's systemic risk: "When more than a third of popular domains depend on two providers to receive mail, an outage, a filtering change, or a policy decision at either one propagates through the whole ecosystem at once." And unlike with a CDN, there's no second try — a rejected message is simply gone. On top of that there's a feedback loop: independent operators inherit the "deliverability problems of a world tuned for the big two," because the two providers' spam filters effectively set the standard. Berezin acknowledges that his classification dictionaries don't capture the entire mail infrastructure — the finding is the direction of the curve, not the second decimal place.

My take: The Zimbra story elsewhere in this issue is the other half of the same story. Self-hosted email is being squeezed from two sides — deliverability calibrated to two providers, and a stack whose unpatched instances get taken over in droves. Anyone still self-hosting today is doing it out of conviction — and needs to actually keep up the patch cadence that requires.

GitHub Actions Went Down Again — Five Days After the Promise

GitHub Actions was disrupted yesterday from 15:11 UTC until 18:00 UTC — nearly 2 hours 49 minutes. According to the incident report, the trigger was a database primary; GitHub failed over to a replica, which "did not fully mitigate" the disruption, and then throttled incoming traffic while investigating upstream Vitess issues. By 18:00 UTC, Actions was running "as expected" again.

Vitess: a layer that spreads a single MySQL database across many machines while making the application believe it's talking to just one. Like a single counter at a government office with fifty clerks behind it — fine, until the desk that decides who gets your form jams.

The timing is the real irritant. GitHub CTO Vladimir Fedorov wrote in a postmortem on Aug 21: "We'll earn your trust through the scaling and reliability of the platform." That postmortem followed the Aug 17 outage, which ran nearly eight hours and hit Issues, Pull Requests, APIs, Actions, and Copilot simultaneously. Five days later, Actions is down again. The Register counts at least 23 reliability incidents per month for 2026; for August, Actions stands at 98.13% availability — over a full 31-day month, the missing 1.87% works out to nearly 14 hours.

My take: Two outages in ten days isn't a trend yet, but it exposes an architectural decision many of us made without deciding to make it. If you deploy on push — merge to main, workflow runs, artifact goes live — an Actions outage leaves you with no deploy at all. No hotfix, no rollback, no emergency exit. That's negotiable, but only in advance: a documented local path that doesn't need a runner, tested once a quarter. For registry publishes through OIDC trust chains, that's structurally impossible — there, CI is the only legitimate sender, which is exactly why you should know which of your pipelines fall into that category.

Kubernetes 1.37 Cleans House: kube-dns, IPVS, cgroup v1

Kubernetes 1.37 shipped yesterday: 67 changes, 16 of them graduating to stable, 23 in beta, 27 new alpha features, and one deprecation. The three cleanup items run on different clocks. kube-dns, the original built-in DNS server, is being retired in favor of CoreDNS — clusters that haven't switched by 1.40 will simply stop working. IPVS in kube-proxy is being removed as of 1.43, with nftables as the successor. And cgroup v1 no longer supports node bootstrapping.

Along the way, the metrics API metrics.k8s.io graduated to stable after nine years in beta. Release lead Dipesh Rawat on that: "Despite the Beta label, the metrics.k8s.io API has been widely used in production for years." Nothing functional changes — it's a recognition of reality, not a rebuild.

My take: Two of the three deadlines are comfortable — at three releases a year, 1.40 is roughly a year out, 1.43 two years. The line that matters even outside Kubernetes is cgroup v1: container runtimes, systemd, and observability tools have written it off one by one. So the real takeaway from this story applies even to plain Docker hosts with no Kubernetes in sight: run stat -fc %T /sys/fs/cgroup once. If that comes back tmpfs instead of cgroup2fs, a distro upgrade is due, and that's not an afternoon job.

Debian: Eight Options, One Day Left

The general resolution vote on LLM-generated contributions ends tomorrow at 23:59:59 UTC, with the result expected Saturday. Eight proposals are on the ballot — numbered and lettered, each backed by six to seventeen developers. The range runs from an outright ban on any contribution produced with LLM assistance, through conditional acceptance — license compatibility, attribution, accountability, disclosure, coordination on bulk changes — to a mere recommendation to avoid generative AI where practical. Only recognized Debian developers are eligible to vote.

What's interesting isn't so much which option wins as the fact that eight of them found backers at all. Debian isn't really negotiating a tool here, it's negotiating who stands behind a line of code — and other projects will cite whatever answer it lands on.

Zimbra: The Deadline Has Passed, 270 Servers Have Not

Update since Fri CVE-2026-73570 is an unauthenticated remote code execution flaw in the Zimbra Collaboration Suite's SNMP component, CVSS 8.9 (vector AV:N/AC:H/PR:N/UI:N/S:C/C:H/I:H/A:L). CISA added it to the KEV catalog on Aug 21, with a three-day deadline for US federal agencies, Aug 24. Versions before 10.1.20 with the zimbra-snmp package installed and SNMP notifications enabled are affected; the patch dates to Jul 20.

The number that matters after the deadline: Shadowserver's scan for signs of exploitation found more than 270 already-compromised instances. CERT Polska had reported active attacks since Aug 17 — four days before the KEV listing, and exactly four weeks after the patch.

Heads up With this pattern, patching alone isn't enough. Five weeks separate the patch (Jul 20) from the deadline (Aug 24) — anyone updating only now may be updating a system that's already been taken over. After the update, check accounts, forwarding rules, and filters, not just the version number.

ReliaQuest vs. ShinyHunters — and the Fight Over a Word

On Aug 22, the ShinyHunters group targeted security vendor ReliaQuest with a fake SSO login page plus phone calls. An employee entered their password and approved the MFA push. On Aug 23, the attackers posted purported screenshots of an Okta dashboard to a leak site. ReliaQuest confirms the attack but disputes the framing: access was "view only," it says, with no applications or systems reached and no customer data affected.

My take: The argument over the word "breach" is the least interesting part of this. What's interesting is that a security vendor got caught by the oldest trick in the book — and that more training wouldn't have helped here. A push confirmation is weak because a human makes the call, and humans decide differently after a plausible-sounding phone call. Phishing-resistant methods take that decision out of the equation.

Phishing-resistant login: A passkey only signs for the exact domain it was created for — a convincing copy at a different address gets nothing it can reuse. Like a car key that only opens that one car, not every car that looks like it.

Germany's BSI: Chrome and GIMP, Both Critical

Germany's BSI warned on Aug 21 of critical vulnerabilities in the Chrome browser and the GIMP graphics program. They're fixed in Chrome desktop versions 151.0.7922.173 and 151.0.7922.174, and in GIMP 3.2.4. The most severe entry in the Aug 20 Chrome release is CVE-2026-76017, a use-after-free in Chromoting, Chrome's remote-access component: crafted network traffic, code execution outside the sandbox.

That last phrase is the crucial one: the browser normally locks every page into a padded cell, "outside the sandbox" means the foreign code runs with your privileges. Chrome updates itself silently, but only takes effect after a restart — chrome://settings/help answers in five seconds whether one is still pending. For many people, GIMP comes from the distro package, where it's happily two versions behind.

CRA: Two Weeks to the Deadline, Standards Arrive This Weekend

The first hard-deadline stage of the EU Cyber Resilience Act begins on Sep 11, 2026: manufacturers of connected products sold into the EU must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents to ENISA and the relevant national CSIRT within 24 hours — under Art. 69(3), this also applies to already-shipped products. The harmonized standards for this (type A vulnerability handling, type B product security) aren't expected until Aug 30.

Twelve days between the standard and the deadline isn't preparation time. Anyone subject to the reporting duty should build the reporting chain now on a best-effort basis and adjust it later: name who's responsible, clarify the contact path to the CSIRT, prepare a report template. The clock starts on awareness, not on how mature your understanding of the incident is.

It's Thursday, August twenty-seventh. Yesterday, Wednesday, produced four stories that happen to all point in the same direction: the independently run middle of the internet is getting rolled up.

The biggest one: Amazon Web Services bought DuckLabs, the company behind DuckDB. DuckDB does for analytics what SQLite does for everyday application data — it doesn't run as a server, it runs directly inside your program. The purchase price wasn't disclosed. Amazon says DuckDB will stay free under the MIT license, with the nonprofit Foundation still steering it.

Still, fixating on the license would be the easy mistake. MIT can't be revoked for code that's already out there, so that promise costs Amazon nothing. What's actually shifting is the attention of paid, full-time developers. One AWS engineer says DuckDB is deeply loved by S-three customers — that tells you exactly what Amazon is paying for. Nothing to do today, but if you've built DuckDB into your stack, watch the first release after the deal closely.

The second story is shorter and sharper. On Monday night, X Corp sent cease-and-desist letters to Nitter and XCancel, the two open frontends that let people read X content without an account. Both shut down immediately; nitter dot net is offline, and its GitHub repository is archived. A cease-and-desist letter isn't a court ruling — nobody proved Nitter was illegal. It's just that nobody has the resources left to find out. You don't have to win; the other side just has to be unable to afford asking the question.

A related data point fits right in. A researcher analyzed ten years of DNS records for the internet's top one million domains for the Internet Society. Self-hosted mail servers fell from forty-four point six percent back in twenty sixteen to twenty-two point four percent today. Google sits at twenty-one point eight percent, Microsoft at sixteen point eight — thirty-eight point six percent combined. His point isn't that the big providers are evil, it's that a single filtering change at either one now ripples through the whole ecosystem at once. And unlike a content delivery network, there's no second chance — a rejected email is simply gone.

The fourth story is the one that stings the most. GitHub Actions went down for almost three hours yesterday afternoon, from eleven minutes past three to six p.m. UTC. The cause was a database issue; failing over to a backup only partly fixed it. The timing is what hurts: five days earlier, GitHub's CTO had written in a report on the previous outage that the company would earn back trust through reliability. That earlier outage, on August seventeenth, lasted almost eight hours. If you only deploy by pushing to your main branch, an Actions outage leaves you with no way to deploy at all — no hotfix, no rollback. Everyone should have a documented, manual fallback, and test it once a quarter.

Two more items from the security desk. The US federal deadline for patching a critical Zimbra flaw passed on Monday — the bug scores eight point nine out of ten and allows code execution without logging in — yet researchers still found more than two hundred seventy already-compromised servers. More training wouldn't have prevented that — what would have is a login method where the key itself checks which address it's talking to.
