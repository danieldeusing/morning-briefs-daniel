Berlin: 5.8 Terabytes Gone, 30 Bitcoin Demanded — and We've Seen This Number Before

The ransomware group Rhysida is extorting the state of Berlin. According to reporting by Der Spiegel, covered by heise on 08/29, the gang is demanding 30 Bitcoin — roughly €2 million — to keep around 5.8 terabytes from Berlin's Senate administration from being published. Governing Mayor Kai Wegner publicly confirmed the extortion attempt on 08/28, with a sentence that leaves no room for interpretation: "The state of Berlin will not be extorted." According to the report, the data was exfiltrated between 08/07 and 08/12; the attack became known in mid-August, the administrations were disconnected from the state network for about a week, and have been reconnected since 08/23. The investigation is being carried out by the public prosecutor's office, Berlin's state criminal police, and federal authorities.

Double extortion: Extortionists used to just encrypt data — if you had a backup, you restored it and were done. Today they copy the data out first and threaten to publish it. Like a burglar who doesn't just change the lock, but also takes photos of the living room: a spare key does nothing against the second problem.

What the gang lists on its leak site is the actually uncomfortable part: around 46,500 municipal contracts, 80,000 regulatory-offense case files, documents on critical infrastructure, emergency plans — and 6,000 files with access credentials. The first items are a confidentiality and data-protection problem. The last one is a blueprint for the next round.

My take: There's a number attached to this story that shouldn't get skimmed past. 30 Bitcoin is exactly what Rhysida demanded from the city of Columbus, Ohio, in 2024 — back then for 6.5 TB, including server dumps from emergency services and camera access.[3] Same amount, same data category, two continents, two years apart. What that means: the price isn't an appraisal of Berlin's data, it's a tariff for the category "large public administration." What's being negotiated is a discount, not a value — which is why "we won't pay" here isn't a negotiating tactic, it's the only position that doesn't confirm the tariff.

Heads up The five days between the data exfiltration (08/07–08/12) and it becoming known are the metric that matters for your own environment — not the terabytes. Exfiltration at this scale isn't a quiet process: it's an unusually large volume of outbound data over several days. Anyone who only monitors inbound traffic sees none of it.

Citrix: the Deadline Has Passed, the Devices Are Still There

The CISA deadline for CVE-2026-8452 in NetScaler ADC and Gateway expired yesterday — mandated via directive BOD 26-04, after the flaw entered the KEV catalog on 08/26. Shadowserver supplies the scale behind it: over 22,000 NetScaler ADC appliances and just under 1,800 Gateway instances are reachable from the internet. That's the reachable surface, not the vulnerable one — the count doesn't say how many are patched. Exploitation is widespread: attackers are spraying exploits across unpatched instances and dropping webshells.

Webshell: A tiny file the attacker drops on the server and then calls up through an ordinary browser — it takes commands and runs them. Like a spare key that isn't hanging by the lock but is embedded in the wall: replacing the lock (i.e., patching) doesn't make it useless.

My take: The instructive part is the backstory. Citrix classified the flaw in June as a memory overflow that "only" causes a crash; only an external analysis later showed code execution as root. A vendor's initial assessment is a hypothesis, not a fact — and anyone who sets their patch window by it loses exactly the two months that got lost here. The second point follows directly from the webshell: for a device that's been sitting open on the network for weeks, the version number after the update only answers half the question. The other half is: what's sitting on the box now, and which credentials passed through it?

CISA Crunches the Numbers: the Classics Are Still Winning

For its Vulnerability Review, CISA analyzed which flaw classes were actually exploited in 2024 and 2025 — and the result is depressingly unspectacular. At the top sit injection flaws (XSS, OS command injection, SQL injection) and improper input validation (CWE-20), the latter described by the agency as "the single most-common weakness type across the KEV catalog and registered CVEs." For 2024: seven of the ten most common classes in the KEV catalog are so-called "stubborn weaknesses" — together 41.5% of all entries. 2025 looks the same, with seven of the top ten having been rated "unforgivable" since a 2007 MITRE assessment. Memory safety and input validation together account for 16.7% of 2025's KEV entries.

CWE: A CVE is a single flaw in a specific product. A CWE is the type of flaw it belongs to — not "the broken lock at house number 12," but "locks that can be picked with a paperclip." Here, CISA is counting types, not houses.

My take: The sentence the agency hangs the entire analysis on is the remarkable one: "the problem is not technical complexity: it is organizational culture, developer workflows, and systemic gaps in Secure by Design adoption." That's a strikingly uncomfortable admission from the industry about itself. We've known how to prevent SQL injection for twenty years — it's in every intro course, there are libraries that make it structurally impossible. And yet it's still a top offender in 2025. That's not for lack of knowledge — it's because nobody pays for the time it takes to migrate old code onto safe building blocks. If there's one takeaway: the lever isn't the next training session, it's retiring the patterns that allow the flaw to exist in the first place — parameterized queries with no exceptions, templates that escape by default, types instead of strings at system boundaries.

npm Supply Chain: No New Stage, But a Trick That's Here to Stay

Since the ChainDrop wave of 08/04 — which began through the hijacked maintainer account of keyv and spread via preinstall scripts in cacheable, flat-cache, and relatives — there's been no new escalation stage. The circulating numbers keep growing (from 435 packages at StepSecurity to over 1,300 in later analyses, with reach estimated around two billion downloads per month), but that's a counting-method effect: packages versus package versions, narrow versus broad definitions of the Shai-Hulud family. Not spread — different rulers.

My take: What actually endures from this campaign isn't the package count, it's the anchoring of the control infrastructure in an Ethereum smart contract: the malware asks the blockchain where to phone home, and the operator switches the address with a single transaction. That's immune to domain blocklists — you can't shut down a blockchain. The only layer that sits upstream of that is the one where the script starts running at all: install scripts from dependencies. That's exactly where npm v12 comes in, with npm approve-scripts and a project-level approval requirement. Until that reaches broad adoption, --ignore-scripts in CI is the cheapest insurance policy you can buy.

Brave 1.94: Disposable Addresses Straight in Autofill

Brave's desktop version 1.94 brings Email Aliases: when signing up on a website, the browser generates a disposable address, forwards mail to the real one, and keeps the real address out of the form. It's generated straight from the email field via autofill or the context menu, and managed in settings. In this first version, five aliases are free; a paid tier and mobile support have been announced. Behind it is a new account system in which, per the vendor, the password never reaches Brave's servers.

My take: Technically, this is nothing new — Apple, Fastmail, SimpleLogin, and a self-hosted catch-all alias have been able to do this for years. What's new is the location: inside the browser, right in the signup form, with no second service. The benefit isn't primarily anti-tracking either, but small-scale forensics: if spam arrives at shop-xy@…, there's no debate about who leaked or lost the address. The flip side is lock-in — five aliases are bait, and anyone running a hundred of them is tied to the forwarding service. Anyone with their own domain gets the same thing, vendor-independently.

The Week in Review: the Attack Surface Was Procurement

Six stories, one pattern. PaperCut: a printing solution that nobody perceives as software. ZBT: routers with a factory-installed backdoor, sold under other brands' names. The npm wave: packages nobody consciously installed, but that came along as third-order dependencies. Citrix: an appliance bought as hardware and then treated like furniture ever since. Berlin: an administration whose attack surface has grown over decades. And the TeamPCP arrests in Australia, with over a thousand affected organizations, were the tally of exactly the same layer.

My take: None of these stories is about a clever exploit. They're all about someone bringing something into the house without anyone ever being responsible for it afterward. So this week's real checklist has nothing to do with CVEs: which boxes on my network were purchased and never maintained? Which dependencies execute code before I ever call them? Which outbound connections do I actually see? Three questions worth asking in a quiet week — and the answer holds up longer than any patch status.
