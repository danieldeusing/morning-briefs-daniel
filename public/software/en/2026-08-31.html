Debian Has Voted: Neither a Ban nor a Blessing for Generative AI

The general resolution this brief has been following for two weeks is counted. On 08/30, the result appeared on the official voting page: of eight competing proposals, Option 5 — "Responsible Use of Generative AI" — won out. The decisive sentence reads, verbatim: "Debian neither endorses nor prohibits the use of generative AI tools in the development, maintenance, or documentation of software, packaging, documentation, and other media published within the Debian Project." Contributions made with AI tools have to meet the same standards as everything else — quality, correctness, maintainability, legal compliance — and responsibility for them rests entirely with whoever submits them. Voting ran from the 15th through 08/28; the quorum of 48.49 votes out of roughly 1,045 eligible developers was reached by every option.

General Resolution & Condorcet: Debian has no boss who decides matters of principle — instead, every registered developer votes. Not with a single checkmark, but by ranking all the proposals against each other. The count then pits every proposal against every other one head-to-head and looks for whichever wins all its duels. Like a round-robin tournament instead of a sprint: it's not whoever has the most first-place votes who wins, but whoever would hold a majority against every single rival.

My take: This reads like a refusal to decide, but it's the only rule that could actually be enforced. You can't tell what a patch was written with just by looking at it. A ban wouldn't have stopped AI-assisted contributions — it would only have stopped honest disclosure of them, an unenforceable rule whose only measurable effect would have been silence. Debian instead shifts the question from "what was this made with" to "who's accountable for it." There was never a promise that a maintainer typed every line themselves; there was always a promise that they'd stand behind it. Expect other major projects to lift this wording wholesale — it's the first formulation that doesn't tangle itself up in controls nobody could actually enforce.

kernel.org Does the Math: 2% of Requests Are Still Human

Konstantin Ryabitsev, who runs the infrastructure behind kernel.org, wrote up on 08/29 what the AI training scrapers are doing to it. The numbers are uncomfortably specific: git.kernel.org currently gets around 6 million requests a day looking at random commits. Of the 90 CPU cores total, spread across five geographically distributed nodes, 14 to 16 are permanently busy doing nothing but rendering commits as HTML — for scrapers. And, in his own words, with "a bunch of generous assumptions": legitimate requests now account for only about 2% of traffic.

The scale comes from combinatorics: linux.git has around 1.48 million commits and roughly 922 forks — which adds up to "several BILLION valid URLs that can be scraped." As a countermeasure, Anubis, a proof-of-work bouncer, is now running; the difficulty was raised from four to five leading zeros after the bots adapted. Currently, 33% solve the computational puzzle and get through anyway.

Proof of work: Before the server responds, the browser has to solve a small, deliberately mindless computational puzzle — finding a number whose checksum starts with five zeros. For a single visitor, that's a fraction of a second. For someone trying to pull millions of pages a day, it turns into a real electricity bill. Like a turnstile that only spins slowly: it doesn't bother one person, but it bothers a tour group of a million.

My take: The bitter part is that there's a cheap way to do this. Anyone who wants the complete kernel history can just run git clone — the server packages up an archive once and is done. Scraping that same history through HTML commit pages costs it billions of renders. The scrapers pick the most expensive access method imaginable, because they're only optimizing their own costs, and the bill lands on a project that isn't selling anything. For anyone who hosts something themselves, though, the lesson isn't "install Anubis" — it's: how many valid URLs does my site actually generate? A git web interface with a diff view per commit pair, a wiki with version comparison, a calendar with no end date — combinatorial bombs that look unremarkable in the log until someone systematically walks through them.

Linux 7.3-rc1: 15,267 Commits, Second-Largest Merge Window

Update since Sun Yesterday's brief inferred the timing from the two-week rhythm — and got it right: on 08/30 at 22:52 UTC, Linus Torvalds released 7.3-rc1 and closed the merge window with it. In those two weeks, he pulled in 15,267 commits. His own assessment: "… it's big. It's not the biggest rc1 we've ever had, but it's certainly up there, at least in number of commits." Per LWN, it's the second-largest merge window in kernel history by commit count; only the 6.7 cycle came in higher.

My take: The timing is too good to leave uncommented. On the same weekend that two projects are arguing over what language models mean for open source, the world's largest codebase delivers its second-most-productive merge window. Whatever the tools are changing, the kernel community's output is growing, not collapsing. In practical terms, that means from today on: no more features, only fixes. Anyone running in production stays on 7.2.2, or their longterm branch.

Chrome Web Store: 19 Malicious Modules That Have Been Running Since Early 2024

Security firm Socket has uncovered a campaign running 19 malicious modules across 20 different extension IDs — in Chrome and Edge. The investigation suggests it's been active since early 2024. The most prominent example is an extension called "Enable Right Click & Copy," which had at least 70,000 Chrome users and 10,000 Edge installs by the time it turned malicious. The feature list reads like a catalog: theft from EVM, Solana, and Tron wallets, harvesting credentials for Coinbase, Binance, and Kraken, phishing for seed phrases for Ledger and Trezor, capturing form input, reading browser history — and fake "browser update" pop-ups following the ClickFix pattern. At the time of publication, none of the extensions were still in the Chrome Web Store; the Edge versions were still up.

ClickFix: A scam pattern where the page claims something is broken — "Your browser is out of date," "Confirm you're human" — and then shows instructions that you dutifully type or paste in yourself. You end up installing the malware yourself, believing you're fixing a problem. Like a fake repairman who tells you that to fix the damage, you first have to unlock the front door for him.

My take: For years, the extension was exactly what it promised to be — a right-click unlocker, a completely harmless tool. The break doesn't happen in the code, it happens in ownership: extensions update silently, with exactly the permissions you once granted, and the "access to all websites" permission survives every change of owner. That's the same class of problem as the hijacked npm maintainer accounts: it wasn't the package that was the problem, it was a trust relationship that changed without anyone noticing. The only effective response is uncomfortable and boring — regularly go through what's actually installed, and throw out anything whose value you can't explain in one sentence.

When the Session Is Worth More Than the Password

Anthropic is warning affected users that infostealer malware on their machines has stolen active Claude login sessions — "allowing attackers to access accounts and consume their usage." The usual suspects are named: Vidar, LummaC2, StealC, RedLine, Acreed; on affected Macs, Atomic Stealer also turned up. The chain is short: download → infection → theft of the session cookie → replay at Anthropic → someone else gets into the account. Passwords and two-factor checks never enter the picture at all. Anthropic is forcibly signing out affected accounts, removing stored payment methods, and refunding charges it identifies as unauthorized. The telltale sign for anyone affected is strikingly mundane: the usage limit fills up and empties again without you having done any work yourself.

Session cookie: After you log in, the service puts a kind of wristband on your browser: "this person has already been checked." On every click after that, the browser just shows the wristband — the password never gets asked again. Whoever steals the wristband doesn't need to talk their way past the bouncer, they just walk right by. That's exactly why changing your password afterward often does nothing at all: the old wristband still works until someone actively invalidates it.

My take: That it's an AI subscription getting hit this time is the least interesting line in the story — the mechanism applies to every account that stays logged in in a browser: git forge, cloud console, registry, payment service. Two-factor protects the login, not the hours afterward. Anyone who finds an infostealer and then only changes their passwords has accomplished precisely nothing. The correct sequence is: revoke sessions individually everywhere ("sign out all other devices"), rotate API tokens, and only then change passwords. The reason this so rarely happens is mundane — on most services, that button sits three menu levels deep.

npm Mirrors as Free Web Hosting for Phishing

OX Security has found 24 npm packages containing the same malicious HTML page — served through npm itself and through various mirrors. The page imitates a Cloudflare CAPTCHA and forwards visitors to attacker-controlled servers; exactly where is remote-controlled via api.keyval.org and can be switched at any time. The trick relies on a property of the package CDNs: "Because some of these platforms let individual files in npm packages be accessed directly in a browser, they effectively turn these developer sites into free web hosting for phishing pages." Affected platforms include UNPKG and npmmirror. The first package — china_airlines — had already been flagged by researcher inf0stache back in July 2026.

My take: The real damage here isn't technical, it's reputational. An unpkg.com URL sails through every mail filter and every firewall rule, because it looks exactly like what it's supposed to be: developer infrastructure. The sentence worth remembering sits at the end of the report and applies to anyone cleaning up a supply-chain incident — packages can keep sitting on mirrors after they've been removed from the official registry. A takedown at npm, then, isn't a takedown — it's one at just one of many locations.

Manchester Airports: 86 GB Claimed, 8.7 Million Affected

The extortion group FulcrumSec has claimed responsibility for a breach at the Manchester Airports Group and says it took roughly 86 GB: customer profiles, bookings, travel data, phone numbers, license plates, postal codes, parking times, and IP and device information. Around 200,000 of those records reportedly concern still-upcoming trips in 2026. MAG disclosed the incident on 08/27; per the company, around 8.7 million customers are affected, though for the "vast majority" only the email address was involved. MAG hasn't commented on the specific claims: "MAG is confident that we have taken effective measures to protect our customers and we have contacted all those affected." FulcrumSec has been active since 2025, doesn't encrypt anything, and threatens exclusively with publication.

Heads up Records about upcoming trips are a different category than an old address list — anyone who knows a person is flying Thursday at 06:40 and where their car is parked has a template for a highly credible phishing email. If you're flying through a UK airport soon, treat any "parking booking" or "flight change" email as bait by default.

CRA: Eleven Days Until the 24-Hour Clock

On 09/11/2026, the first hard obligation under the Cyber Resilience Act kicks in: manufacturers of products with digital elements have to report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe security incidents through the central reporting platform. The Commission published practical guidance on this on 07/27. As of today, that's eleven days away.

My take: The mistake I see everywhere is treating this as a documentation task. It isn't — it's a readiness question. A 24-hour deadline runs just as much on a Saturday at three in the morning, and it doesn't start when someone gets around to looking, it starts when the exploitation becomes known. What needs to be in place by 09/11 isn't a folder, it's a name with a phone number, a backup contact, and a pre-filled reporting form. Anyone who only sorts this out on the day of the incident will burn the deadline arguing over who's responsible.
