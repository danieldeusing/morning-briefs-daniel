Cloudflare Makes Blocking the Default

The sentence in question sits in a Cloudflare blog post from July 1 and takes effect on September 15: "For all new domains onboarding to Cloudflare, the categories of Training and Agent will be blocked by default on the pages that display ads, while Search will remain allowed by default." In plain terms: starting 09/15, every newly onboarded domain has two of three crawler classes blocked by default on ad-carrying pages, without anyone having to flip a switch.

Training, Search, Agent: Cloudflare sorts automated visitors into three intentions. Search notes where something is and later sends you readers back via a link. Training takes the text away to build a model out of it — you never see anyone again. Agent fetches the page in exactly this moment, because a single person just asked a question. Like a library: one person writes down which shelf the book is on, a second copies it for their own collection, a third reads a caller the one page they wanted to know.

What's new isn't the blocking itself — the one-click toggle against AI crawlers has existed for a while. What's new is that the default flips. And the default is, in practice, the rule, because almost nobody touches it. At a provider that terminates a substantial share of web traffic, that doesn't just shift a setting — it shifts the underlying assumption about who gets to read the web.

Still, it's worth arguing against your own alarm, because the scope is considerably narrower than the headline: it applies only to newly onboarded domains, only on pages that carry ads, and Search stays allowed. This isn't a rejection of AI — it's the targeted protection of exactly the business model that AI answers take clicks away from. According to Cloudflare, existing customers can lock in their own choice in the security settings any time before 09/15.

The genuinely lasting part is the enforcement path. robots.txt is a note on the door: a request that whoever wants to comply, complies with. A block at the edge is a lock — the request never reaches the origin server at all. That moves a question that was a courtesy convention for 30 years into the infrastructure itself.

Crawler classified→Training/Agent on ad page→Edge blocks→Origin server sees nothing
For your own domains Existing zones don't change on their own — but the next domain you onboard inherits the new default. Anyone who wants their own content to show up in AI answers has to actively choose that starting 09/15. And anyone building something that fetches pages themselves — a monitoring tool, a link preview, an agent — will increasingly fall into the Agent class and should expect a 403 instead of a page.

CRA: The Clock Starts Friday, the Weekend Right Along With It

Starting September 11, manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe security incidents in products with digital elements — so states the EU Commission's page on the CRA reporting duty. This topic has appeared here twice before; what's new is only that it now falls this week instead of further out.

Awareness→24h early warning→72h full report→Final: 14 days or 1 month

The final report is timed differently for the two tracks: 14 days after a countermeasure becomes available (exploited vulnerability), or one month (severe incident). Submissions go through the central CRA reporting platform to the national CSIRT, which forwards the report to the other responsible CSIRTs.

The practically interesting number appears in no legal text: 09/11 is a Friday. The first 24-hour deadline under the new regime therefore ends on Saturday, the first 72-hour deadline on Monday. Anyone treating the reporting duty as a compliance document instead of an on-call rotation will find that out on exactly that weekend. The honest question to answer, then, isn't "are we affected" but: who on the team is allowed to file the 24-hour report on a Saturday, and does that person know where the form lives?

Flagged honestly The Commission's page covers only the reporting duty. When the remaining CRA obligations kick in and how open-source stewards are treated differently isn't covered there — both get added in casually elsewhere, but aren't backed by this source, so they stay open here.

Chrome 153 on Tuesday: 13 Dates Become 26

On September 8, Chrome 153 ships stable, and with it the two-week cadence begins: "Starting September 2026, Chrome will move to a two-week release cycle, from the current four-week cycle." The four-week rhythm had run since 2021; the change applies to Android, iOS, Linux, macOS, and Windows simultaneously.

What doesn't change because of this: the pace at which features mature. A feature doesn't ripen faster just because the train runs more often — it just sits on the platform for less time on average. What very much does change is the number of events you have to react to. 13 milestones a year become 26. Firefox and Thunderbird moved to the same cadence the same month; together, that's roughly 52 browser release dates a year — practically one every week.

Extended Stable / ESR: Both browsers offer a slow lane — the same security fixes, but interface and feature changes arrive bundled instead of trickling in. Like the bus that only comes every two hours but always stops at the same stop: you still get there just as reliably, you just have to check less often whether the route changed. Chrome stays at eight weeks here; at Mozilla, 153 is the new ESR line.

For day-to-day work, that has two concrete consequences. First: pinned browser versions in CI — Playwright, Selenium, every visual regression baseline — now age twice as fast starting Tuesday. Second, and this is the more uncomfortable one: a support promise of the form "we support the last N Chrome versions" now covers only half the calendar time it used to. That's a good occasion to rewrite such promises from version counts to time spans — "the last six months" stays stable, "the last three versions" quietly shrinks from twelve weeks to six.

And anyone maintaining an application that can't go through a full regression cycle every 14 days shouldn't see the slow lane as a step backward, but as what it now is: the sensible target.

V8 Zero-Day: The Counterproof to the Cadence Debate

Right in the middle of the transition falls CVE-2026-85046, a type confusion in V8 rated CVSS 8.8. Google shipped the fix on September 3 in 152.0.7977.82/.83 for Windows and macOS, confirming at the same time that an exploit already existed by then. CISA added the entry to the KEV catalog on September 4.

Type confusion: The JavaScript engine keeps track of what sits in each block of memory — a number, a text string, a reference to an object. In a type confusion, an attacker manages to have that same block read back later as something else. It's as if someone filled in a form field as "phone number" and a second clerk read it as "house number, please go there": the digits are correct, but the result is a visit to an address that was never meant. In the browser, that means: foreign JavaScript gets access to memory it was never supposed to see.

This exact case is the argument against expecting the two-week cadence to solve zero-days. It doesn't — for actively exploited flaws, Chrome has long shipped outside the milestone train anyway, just as it did here on 09/03. The shorter cadence helps the unremarkable rest: the medium-severity flaw that used to wait for the next milestone.

The part usually missing from the coverage: V8 isn't only in Chrome. Every Chromium derivative follows suit, and every Electron application brings along its own engine — editors, chat clients, a good chunk of the toolbox on a developer's machine. Those don't update just because the browser restarts; they update only once the respective vendor bumps their Electron version. That's regularly the longer wait, and it shows up in no patch report.

Firefox 155.0.1: When Frosted Glass Freezes the Tab

On September 4, Firefox 155.0.1 shipped as a follow-up. The most interesting item on the list is a rendering freeze: pages that use blur and backdrop-filter together could make Firefox unusable. That's exactly the recipe for the frosted-glass effect behind modals, navigation bars, and cards — a combination baked into every other current UI kit. Anyone shipping something like that with a bug report sitting around that says "page hangs, but only in Firefox": that report is probably real, and it's been fixed since two days ago.

PostgreSQL: The Bundled Patch the Backup Script Never Hears About

Not a new find this week, but a follow-up with a long half-life: on 08/13, 18.6, 17.11, 16.15, 15.19, and 14.24 shipped. Among other things, they include CVE-2026-19385, a heap buffer overflow in pg_dump rated CVSS 8.8 — meaning the flaw sits in the client, not the server. That's why it stays open more often than average: the server gets maintained along with the distribution, while the pg_dump inside a backup container or a GitHub Actions runner runs on its own, often frozen, schedule. The patch set also fixes a SQL injection via the deparsing of an EXTRACT argument.
