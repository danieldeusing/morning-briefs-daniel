The Countdown Ran Out — In the Worse Direction

This newsletter reported on September 1 and 2 that a deadline was running in Berlin. It expired Friday around 3:35 pm, the Senate refused to pay — and the extortion group Rhysida delivered on what it had threatened. Since Sep 4, around 1.44 million files totaling 5.8 terabytes have been public on the darknet. The demand had been 30 Bitcoin, worth roughly two million euros at the time.

Two Senate departments are affected — Urban Development, Building and Housing, and Mobility, Transport, Climate Action and the Environment. The timeline is the usual one, and still sobering every time: the attackers sat undetected on the network from August 7 to 12 and spent that week copying what they wanted to take. It was noticed on August 14, made public on the 17th. So two days passed between the last copy operation and discovery; three weeks between discovery and the publication of the haul.

Double extortion: Classic ransomware encrypts data and sells the key. The second stage is copying it beforehand and threatening to publish it — at that point, even a perfect backup no longer helps. Like a burglar who doesn't just swap the lock, but photographs every file folder first: you can replace the lock, but you can't get the photos back.

The decision not to pay is the right one, and still costly. Right, because a payment deletes nothing — it buys a promise from people whose business model is breaking promises. Costly, because the price for that is exactly what's happening now.

Plaintext Passwords Aren't a Privacy Footnote — They're Access

The content is the real story. According to the extortionists, the package holds over 5,000 personnel files, payroll records, fine proceedings, employment references, and confidential documents from Bundesrat committees. Plus material that has nothing to do with routine administration: data on heating plants, fuel depots, backup power supplies, substations, waterworks, correctional facilities, and files touching defense contractors and the Bundeswehr. The group itself lists "vulnerability analyses of Berlin's drinking-water supply" among its files. CDU member of parliament Roderich Kiesewetter calls it a threat to national security; the BSI warns of an elevated threat level.

For this newsletter's focus, though, a different item is the most interesting one, because it converts into attacks immediately: plaintext passwords for databases and payment providers. Not hashes, not expiring tokens — plain strings. That's exactly why, according to the report, around 12,000 government systems have been under continuous scanning ever since.

The aftershock A leak like this doesn't hit hardest on the day it's published — it plays out over months. From 5,000 personnel files and real case numbers, you can build phishing that doesn't look like phishing: the right name, the right department, the right file number. Any vendor doing business with Berlin's administration should treat every "follow-up on your case" message over the coming weeks as potentially fabricated — and check today whether a shared password now sitting in that public data is still in use anywhere.

The Senate has set up a coordination unit that's reviewing the data and plans to notify affected people by letter or email, based on risk. "Risk-based," here, honestly means: with 5.8 terabytes, nobody currently knows the full contents.

And of All Times, the Federal Government Drops the BSI Central-Authority Plan

The report is three days old and, against the Berlin backdrop, reads like a punchline nobody planned. The coalition agreement called for expanding the BSI into a "central authority" for information and cyber security — which would have required amending the constitution. In response to an inquiry from Green politician Konstantin von Notz, the Federal Interior Ministry has now told heise online that it wants to make full use of the existing constitutional framework instead. Translation: the plan is off the table.

Why this is more than bureaucratic prose: without that amendment, the BSI may only help a state during a serious attack once that state explicitly requests administrative assistance — and to this day, the bilateral agreements needed for that don't exist with all 16 states. So the question isn't whether experts are available, but whether the paperwork is in place that lets them start. In an incident that runs over a weekend, that's the most expensive sentence in the entire process.

Incident in a state→Administrative-assistance request→bilateral agreement required→only then, BSI

My take: the federal jurisdiction question is a legitimate debate, but it's currently being settled on a calendar that won't wait for it. Germany is expanding reporting duties (see the CRA, four days) faster than it's expanding its ability to help. For operators, that means, quite practically: the call that actually helps in an emergency isn't to a federal agency, but to your own contracted service provider.

Four Days Until the 24-Hour Clock

On Sep 11, the reporting duties of the Cyber Resilience Act begin: an early warning within 24 hours, a detailed report within 72 hours, a final report 14 days after a fix becomes available. The European Commission puts it succinctly: the main obligations apply from December 11, 2027, "with reporting obligations to apply as of 11 September 2026".

What's new today is only the connection: Berlin isn't a CRA case — a state administration isn't a product manufacturer. But it's the best available test run for the question that starts mattering Friday. There, weeks passed between the first suspicion and a solid statement about the scope; the deadline gives a manufacturer one day for an initial report. That's doable, but only if the report is explicitly allowed to be incomplete — and if it's clear who files it on a Saturday.

Zod Now Compiles Itself

The most pleasant news of the weekend comes from everyday TypeScript work. Zod 4.5 introduces z.compile(): instead of recursively walking a schema again on every parse(), it's traversed once and translated into flat, loop-free JavaScript code that then runs via new Function(). If validation fails, the library falls back to the normal path, so error messages stay as precise as before.

Runtime schema validation: TypeScript only checks types at compile time — whatever arrives through the API at runtime is, at first, arbitrary JSON. A library like Zod checks the object against a description at runtime. Until now, that was like a clerk who re-reads the checklist from the top for every single application; now they write themselves a cheat sheet once for this exact form and just work through it.

The numbers in the release notes are unusually striking: an object with 20 fields goes from 301 ns to 38 ns, an array of ten objects from 377 ns to 68 ns, an array of ten strings from 241 ns to 33 ns. In the benchmark, that's 47.5 million operations per second versus 11.6 million without compilation. Anyone who wants everything compiled automatically can import zod/compile. The current version is 4.5.4 from Aug 29.

Check first The trick relies on dynamically generated code. In a browser environment with a strict Content Security Policy, and in many edge runtimes, that's exactly what isn't allowed. On the server it's a pure win — in a browser bundle or at the network edge, test before switching over.

Python 3.15: The Final Candidate Is In

3.15.0rc2 was released on Sep 1 and, per the announcement, is the final release candidate; no further ABI changes are expected. The final release is planned for Oct 1 — the download page still lists 3.14.7 from Aug 5 as the stable version. The core team spells out its request to library maintainers clearly: "We strongly encourage maintainers of third-party Python projects to prepare their projects for 3.15 during this phase."

In practice, that means the three weeks before release are the window in which a bug in a C extension is still cheap. Asahi Linux on M3 — Official, with Two Big Holes

Since Sep 6, the M3 lineup is officially in the Asahi Linux installer: M3, M3 Pro, and M3 Max in MacBooks and iMacs, excluding the Mac Studio with M3 Ultra. Working: webcam, internal microphones, USB up to the 10 Gbit limit, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and hardware-accelerated video decoding — essentially the same state M1 and M2 are at.

Two holes remain, and they're big: 3D acceleration is neither fast nor power-efficient, and there's no sleep mode because support for the display coprocessor is missing. For a laptop you close the lid on, that second point is the harder one. As a plugged-in workshop machine it's usable; as a daily driver, not yet.

Qt Retires Its AI Assistant

A story that goes against the grain: Qt is discontinuing its AI Assistant. It's being pulled from Qt Creator on Sep 30, 2026, with support ending Dec 31, 2026. So a tool vendor is retiring an assistant feature instead of expanding it — after two years in which every IDE got one.

What's interesting here isn't the AI angle, but the lifecycle question: anyone who built an assistant feature firmly into their workflow has tied themselves to a product roadmap that changes faster than the compiler underneath it. Assistance belongs in the toolbox, not in the blueprint.
