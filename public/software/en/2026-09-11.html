The clock is running — since this morning, with no dry run

The Cyber Resilience Act has been in force since December 2024, but its obligations phase in over time. Today, September 11, 2026, the part that actually affects developers goes live: the reporting duty. Anyone placing a product with digital elements on the EU market must report an actively exploited vulnerability as an early warning within 24 hours, follow up within 72 hours "where available," and deliver the final report within 14 days of a fix or workaround becoming available. For severe security incidents, it's one month from the initial notification.

Actively exploited vulnerability: Not every bug counts. This means a case where someone has demonstrably used the flaw — there are traces, victims, an ongoing attack. Like the difference between "the basement door lock is loose" and "someone came through the basement door last night." Only the second case starts the 24-hour clock.

The path there is new and centralized: the EU agency ENISA's Single Reporting Platform. It forwards the report to the responsible national body — in Germany, the BSI-CERT, the government's fire department for IT incidents. The intent is sound, and ENISA states it plainly: "report only once, rather than having to notify multiple national authorities individually."

And here's the catch that shouldn't be glossed over. ENISA's own page doesn't say "the platform has been running since X and is battle-tested" — it says: "From 11 September 2026, manufacturers are required to submit these mandatory notifications through the SRP." The mandate and the tool launch on the same day. As of late August, the platform wasn't yet publicly reachable. A reporting process whose first deadline is measured in hours, whose intake channel gets its first real load test on day one — that's the kind of setup where the first reports double as the test cases.[1][2]

Heads up The clock runs from the moment you learn of it, not from the next business day. Discover an exploit Saturday at noon, and you have until Sunday noon. That's not a footnote — it's a requirement on your on-call process, and the point where small manufacturers will first stumble.

MikroTik hits the mandatory catalog — the thread closes

On Sept. 8, this space covered six MikroTik flaws found in the middle of a live attack. On Sept. 9, the open question was why CISA hadn't yet added them to its mandatory catalog. As of yesterday, it has: CVE-2026-67277 (missing authentication for a critical function) and CVE-2026-86060 (improper neutralization of argument delimiters in a command) were added — with the justification "Both vulnerabilities were added based on evidence of active exploitation in the wild."

The second entry is the more interesting one. An argument-delimiter issue means: the software assembles a command line from user input, and someone smuggles in an extra flag via a space or a hyphen — the same class of bug as every exec() call built with string concatenation, just sitting on a router between the internet and everything else. The KEV listing doesn't change the patch status, but it changes the urgency completely: CISA doesn't add anything that's only theoretically exploitable.

React 19.3.0: view transitions land in the framework

On Sept. 9, React 19.3.0 shipped. The release's headline is <ViewTransition /> plus addTransitionType — per the release notes, "to power View Transition animations in React."

View transitions: A browser feature that crossfades between two states of a page instead of hard-switching. The browser snapshots the page before the change, snapshots it again after, and animates in between — like a cartoon where you only draw the first and last frame and the rest fills in automatically. Until now you had to steer this by hand around React's rendering; now it's a component.

The addTransitionType addition is the practical part: you tag a transition with a label ("back," "forward," "delete") and respond to it differently in CSS. Without it, every state change gets the same animation — which looks flat-out wrong on back navigation.

Second change, quiet but valuable in library code: refs on <Fragment> — "Add Refs to <Fragment /> to support composable platform behavior." Previously you needed a wrapper <div> just to reach the children's DOM nodes. Every one of those wrapper divs eventually broke a flexbox or grid layout somewhere. That's gone now.

github.com/facebook/react (release notes 19.3.0, Sept. 9)

The paragraph further down the React changelog that matters more

The same release contains a change that isn't a new API and therefore gets barely cited: "Transitions now render independently instead of being entangled into a single render, so a slow transition no longer holds up unrelated ones."

That's the fix for a behavior you'd previously chalked up to your own fault. If you triggered two startTransition updates in quick succession — say, a sidebar filter and a table reload in parallel — they got glued into a single render. The slower one set the pace for both. The symptom was always the same: a click that costs nothing locally feels sluggish because something else, somewhere, is doing expensive work. As of 19.3, they run separately. For a dashboard with several independent sections, that's the most noticeable part of the whole release.

On top of that comes Trusted Types integration. That's a browser mechanism that forbids writing raw strings into dangerous DOM sinks like innerHTML — only values that have passed through a named policy are allowed. Put differently: the browser now only accepts text for the page with a stamp of approval. If you wanted a strict Content Security Policy, React was previously the exact thing you couldn't get past.

github.com/facebook/react (release notes 19.3.0)

JDK 27 on Tuesday: G1 everywhere, smaller objects

The OpenJDK schedule sets general availability for JDK 27 on 2026-09-15; the release candidate has been up since Aug. 20. Nine JEPs are locked in, and two of them change runtime behavior without a single line of project code.

JEP 523 makes G1 the default garbage collector in all environments. Until now, the JVM fell back to SerialGC on small machines — few cores, little RAM, essentially any container. If you're running in a tightly sized pod, you may now get a different collector than before, with different pause behavior. Not a disaster, but exactly the kind of change you notice in a load test and miss in the changelog.

Compact Object Headers (JEP 534): Every Java object carries a small administrative header with it — who am I, who has me locked. That header used to be 96 or 128 bits; going forward it's 64. Like halving the label on every box in a warehouse: on one box it saves little, but across millions of boxes, suddenly a lot more fits in the same building. For heap-heavy applications, that means less memory and less GC pressure without rewriting anything. As of 27, it's the default.

The rest is continuation: Structured Concurrency in its seventh preview, Primitive Types in Patterns in its fifth, the Vector API in its twelfth incubator. What the project page does not say: whether 27 is an LTS release. It lists only dates and JEPs — support commitments come from your distributor.

openjdk.org (schedule, GA 2026-09-15, JEP list)

Post-quantum becomes standard Java kit

In my view, the most important entry on the JDK 27 list is JEP 527: Post-Quantum Hybrid Key Exchange for TLS 1.3. It gives the standard library a key exchange that combines classical elliptic-curve cryptography with a quantum-resistant scheme.

Hybrid key exchange: Two parties need to agree on a shared secret without sending it over the wire. In a hybrid scheme, they do this twice at once — once with the proven method, once with the new one — and combine both results into a single key. Like two locks on the same door: crack only one, and you're still locked out. That protects against both risks — a quantum computer breaking the old method, and a design flaw in the new one.

The pressure behind this isn't hypothetical — it's a retention question: anyone recording and storing encrypted traffic today can decrypt it later, once the machine to do so exists. Anything meant to stay confidential for longer than a decade is already exposed today. That this ships in the JDK with no third-party library as of 27 is, for Spring apps, the difference between "a project" and "a config line."

What the CRA means for a one-person shop

The honest answer: probably less than the panic posts claim, and more than nothing. The CRA targets manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market. Building and operating bespoke software for exactly one client is a different case from selling a product or shipping a device. ENISA explicitly names the platform's addressees as "manufacturers and, once applicable, open-source software stewards" — stewards, explicitly, only later.

What I'd still put on this week's agenda anyway — not as legal advice, but as an operational matter: first, decide who reports in an actual emergency and through which account — 24 hours is too short to start a registration process on the day of the incident. Second, write down a one-paragraph decision path: how do I recognize "actively exploited," and who do I call if I'm unsure. Third, review client contracts for who in their supply chain has reporting obligations — in practice, the client will demand that information from you long before any authority does.

The most likely way the CRA reaches you in 2026, in other words, isn't a letter from Brussels. It's a questionnaire from a client.

It's Friday, September eleventh. The date that's been sitting on this newsletter's timeline for weeks has finally arrived: the reporting duty from Europe's Cyber Resilience Act takes effect today.

In practice, that means: if you offer a product with digital elements on the European market and discover a vulnerability in it is being actively attacked, you have twenty-four hours to report it. A more detailed follow-up is due after seventy-two hours, and a final report after fourteen days. For severe incidents, you get a month to close things out. That's per Germany's federal cybersecurity agency, the BSI.

Reports go through a new central platform run by the EU agency ENISA, which forwards them to the appropriate national authority — in Germany, that's the BSI's emergency response team. The idea behind it makes sense: report once instead of twenty-seven times. The catch is the timing. ENISA itself says manufacturers must report through this platform starting today — meaning the platform goes live on the exact same day the requirement kicks in. As of late August it still wasn't publicly reachable. A deadline measured in hours is launching with no dry run at all.

For a small shop, this is less dramatic than it sounds, but it's not nothing either. It targets manufacturers of products, not every piece of contract work. What's still worth doing: decide ahead of time who reports in an emergency, and through which account. Twenty-four hours is too short to start a registration on the day something happens. And honestly, for most people the CRA won't arrive as a letter from Brussels — it'll show up as a questionnaire from a client.
