Yesterday this space reported that CISA had added MikroTik to its mandatory catalog. Today comes the number behind it, and that's the actual shock: Shadowserver counts over 122,500 MikroTik devices with their SSH port exposed to the open internet — German reporting puts it at over 100,000 routers under active attack. Then there's the timeline worth remembering: the attacks began Sept. 2, the vendor patch landed Sept. 3, CISA reacted Sept. 10. This isn't zero-day panic — it's the ordinary physics of edge devices: they get patched when someone patches them, and mostly nobody patches a router. The day's second deadline is more bureaucratic, but just as hard: the CISA deadline for Cisco, Citrix, and Fortinet expires today. And Adobe shipped an emergency hotfix for Magento on Sept. 7 — stores hit before that date may still be backdoored.

MikroTrick: the number behind the catalog entry

Yesterday the CISA entry was the news; today it's the scope. Shadowserver counts over 122,500 MikroTik devices whose SSH service sits unprotected and reachable on the open internet; German reporting puts it at over 100,000 routers exposed to active attacks. Those are two different numbers from two sources — one measures reachability, the other attack pressure — and together they add up to the realistic picture: almost everything reachable is also getting touched.

Auth bypass + argument injection: Two bugs that are annoying alone and fatal together. The first lets you past the door without showing a key. The second lets someone secretly tack extra instructions onto an otherwise harmless command — like handing a messenger a note that says "bring bread" and someone adds, on the same line, "…and open the garden gate." Combined, a stranger takes over the device completely with no login at all.
Sept. 2 attacks begin→Sept. 3 MikroTik patches→Sept. 10 CISA catalog

The sequence is the interesting part. The vendor reacted fast — one day after exploitation began is remarkable for a router OS. Yet a week later the affected count is still six figures, because RouterOS update distribution has no automatism built in: no forced auto-update, no distro behind it, no admin updating the fleet on a Friday. If you run one, spend less time thinking about the patch and more about why SSH was reachable from the internet in the first place.

Heads up There's a genuine mix-up in the CVE number — and it's on CISA's own end: the Sept. 10 catalog entry lists CVE-2026-67277, but CISA's own description for that entry doesn't match the MikroTrick chain (auth bypass + argument injection) — it matches a separate bug in the btest service (kernel memory disclosure/DoS). The actual auth-bypass flaw — confirmed by discoverer CERT Polska and by MikroTik itself — is CVE-2026-67276, combined with CVE-2026-86060 for full takeover.

The deadline expires today — three devices at the network edge

For everything CISA added on Sept. 9, today is the deadline. The deadline formally binds only US federal agencies, but it's the best publicly available urgency indicator there is: it only gets set when a flaw is demonstrably being exploited.

KEV catalog: The list of flaws a US agency can prove are actually being attacked out in the wild — not just theoretically dangerous. Like the difference between "this door has a cheap lock" and "someone broke through this door last week." Once something's on it, prioritizing stops and scheduling starts.

Cisco Secure Firewall Management Center — CVE-2026-20079

Authentication bypass in the web interface, CVSS 10.0. The top score only happens when a flaw needs no preconditions and leads to full remote control. Painful, because the FMC is the management layer above the firewalls: whoever has it has the rule sets.

Citrix NetScaler ADC/Gateway — CVE-2026-19490

Auth bypass, CVSS 9.3, affecting devices in AAA or Gateway configuration — exactly the role NetScaler plays as a company's VPN front door. Same bug class as Cisco, same week.

Fortinet FortiOS — CVE-2025-25249

Heap overflow with code execution, CVSS 7.3. The lowest score of the three — and still on the catalog, which tells you: the score describes the technical bar, not the odds it happens to you.

Magento: hotfix since Sept. 7, compromise window stays open

The "Stylesmuggler" zero-day is still running, but the status has changed. Sansec dates the start of the campaign to Sept. 4; the attack smuggles PHP code through crafted style properties that get rendered in payment-failure emails. Affected are all supported Adobe Commerce and Magento Open Source versions 2.4.4–2.4.9 (Adobe's own bulletin gives both product lines the same floor). Adobe shipped an emergency hotfix on Sept. 7 at 20:20 UTC (APSB26-146, CVE-2026-75650, CVSS 10.0) as a Composer patch (VULN-39341, per Sansec, the researchers who found the flaw). For shop operators, that means: patch immediately — and anyone exposed between Sept. 4 and Sept. 7 should additionally scan for a backdoor already planted, since the patch closes the hole but doesn't undo an existing compromise.

CRA, day two: what the fine-print numbers mean

The Article 14 reporting duty has been in force since yesterday. The part that got lost yesterday and matters more than the clock today: the reach and the price. Anyone who violates the reporting duties risks a fine of up to €15 million or 2.5% of the prior year's global annual revenue — whichever figure is higher. That second clause is the real lever: for a corporation, the percentage is always the bigger number; for a small software shop, it's always the flat amount. There's no size at which the rule goes toothless.

And the duty doesn't stop at the line between old and new: it applies explicitly to products placed on the EU market before the full mandate took effect too — not just to new launches and ongoing development projects. For anyone who sold software with networked elements into the EU and now just maintains it, that's the uncomfortable clause: the old thing that's been running quietly for four years falls under the same clock.

The chain stays: 24-hour early warning, 72-hour technical follow-up, 14-day final report for actively exploited flaws (one month for severe incidents). Concretely: whatever went out as an early warning yesterday needs its follow-up Monday and its final report on Sept. 25.

Starlette: the catalog entry with home-field advantage

Of the seven entries CISA added on Sept. 2, there's one this newsletter shouldn't skip past: CVE-2026-48710, HTTP request/response smuggling in Starlette — the ASGI foundation FastAPI sits on. CISA doesn't add anything without evidence of exploitation. As of today, it's still the week's entry with the most direct tie to a Python backend stack.

HTTP request smuggling: Two programs read the same request and disagree about where it ends — the reverse proxy out front sees one request, the application server behind it sees two. Into that gap, the attacker smuggles a second, invisible request. Like handing in a slip at the counter with a second order written on the back that the kitchen fulfills, even though it never crossed the counter. Result: other users' requests can be poisoned or redirected.

From the same batch: Kestra and JFrog

Also added on Sept. 2: CVE-2026-49869, OS command injection in Kestra OSS (workflow orchestration), and CVE-2026-82329, improper authentication in JFrog Artifactory. Two systems that in typical deployments sit between the repository and production — compromise them, and you don't even need to attack the application itself anymore.

It's Saturday, September twelfth. Today has two faces: a number that was still missing yesterday, and a deadline that runs out today.

Let's start with the number. Yesterday this space reported that America's cybersecurity agency had added two MikroTik flaws to its mandatory catalog. Today the measurement of scale caught up. The nonprofit Shadowserver counts more than one hundred twenty-two thousand five hundred MikroTik devices with their SSH access sitting open on the internet. German reporting puts it at more than one hundred thousand routers already under active attack. Those are two different measurements, and together they paint an uncomfortably clear picture: almost everything that's reachable is also getting hit.

The timeline is worth remembering. The attacks began September second. MikroTik published its patch on September third, a single day later. The agency added the flaws to its catalog on the tenth. The vendor moved fast, and yet a week on, the affected count is still six figures. That's not carelessness on any one person's part — it's how the hardware works. Router operating systems don't update themselves, there's no distribution behind them, and usually nobody feels responsible for them either. So the real question isn't whether a patch exists, it's why remote management was reachable from the open internet at all.

A quick accuracy note. There's a genuine mix-up in the CVE number, and it's on the agency's own end: its catalog entry six-seven-two-seven-seven describes a different, unrelated bug. The actual auth-bypass flaw is six-seven-two-seven-six, confirmed by discoverer CERT Polska and by MikroTik itself.

The day's second story is a clock running out. For three devices at the network edge, the government-set patch deadline expires today: Cisco's firewall management platform with the maximum score of ten point zero, Citrix NetScaler at nine point three, and Fortinet at seven point three. Formally, that only binds US federal agencies, but it's the best public urgency signal available — this catalog only takes flaws that are demonstrably under attack.

Magento now has a hotfix. That campaign has been running since September fourth, and Adobe shipped an emergency patch on September seventh for every affected version. Anyone running a shop should patch immediately and also check whether a backdoor was already planted before the patch.

On the regulatory side, today is day two under Europe's new reporting duty. The fine print now matters more than the clock. Violating the reporting duty can cost up to fifteen million euros or two point five percent of global annual revenue, whichever is higher. And the duty explicitly covers products already on the market too, not just new launches. So if you're only maintaining an old, networked product at this point, you're still under the same clock.

From the toolbox, briefly: Angular updated three branches at once on September ninth, current stable is twenty-two point one point six. Microsoft released the first release candidate of dot-net eleven with a production license, with the final build coming in November. In the Spring world, nothing new has shipped since August twentieth besides the developer tools. And LibreOffice twenty-six point eight collected more than a million downloads in its first week, a project record.
