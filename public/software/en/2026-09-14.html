The PaperCut wave only ran here as a footnote last week — now the analysis behind it is out, and it's the real headline. From an empty working environment to the first remote control of a real victim server took just under four hours. At the peak, eleven organizations fell in 26 seconds. In the end: 440 instances across 395 organizations in 48 countries. But the remarkable part isn't the number — it's what the number doesn't include: at only twelve of those 395 organizations did the attacker reach domain admin. Breadth and speed exploded; depth didn't. That's the actual news, and it shifts the priority between "patch faster" and "detect better."

PaperCut: Four Hours From Zero to Remote Control

Update since yesterday Until now, the PaperCut wave only ran here as a raw number. Now GreyNoise's reconstruction is out, and it changes the read. Two flaws in PaperCut MF/NG were exploited: CVE-2026-81578 bypasses login, CVE-2026-82078 then runs arbitrary code on the server. On its own, that's nothing special — print-server software with weak authentication has burned people before. What's new is how the chain was carried out: the attacker, according to GreyNoise most likely Russian-speaking, wasn't at the keyboard from Aug. 31 onward — the work was handed off to a fleet of automated agents.

The timeline is the real argument. From a completely empty working environment to the first working remote code execution against a real victim took just under four hours; the first domain admin rights fell two hours after that. Once the machine was running, breadth came almost for free: in one stretch, eleven organizations were compromised in 26 seconds. In total, GreyNoise counts at least 440 affected PaperCut instances across 395 organizations in 48 countries.

Flaw disclosed → Exploit in < 4h → 440 instances → Domain admin: 12
Domain admin: On a Windows corporate network, every machine and account hangs off one central directory. Whoever is domain admin there can do anything, anywhere. Think of an office building: breaking in through a poorly secured basement window only gets you into the basement. The master key from the janitor's office opens every door in the building. That difference decides whether an incident is an annoyance or a catastrophe.

Downstream, off-the-shelf tools did the work: Mimikatz, SharpHound, Certipy, Rubeus, Impacket — all freely available, all known for years, all easy to detect. The automation was in the orchestration, not the weapons. How the agent layer was built technically (Codex harness, DeepSeek model) is covered today in the ai-dev brief; here, what matters is the effect on the defender's side.

The Second Case in the Same Week

PaperCut would be an isolated case, if not for a second, entirely independent account of the same pattern on Sept. 8. Google's Threat Intelligence Group published a Mandiant-investigated incident from Q2 2026 in its "AI Threat Tracker": an attacker on compromised cloud infrastructure deployed a self-directing agent chain that ran vulnerability scans and IP rotation on its own. Result, per the report: a credential-harvesting campaign planned, built, and executed in under six hours — thousands of stolen credentials compromised.

Two vendors, two investigations, two victim groups, the same picture. For context, that matters more than any single number: this is not an outlier a vendor is inflating for marketing.

The Uncomfortable Math

My read, and I'll defend it against the reflex: the right response to PaperCut is not "patch even faster." Four hours from flaw to breach is a window no approval process on earth wins — not for software running on thousands of servers across government agencies and mid-size companies. Anyone taking "tighten emergency patching" as the lesson from this campaign is optimizing a spot that's already lost.

The other number is more interesting. At 12 of 395 organizations, the attacker made it to domain admin — just under three percent. At the remaining roughly 383, the attacker was inside and got no further. Automation industrialized the entry point, but not the breakout from initial access. And that second step is exactly the one that's loud, slow, and runs on known tools — Mimikatz and SharpHound have been throwing off signals for years.

In practice that means: segmentation, least-privilege service accounts, and working detection inside the internal network now pay off more than any further shortening of the patch window. That's unglamorous, costs months instead of one night shift — and it's the lever that actually decided something in this incident.

Ivanti: Ten CVEs Across Three Products

On Sept. 8, Ivanti closed ten vulnerabilities across Neurons for ITSM, EPMM, and Sentry. Six of them allow arbitrary code execution in various configurations; the top group, CVE-2026-12647, CVE-2026-12645, CVE-2026-12646, and CVE-2026-12650, sits at CVSS 9.9, with two more (CVE-2026-12744, CVE-2026-12745) at 9.8.

The catch is in the rollout: part of the fixes are included in versions 2025.2 through 2026.1, the rest doesn't arrive until 2026.2 on Sept. 21. For operators, that means a week with half the door closed. At least there's no confirmed exploitation in the wild so far — unlike Ivanti flaws in recent years. That's the good news, and with this vendor it historically has a short shelf life.

Check Point: Two Nines in the VPN Edge

On Sept. 9, Check Point patched two flaws at CVSS 9.8 each, both in the certificate handling of its VPN gateways. CVE-2026-85102 affects certificate validation and allows unauthenticated code execution; CVE-2026-85103 is a heap overflow when decoding ASN.1 certificates. Affected are Security Gateway, Management Server, and Spark across the R81.20, R82, and R82.10 branches; fixes ship via Live Patch or Jumbo Hotfix.

The location is the uncomfortable part: a VPN gateway must by definition be reachable from the open internet, and certificate validation happens before any login. So there's no intermediate step where an attacker would first need valid credentials.

SAP Patch Day: A Perfect Ten — and an npm Package

SAP published 19 new security notes plus one update on Sept. 8. Top of the list is CVE-2026-44756 at CVSS 10.0 — a buffer overflow in the Extended Passport Protocol, the internal tracing mechanism SAP components use to pass requests across system boundaries.

More interesting for developers is number two. CVE-2026-76969, CVSS 9.4, sits in @sap/cds-mtxs — an open-source npm library from the SAP Cloud Application Programming Model that can expose credentials. Affected are versions 1.18.3, 2.7.6, 3.9.6, and 4.0.2. This isn't a case for the basis team, but for package.json: the flaw travels through the dependency tree into applications that want nothing to do with an "SAP landscape" at all.

CRA Reporting Duty: Who Counts as the Manufacturer of an npm Package?

Since Sept. 11, 2026, Article 14 of the EU Cyber Resilience Act is in force. Manufacturers "of products with digital elements" must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe security incidents — a 24-hour early warning, a 72-hour full report, a 14-day final report, all through the EU agency ENISA's Single Reporting Platform.

Cyber Resilience Act: An EU law that demands the same of software and connected devices that product-safety rules demand of kitchen appliances: the manufacturer is liable for building the thing safely, and must report it when it catches fire. What's new above all is the reporting part — before, it was largely left up to each company whether and when to disclose an incident publicly.

And this is exactly where the SAP case next door becomes the teaching example. @sap/cds-mtxs is open source, sits in the npm registry, is distributed free of charge — and comes from a European manufacturer that builds commercial products on top of it. The regulation explicitly exempts pure hobby projects, but the line doesn't run along "open source yes/no" — it runs along commercial activity. Which libraries fall under this duty won't be decided in Brussels, but in the first reports filed over the coming months. Anyone publishing packages in the EU and making money from it shouldn't put off that question.

Berlin: 5.8 Terabytes on the Dark Web

The attack on Berlin's government has moved out of the acute phase and into the phase that's worse for those affected. On Sept. 4, the attackers published roughly 1.44 million files — a package of about 5.8 TB — on the dark web, after the city-state rejected an extortion demand. The data had already been exfiltrated from two Senate departments between Aug. 7 and 12.

The content is the problem: personnel files, disciplinary records, financial documents, IBANs, passwords. The city-state has set up its own coordination unit to contact those affected. The decision not to pay is still the right one — it's just that the follow-on cost is now borne by every individual person in those files, not by the budget.

Broadcom Shuts Off the VDDK

Since Aug. 25, 2026, the public download of VMware's Virtual Disk Development Kit has been closed — without warning; the known URLs simply stopped responding. It wasn't reported until Sept. 10.

VDDK: A programming interface that lets third-party software read a VMware machine's virtual disks directly, without touching the VM itself. The shop analogy: the standardized port every diagnostic computer plugs into. Remove the port, and only the manufacturer itself can look under the hood.

That's exactly what Nutanix Move and the Red Hat toolkit around virt-v2v depend on — the tools operators use to migrate away from VMware. Backup partners' access continues unaffected. VMware argues that migrating to a competing hypervisor was never a licensed use in the first place. That may be true; the timing remains notable. Anyone with a VMware migration planned should first check whether their tool needs a locally stored VDDK copy.

theregister.com

GitHub: 88 Minutes of Hiccups

On Sept. 13 between 09:16 and 10:44 UTC (06:16–07:44 in Ribeirão Claro), API requests, Issues, Pages, Pull Requests, and Actions were degraded at GitHub. The status report names "increased latency in database replication" as the cause, which led to errors in the authorization endpoints.

Not a security matter, but a reminder that "Actions won't run" and "login is stuck" can share the same root at GitHub. Anyone who shipped a release Sunday morning and wondered why workflows were broken — that was it.

Browsers Move to a Two-Week Cadence

Chrome and Firefox are both switching to a two-week stable cadence starting September 2026. Chrome leads with version 153, per ghacks; the official Chrome Releases blog lists the corresponding desktop stable-channel update dated Sept. 8, 2026. Firefox shipped 155.0.1 on Sept. 4, with the ESR branches following on Sept. 1.

For application developers, that's mostly good: security fixes arrive faster, and the window where a feature is "already in Chrome, not yet in practice" gets shorter. For anyone testing against a browser matrix, it doubles the pace instead. A good moment to shift a target-version definition from "current version" to "last N weeks."

It's Monday, September fourteenth. The news day itself is quiet — no major releases, no new emergency patches. But the analysis of an attack wave that only crossed this desk as a raw number last week is out now, and it's the real story of this briefing.
