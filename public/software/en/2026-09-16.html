Cisco Secure Email Gateway: One Email Is Enough, and It Runs as Root

CVE-2026-76461, CVSS 9.8, vector AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H — that is, over the network, with no privileges, with no human interaction, and full impact on confidentiality, integrity, and availability. The bug sits in the email parsing of Cisco's AsyncOS. A message containing suitably crafted SQL statements passes through the device and ends up as command execution with root privileges on it. Secure Email Gateways are affected both as physical and virtual appliances, and according to the advisory this is independent of device configuration — not just tied to one particular setting. Affected versions: 15.5 and earlier, 16.0 and 16.5 prior to the fixed releases.

Secure Email Gateway: The bouncer standing in front of the mail server. Every message from outside passes through this device first, which unpacks attachments, checks links, and throws out spam. To do that, it has to open and take apart every letter itself before passing it along. A bug in that unpacking step is therefore a bug that any sender in the world is allowed to trigger.

That's exactly what makes this case structurally worse than the usual edge-device flaw. With a VPN concentrator or a management interface, the interim fix is always the same: restrict access to trusted networks, then patch at leisure. That release valve doesn't exist here — the untrusted network is the input. Cisco puts it just as plainly: "There are no workarounds that address this vulnerability." Either the device has the patch, or it stands wide open.

The timeline here is the shortest seen all week. Cisco published the advisory on 2026-09-14 and confirmed in it that PSIRT learned of active exploitation in September 2026 — that makes this a zero-day, not a precautionary fix. CISA added the flaw to the KEV catalog the same Monday and gave US federal agencies a deadline of 2026-09-17 — tomorrow. Three days. GitLab followed the same pattern last week: patch on 2026-09-10, catalog on 2026-09-11, deadline on 2026-09-14.

There's no such device sitting in Daniel's setup, and yet this isn't a topic to skip. The last four maximum-severity cases in this brief have all involved a box at the edge: self-hosted GitLab, Cisco FMC, Cisco Nexus, now Cisco ESA. For a small operation, the security boundary isn't your own code — it's whatever accepts a connection from outside: the reverse proxy, the SSH port on the VPS, and the email provider. And the email provider may well be running exactly this kind of device.

Why the Patch Queue Has Doubled — and Where It's Jammed Now

Brian Krebs crunched the numbers for September's Patch Tuesday: 974 flaws this month, 113 of them rated "critical" by Microsoft. And then the number that puts the month in context: "September's Patch Tuesday brings this year's total to more than 2,600" — more than double the previous record year, 2020, at 1,245. Nine months in, and two annual records have already been blown past.

Heads up The total for 2026 isn't even settled: Krebs writes "more than 2,600," InfoQ put it at "roughly 2,750" on 2026-09-15. That's the same counting problem one level up that already showed up here on 2026-09-11 for the monthly figure (964 / 966 / 974 depending on the outlet) — depending on whether only Patch Tuesday counts, or all products across the whole month. Both numbers point to the same order of magnitude; neither works as an exact figure.

The interesting part isn't the record, it's the explanation. Microsoft explicitly attributes the acceleration to AI-assisted vulnerability hunting. And that's not a Microsoft quirk: Krebs lists Adobe, Cisco, Google, Mozilla, and Oracle as citing the same reason for their own rising patch cadence. When six major vendors independently give the same explanation, that's no longer a marketing footnote — it's a shift in the process itself.

AI-Assisted Vulnerability Hunting: Until now there were two ways to find bugs — a human reads the code, or a fuzzer blindly throws millions of random inputs at a program and watches for when it breaks. The second way is like a burglar trying every handle in the house: it works, but it takes forever. A model that actually reads the code instead sets out with the floor plan in hand. It tries fewer things, but finds something more often.

InfoQ draws the conclusion from the operations side, and it's the real story: finding has scaled, deploying hasn't. Between a discovery and a live fix there's still manual work — figuring out whether you're affected, testing, assessing what else the patch touches, rolling it out. Those steps cost exactly as much as they did in 2020; it's just that twice as many cases are arriving now. "Hundreds of updates landing at once" isn't a capacity problem you solve with overtime.

My take: at roughly a thousand disclosures a month, "work through them by severity" isn't a strategy anymore, it's a wish. The only list that still carries real signal is the KEV catalog — and this week it delivered exactly two entries with a three-day deadline, GitLab and Cisco ESA. The uncomfortable flip side: the same tools sit on both sides of the table. Whoever's attacking doesn't have to take the slow step.

Android September: 180 Flaws, Two Patch Levels, One Labeling Problem

Google published Android's September updates on 2026-09-09: 180 vulnerabilities, split across two patch levels — 95 at the 2026-09-01 level, another 85 at the 2026-09-05 level. The most severe is CVE-2026-28662, a memory corruption flaw in the Wi-Fi part of the System component that can lead to remote code execution, with no extra privileges and no action required from the user. The breakdown: System 56 entries (23 critical), Framework 37 (3 critical), Android Runtime 1, kernel and other components 85. The report notes no active exploitation for any of these entries.

Android Patch Level: The phone's settings show a date like "September 1, 2026." But there are two of those every month: the first covers the Android core, the second adds fixes to drivers and chip firmware from individual manufacturers. Like two shots in a series, where the second one looks optional. A device maker is allowed to ship only the first and still, entirely correctly, label it "September update" — the label is accurate, the coverage is something else.

The reason this is here isn't the individual bug — it's the second data point for the story one column over. 180 at Android, 974 at Microsoft, 230 fixes in Chrome 153 last week. Three vendors, one month, nearly 1,400 entries. Anyone still running "read every advisory" as a process has already lost that process and just hasn't noticed yet.

Shai-Hulud: 111 Days of Silence Isn't a Pause, It's a Design

This number was only a side note here yesterday; it deserves more. Aikido counted 319 npm package versions carrying the file hash of the Shai-Hulud payload — and all 319 were first seen on the same day, 2026-05-19. After that: nothing. Between 2026-05-20 and 2026-09-06, not a single new hit on that hash. Then on 2026-09-07, four new package versions, all within one hour, all from the same npm account: feishu-docx-mcp@0.3.2, bmc-i18n-extract-cli@1.1.1, blueai-cli@0.7.0, bmc-translate-utils@1.1.1. Aikido calls this the longest dormancy period seen from this payload so far.

What matters here isn't the return, it's what the pause defeats. A dependency scan is a point-in-time snapshot. Anyone who checked in June or July got a clean result — not because things were clean, but because nothing new was published in those weeks. The hash itself had been known since May. So neither detection nor the signature was missing: known, byte-for-byte identical malicious code was republished four months later and was installable all over again.

npm has tightened things up since the May incident — mandatory two-factor authentication for local publishing, token lifetime capped at seven days, and staged publishing, generally available since npm CLI 11.15.0 in May 2026.

Staged Publishing: Uploading gets split into two steps — upload, then release. Like a package that first sits in a locker and needs a second key before it goes out. A stolen token alone is no longer enough — a human has to approve the second step.

For a one-person setup, the advice stays unglamorous, but it's the only kind that survives a sleeper: commit the lockfile, use npm ci instead of npm install in CI, disable install scripts there, and keep a dependency update as its own commit you can roll back. None of this is new. The 111 days are just the strongest argument yet against relying on "we'll notice when it happens."

Postgres 19 Hasn't Shipped — and That's Exactly the Story

This is usually about the time the new Postgres major version lands, and plenty of blog posts are already queued up for it. The current state: Beta 3 was the latest 19 build as of mid-August, no release candidate has been announced, and the project still names a September-to-October 2026 window as the target for the final release. Anyone planning an upgrade window: not yet. The signal worth waiting for is the first RC, not the next beta.

Brazil: 49,291 Applications for 1,823 Positions at the State IT Agency

Dataprev — the federally owned data center that, among other things, runs the IT for Brazil's social security system — has published the preliminary applicant numbers for its selection process: 49,291 registrations for 1,823 advertised positions, roughly 27 applicants per slot on average. The breakdown by profile and location came on 2026-09-15 from Fundação Getúlio Vargas, which runs the process. Software development is among the most sought-after profiles: João Pessoa counts 4,259 applications for 100 positions, Florianópolis 2,497 for 100, Brasília 1,098 for 17 — about 65 per slot there. The written exam is scheduled for 2026-10-11.

Two readings, both relevant. First, the labor market: a permanent civil-service post with starting salaries up to around R$10,600 draws 27 applicants per position. That's not a statement about Dataprev, it's a statement about the perceived instability in Brazil's private tech sector — people are trading growth speed for predictability.

Second, and this is the more interesting part: the toughest competition isn't on the Rio–São Paulo axis. João Pessoa and Florianópolis top the list. Public-sector IT in Brazil is currently spreading out deliberately across the country, and for anyone based inland, that shifts the answer to what a "serious tech employer within reach" even means.

What's Worth Twenty Minutes Today

A day with exactly one hard deadline, and it involves a device nobody here runs. All the more reason to keep the list short and honest:

Sort by KEV, not CVSS → Lockfile + npm ci → Check the patch level on your phone

One: flip the patch order for once — whatever's in the KEV catalog first, everything else after. At a thousand disclosures a month, severity score is only a measure of the damage if an attack succeeds, not of the likelihood. Two: in your own projects, check whether CI actually runs npm ci against a committed lockfile and has install scripts disabled. That's the one move that protects against a payload that sleeps for four months. Three: on an Android phone, don't just check whether it says "September" — check which of the two September levels it's on. The 85 entries in the second level are simply missing on plenty of devices.

What explicitly doesn't belong on the list: JDK 27 in production, a Postgres 19 upgrade plan, or any reaction to the Cisco deadline. All three are either too early today or don't apply at all. A to-do list made up of things that don't affect you gets ignored as a whole — and then point two gets missed along with it.
