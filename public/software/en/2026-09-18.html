The System That Checks Logins Doesn't Need One Itself

Cisco's Identity Services Engine is, in many corporate networks, the instance that decides which device gets in at all. As of Wednesday, it's confirmed you can skip that decision entirely: no login, over the network, with command execution as root on the exact box that checks the badges. No workarounds, active exploitation, a federal deadline tomorrow. That puts three US federal deadlines on three consecutive days — yesterday Cisco's mail gateway, today the Chrome zero-day, tomorrow the ISE. And behind that sits the more interesting observation: this week's fourth maximum-severity flaw, a memory corruption bug in the shared SAP kernel, has by far the largest reach — and no deadline, because nobody's exploiting it yet. The industry's patch order is now dictated by attacker activity, not by damage potential. That's pragmatic. It's also why the SAP flaw will still be open in January.

Cisco ISE: The Bouncer Never Asks Itself for ID

CVE-2026-76460, CVSS 10.0 — the maximum score, which only happens when every single metric sits at its worst: reachable over the network, exploitable with no prior knowledge, no privileges required, no human interaction, and impact reaching beyond the boundary of the affected component. In CISA's phrasing, the bug is called "Incorrect Use of Privileged APIs": an unauthenticated attacker reaches privileged functions through the management API — the ise-kong-gateway that ISE runs its administrative calls through — without ever touching the web interface, and therefore without touching the login at all. The result, according to the advisory, is command execution with root privileges.

Identity Services Engine (ISE): The bouncer standing at the corporate network's door. Before a laptop, a phone, or a printer even gets an address, the switch or the Wi-Fi checks in with the ISE: do I know this device, is it allowed in, and which network segment does it go to? To do that, the ISE holds the IDs, the rules, and the protocols — and because it has to be able to, it gets a say everywhere. Whoever takes it over doesn't need to break down any more doors. They just issue themselves the ID.

Affected are ISE and the Passive Identity Connector (ISE-PIC) across all releases from 3.0 to 3.5. The first fixed release, depending on the line, is 3.1 Patch 12, 3.2 Patch 11, 3.3 Patch 12, 3.4 Patch 7, or 3.5 Patch 4. There's no patch for 3.0 — that line has reached end of life, so migration is the only option. And, just like the mail gateway on Monday: "there are no workarounds." No access filter, no feature you can turn off. Either the patch is on, or the device is wide open.

Cisco confirms active exploitation; CISA added the flaw to the KEV catalog on 2026-09-16 and gave US federal agencies a deadline of 2026-09-19 — 72 hours, ending on a Saturday. That's not an exception to a still-standing two-week rule — there isn't one anymore. The new Directive BOD 26-04 replaced BOD 22-01 in June and sets 3-, 14-, or 60-day deadlines based on risk (exposure, KEV status, exploit potential, impact). 72 hours is the top risk tier here — the agency only grants it for maximum-risk cases.

For a one-person shop in Paraná, there's no such device in the rack. Even so, this is the fifth maximum-severity flaw in a row that doesn't sit in your own code but in the box at the network edge — GitLab, Cisco FMC, Cisco Nexus, Cisco ESA, now Cisco ISE. A small operation's attack surface isn't its repository, it's the list of services that accept a connection from outside. That belongs on a sheet of paper, not in someone's memory.

Four Perfect Tens, Three Deadlines — and the One With the Biggest Reach Has None

Within nine days, four vulnerabilities have piled up with a CVSS of 10.0 or just below it, all four exploitable without authentication. Laid side by side, they tell a bigger story than any one of them alone:

CVE-2026-85046Chrome / V88.8today

Three of them are in the KEV catalog because they're demonstrably being exploited — and that's what gets them a deadline. The fourth, OVERPASS (CVE-2026-44756), is a memory corruption flaw in the Extended Passport processing of the shared SAP kernel. Shared means the same code runs under S/4HANA, ERP/ECC, NetWeaver, Web Dispatcher, BW/4HANA, Enterprise Portal, PI/PO, and Solution Manager. Because that processing runs before role and authorization checks, an attacker doesn't need an account. Its reach is the largest of all four — SAP sits inside the accounting systems of half of Europe. And yet there's no catalog entry, and therefore no deadline.

The point A CVSS score describes how bad it is if it happens. A KEV entry describes whether it's happening right now. Both together would give you a real priority order. In practice, the industry now prioritizes only by the second value — understandable given the volume, but it means a pre-auth memory corruption bug in the ERP kernel slides through until somebody uses it. Then it moves fast — and in SAP landscapes, patch windows for production systems get measured in quarters, not days.

The second flaw from SAP's patch day fits the pattern: CVE-2026-58240, CVSS 9.8, a missing authentication check in the NetWeaver Message Server — the component application servers use to find each other. An internal-plumbing service that should never be reachable from an outside network, and astonishingly often is.

Swift 6.4: One Build System for All Three Operating Systems

Swift 6.4 landed on 2026-09-15, and the most important line in the announcement is a housekeeping note: "Swift Build is now the default in Swift Package Manager, so your projects build the same way on Linux, macOS, and Windows." That erases the split Swift outside Apple devices has suffered under for years — one build machinery for Xcode, a different one for everything else, with the usual differences in flags, ordering, and error messages.

Alongside that, three things that matter server-side. Subprocess hits 1.0, giving Swift a stable, cross-platform way to launch other programs and talk to them — exactly what tooling code constantly needs, and until now had to be rewritten per platform. Swift's Span now talks directly to C++20's std::span, which bridges to existing C++ libraries without a copy. And in the browser, Swift running over WebAssembly and JavaScriptKit is, according to the release notes, up to 40 times faster than before.

Swift isn't a topic for Daniel's stack — but the pattern is familiar from Java, .NET, and Go: a language only becomes a server language once its Linux build stops being the second-class option. Whether this step actually holds won't show up in the release, but in whether, a year from now, Docker images for Swift services are simply a given.

GitHub, September 13: When the Token Service Coughs, the Whole CI Coughs

A week ago last Sunday, starting around 09:16 UTC, GitHub reported degraded availability across roughly 28 services — API, Issues, Pull Requests, Pages, Actions, Codespaces, Git LFS. The status updates name a delay in database replication as the cause. The two numbers that make the outage concrete: at peak, 8.8% of requests generating an installation access token for a GitHub App failed, and roughly 4% of token issuance for Actions workflows was affected.

Sourcing note The percentages come from GitHub's own incident summary, which today can be read via two independent status aggregators — the original status page returned no parseable text when queried. So double-checked, but not pulled directly from the primary source. Treat the numbers as reliable in order of magnitude, not down to the decimal.

What's interesting isn't the outage itself but where it breaks. An installation token is the short-lived password a GitHub App or an Actions run uses to authenticate for a few minutes. When issuing it fails 8.8% of the time, it's not one service that goes down — every automation hooked into GitHub anywhere gets a sporadic "no," and that shows up as a red build, an aborted deploy, or a wrong status check in projects that are otherwise completely healthy. Anyone who got annoyed at a second red run that hour: it wasn't your code.

Omarchy: $18.5 Million, Partly in AI Credits, and a Backlash

Omarchy is David Heinemeier Hansson's Arch-based Linux distribution — Hyprland as the window manager, Quickshell as the shell, everything preconfigured under the "omakase" principle: the chef picks, the guest goes along. The Omacom Foundation set up to fund it now puts its pledges and donations at roughly $18.5 million, explicitly including multi-year commitments and AI tokens. DigitalOcean joined as founding corporate patron with $1 million a year for three years; the seed funding was eight private contributions of $1 million each, including from Michael Dell, Jack Dorsey, Patrick Collison, and Tobi Lütke.

The part that says the most from a software standpoint is the currency. Some of the pledges are token credits from Meta Superintelligence Labs, Anthropic, OpenAI, and Fireworks — inference allowances instead of money. A distribution that calls itself "agentic," and whose funding partly consists of model credits from four providers, has built a dependency into its foundation that no Linux project had before: it doesn't just rely on money, but on ongoing service access from companies that can reprice that access at any time.

In parallel, an unusually loud backlash is running through the free-software scene, driven mostly by Hansson's political statements: Matthew Garrett calls the project "fundamentally incompatible with the goals of free software," and a "Stop Omarchy" campaign describes it as "a political movement disguised as a Linux distro." Beyond the politics, that's also a governance question — a distribution project whose pull depends on one public individual carries exactly that person's risk along with it. Noted, not joined.

CRA Reporting Duty, Week Two: The Clock Is Running, the Sanctions Aren't

Since 2026-09-11, the reporting obligations of the EU Cyber Resilience Act have been in force: anyone selling products with digital elements in the EU must report an actively exploited vulnerability or a serious incident as an early warning within 24 hours and follow up with a full report within 72 hours. The law's main obligations — security requirements, conformity assessment, documentation — don't take effect until 2027-12-11.

This week was the first real test case for that, and it came in a series: three actively exploited flaws in products used across the EU en masse. Whether early warnings were actually filed for them isn't visible from outside — the reports go to ENISA and the national CSIRTs and aren't public. That's exactly what makes the regime impossible to assess for now, and it's the weakest point in the construction: an obligation nobody outside can verify only works through fear of the regulator.

For a shop like twiceD, the relevant question is simply: are you selling a product in the EU, or providing a service? The CRA doesn't apply to the latter. It does apply to self-hosted software with EU customers — and in that case, it's worth writing down the reporting chain before you need it.
