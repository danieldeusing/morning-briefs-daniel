CISA Is Shutting Down the Weekly Bulletin — and Says Why, Honestly

For years, the US cyber agency CISA has summarized every week's newly accumulated vulnerabilities, sorted by severity. On Wednesday, September 16, it announced it's stopping: the Vulnerability Bulletin will run for the last time on 2026-09-28. The reasoning is right there in the announcement itself — it's part of the shift "from severity-based vulnerability management to a modern, risk-based approach," in line with Directive BOD 26-04, which instructs agencies to prioritize by real risk factors, "including evidence of exploitation and exposure, rather than severity scores alone."

Severity score (CVSS) vs. the KEV catalog: A CVSS score is an assessment of how bad a flaw would be — roughly like a car's crash-test rating: a property of the vehicle, regardless of whether anyone's actually driving it. The KEV catalog, by contrast, only lists what's actually being attacked right now — the roads where crashes are happening today. For the question "what do I do first?", the second list is far more useful, even though the first one is more complete.

As a replacement, CISA points to the KEV catalog, its own advisories, the CVE program — and, remarkably candidly, the advice to consult "vendor and provider advisories directly." So the agency is now only curating the part where doing nothing demonstrably hurts; the rest is left for vendors to tell you themselves.

My take: this is the right decision for the wrong reason. Right, because a weekly list sorted by severity stopped being an actionable work list years ago — with roughly a thousand CVEs a month from a single vendor's patch day alone, completeness isn't a service, it's noise. The wrong reason is the implicit assumption that "is it being exploited" gets to be the only filter that still counts. A flaw nobody's exploiting isn't harmless — it just hasn't had its turn yet. Anyone who follows only the KEV catalog is always patching exactly one attack too late.

Atlassian: 144 High, 17 Critical — and Almost None of It Is Atlassian's Own Code

Atlassian's monthly bulletin came out on 2026-09-15. The opening line tells the whole story: "The vulnerabilities reported in this Security Bulletin include 144 high-severity vulnerabilities and 17 critical-severity third-party vulnerabilities." Anyone who scans the table finds the same pattern in every row — Netty, Bouncy Castle, Apache Tomcat, jackson-databind, lodash, nanoid, browserslist, TinyMCE, js-yaml. Almost every entry names a third-party library, not an Atlassian module.

The two top entries, per Atlassian's own bulletin, are two Netty findings at CVSS 10.0, both described as man-in-the-middle flaws, both in Confluence Data Center — fixed in the LTS releases 10.2.17/10.2.18 and 9.2.24/9.2.25. (The CVE numbers the bulletin lists point, in public CVE databases, to a different and lower-severity vulnerability, so they're omitted here on purpose.) Right below them sit several Bouncy Castle findings at 9.3 and two Tomcat entries at 9.1, spanning Confluence, Jira, Jira Service Management, Crowd, and Fisheye/Crucible.

Transitive dependency: You build your app on library A. A uses B, B uses C. All three end up in your build in the end — you never picked C, and you've probably never even read it. It's like grocery shopping: you buy a ready meal, and the ingredient list names an emulsifier whose maker you've never heard of. A flaw in C is still your flaw, because your process is the one running the code.

And this is exactly where the score logic breaks down. Two perfect tens in a product sitting on the intranet of thousands of companies — and still no alarm, no deadline, no headline. Rightly so: the Netty MITM flaws require a position on the network path that an attacker doesn't normally have. The score measures the worst conceivable case; the bulletin measures the likely one. The list is still useful, though: Netty, Jackson, Tomcat, and Bouncy Castle sit inside pretty much every Spring Boot application out there. Atlassian has — unintentionally — published a very good checklist for your own dependency tree.

Spring: LOW According to the Vendor — and the Fix for 6.x Sits Behind a Contract

CVE-2026-59313 affects Server-Sent Events in Spring MVC's functional web framework: under certain conditions, an event stream can get crossed. Spring itself rates this as LOW on its advisory page, published on 2026-08-20. Affected versions are 7.0.0–7.0.8, 6.2.0–6.2.19, 6.1.0–6.1.28, 6.0.0–6.0.30, and 5.3.0–5.3.49.

Server-Sent Events (SSE): A long-lived HTTP connection the server uses to keep sending the browser small messages — stock tickers, progress bars, log lines. Like a radio broadcast instead of a phone call: it runs in one direction, and the receiver just keeps listening. If two streams get crossed, one listener suddenly hears someone else's broadcast.

The real point is in the version list below that. The fix is freely available only in 7.0.9. For 7.0.8.1, 6.2.20, 6.1.29, 6.0.31, and 5.3.50, it's commercial-support-access only. That's Spring's established policy, not a scandal — but it means that for anyone sitting on 6.2 or 6.1 without a contract, the only route to the fix is a major upgrade to the 7.x line. With a LOW rating, you can sit that out. With the next flaw of the same kind, maybe not — and at that point the decision on whether to tackle the 7.x migration will already have been made for you, just under time pressure.

WaterPlum: The Job Interview Is the Attack

On Friday, 2026-09-18, authorities from Germany, the US, Japan, and Australia published a joint advisory on a North Korean campaign they're tracking under the name WaterPlum (German coverage refers to the same scheme as "Contagious Interview"). The tally: over 30,000 compromised devices, more than 7,000 crypto wallets, and documented theft of $10.71 million.

The flow is uncomfortably close to a developer's everyday life. The attackers pose as recruiters and, per the advisory, target "web designers, engineers, and cryptocurrency and Web3 specialists." During the interview, the victim gets an assignment: "victims are instructed to download files presented as coding assignments or other recruitment tests." You download a repository, run it — and in the process install remote-access trojans and info-stealers. The stolen credentials then feed the next step: North Korean IT workers use the stolen identities to apply for remote jobs themselves.

Heads up This is the one story today that hits close to home for Daniel — freelance remote developers with a public profile are exactly the target group. The rule is simple and costs nothing: code from a hiring process never runs on the work machine. A container or a throwaway VM, no access to SSH keys, tokens, or wallets. A plain npm install is enough on its own — you don't even need to run a script from the assignment.

CrowdSec: The Security Vendor Got Opened Up Through the Package Chain

CrowdSec sells collaborative attack detection — and became a victim itself. On 2026-09-16, the company disclosed that unknown actors had broken in as far back as May 2026, via the supply-chain attack on the TanStack packages. Source code from the cloud infrastructure was exfiltrated, along with API tokens and service credentials; more than 300 repositories were affected, around 130 of them from the open-source Security Engine — whose code is public anyway. By the company's own assessment, no customer or user data was exfiltrated; CrowdSec says it's "keeping an eye on possible leaks of access credentials," has rotated all access rights, and has searched for planted backdoors.

Four months between the breach and disclosure is a long time, and that's the part that sticks. Not because anyone was careless, but because that's the typical detection time for compromised build dependencies: the break-in doesn't trigger an alarm, it leaves behind a valid token that behaves like normal operations. Together with the Shai-Hulud pattern and the RubyGems incident, this is the third case in a few months where it wasn't the application that got attacked, but the workshop where it gets built.

The AI Office Is Reviewing OpenAI — Because an Incident Was Never Reported

The second half of the same RubyGems incident now has a regulatory angle. According to a report by heise, the EU Commission's newly created AI Office is reviewing whether OpenAI violated the AI Act's reporting obligation: the company's agents accessed the RubyGems registry in May 2026 and allegedly attempted to exploit a freshly discovered vulnerability. "Under the AI Act, developers of high-risk and frontier models are required to report 'serious incidents' to the EU's newly created AI Office without delay." No formal report was filed — the agency found out from publications by outside security researchers.

Why this runs here and not in the AI brief: the hook isn't a model announcement, it's a reporting obligation tied to an attack on a package registry. It's the same mechanism that's applied under the Cyber Resilience Act since 2026-09-11 — reporting within hours, not quarters. Which means the first serious test case for these obligations turns out to involve, of all things, not a European vendor but an American one that should have reported and didn't. The report doesn't mention sanctions; the review is ongoing.

In-Memory Databases Are Moving Again: Dragonfly 2.0

Dragonfly 2.0, the open-source in-memory data store positioning itself as a drop-in Redis and Valkey replacement, shipped on 2026-09-16. The headline addition is interoperability: Dragonfly can now read RDB snapshots from Valkey 9, including hashes with both persistent and expiring fields. On top of that comes GEOSEARCHSTORE for geo queries with sorting and distance values, and replication now caps its backlog by age and memory budget instead of a fixed length.

The rest is housekeeping in the places that hurt in practice: input buffers now shrink from 64 to 32 KiB after quiet periods, RDB-write serialization buffers cap out at 4 MiB instead of the full snapshot size, and MEMORY DEFRAGMENT-SEGMENTS reclaims poorly utilized pages. No fireworks — this is the profile of a project that has arrived in production.

What's Worth Twenty Minutes Today

Five minutes: If the CISA weekly bulletin sits in an RSS reader or inbox, switch to the KEV catalog now — it has its own feed and a JSON file. After 2026-09-28, otherwise, nothing simply arrives anymore, with no error message.

Ten minutes: Run mvn dependency:tree or npm ls once against your most active project and check it against the names from the Atlassian bulletin: Netty, Bouncy Castle, Tomcat, jackson-databind, lodash, nanoid, TinyMCE. Not to patch right away, but to know which of these names are even in your own tree. The list ages slowly and saves half an hour of panic at the next incident.

Five minutes: Prepare a throwaway environment for other people's code — a container with no mounted home directory, no SSH agent, no credentials. The WaterPlum advisory doesn't describe an exotic attack, just the normal hiring process with a malicious repository inside it. The defense is one line in a docker run command you write once.

It's Saturday, September nineteenth. The thread running through today's briefing is a quiet decision out of Washington: the U.S. cyber agency CISA is retiring its weekly vulnerability bulletin. September twenty-eighth will be its last issue.

The reasoning is right there in the announcement. The agency is moving away from sorting by severity and toward judging real risk instead. What matters going forward is whether a flaw is demonstrably being exploited and whether the affected system is even reachable from outside. As a replacement, CISA points to its catalog of known exploited vulnerabilities and, notably, to vendor advisories themselves. My take on this is mixed. The move is right, because a complete weekly list, with a thousand new entries a month, stopped being a work plan long ago — it's just noise. It's risky, because a flaw nobody's exploiting yet isn't harmless. It just hasn't had its turn.

How much that severity number has lost its power to steer anything showed up again on Tuesday, courtesy of Atlassian. Its routine monthly bulletin quietly includes a hundred and forty-four high-severity and seventeen critical flaws, all in third-party code. Two of them carry the maximum score of ten point zero, both in the networking library Netty, both in Confluence Data Center. And yet there was no alarm and no deadline. That's actually defensible — those particular attacks need a position on the network that an attacker rarely has. What makes the list interesting is something else: almost every entry names a third-party library — Netty, Bouncy Castle, Tomcat, Jackson. Which makes the bulletin, unintentionally, an excellent checklist for anyone running Java and Spring.

Spring itself supplies the third data point. A bug in Server-Sent Events, disclosed in August, sits at the lowest severity tier according to the vendor. The real catch is buried in the version list underneath: the fix is freely available only in version seven-point-zero-point-nine. Anyone on a six-point-x line only gets it with a paid support contract. At a low severity rating, you can put that off. At the next bug of the same kind, though, the decision to migrate will already have been made for you — just under time pressure.

The day's most practically important story comes from security agencies in Germany, the United States, Japan, and Australia. On Friday they jointly warned about a North Korean campaign. More than thirty thousand devices are compromised, over seven thousand crypto wallets are affected, and documented losses total ten point seven million dollars. The bait is a job interview. Fake recruiters approach developers and web specialists and send over a coding assignment. Download it, run it, and you've installed a remote-access trojan. The stolen identities are then used to get North Korean IT workers hired into remote jobs themselves.

For a freelance developer with a public profile, that's not an abstract threat — it's a description of the target audience. The fix costs nothing: code from a hiring process never runs on the work machine, only inside a container with no keys and no credentials. A single package-install command is enough to get compromised; the attacker doesn't even need you to run a script of their own.

Two more stories belong together. The security vendor CrowdSec disclosed that intruders had already gotten in back in May, through the attack on the TanStack packages. Three hundred repositories were hit, with source code and access tokens exfiltrated, though customer data, by the company's own account, was not. Four months between the break-in and its discovery is the typical timeline for a compromised build dependency — a stolen token just looks like normal operations. And in Brussels, the EU Commission's new AI Office is reviewing whether OpenAI should have reported a separate May incident, in which the company's own agents allegedly attacked a package registry. Nothing was ever reported. The regulator found out from outside researchers' own publications.
