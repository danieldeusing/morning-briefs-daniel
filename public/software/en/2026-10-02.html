FortiMail: Actively Exploited, Still Without a Fix for Several Branches

CISA added CVE-2026-104286 to its catalog of known exploited vulnerabilities on 2026-10-01. It's a path-traversal flaw in Fortinet's mail gateway FortiMail with a CVSS 9.8 score. According to The Register, a crafted HTTP or HTTPS request is all an unauthenticated attacker needs to write arbitrary files to the underlying system. Fortinet confirms in advisory FG-IR-26-175 that the flaw is being exploited "in the wild."

The affected branches are 8.0.0–8.0.1, 7.6.0–7.6.6, 7.4.0–7.4.8, and 7.2.0–7.2.9. The most uncomfortable sentence is in the patch status: for several branches, Fortinet still lists the fixes as "upcoming." So this is a genuine zero-day. CISA requires US federal agencies to complete a forensic review and implement countermeasures by 2026-10-04.

Path Traversal: The server is only supposed to save files inside one specific folder. The attacker writes something like "../../" into the file name and uses it to climb out of that folder, up to the point where the system keeps its own programs. It's like a mailbox that drops every envelope wherever the address says to — even if the address reads "bedroom, top drawer."
Crafted HTTP(S) request→"../" escapes the target directory→Arbitrary file written→Persistence on the gateway
What helps right now Fortinet recommends disabling Identity Based Encryption if it isn't needed, and making sure the management interface isn't reachable from the internet. The important caveat: a workaround doesn't remove any files or backdoors already sitting on the device. Fortinet has published indicators for that — files, configuration changes, and IP addresses. Anyone who had the interface exposed should go looking for those, not just flip the switch.

My take: a mail gateway is, by definition, exposed to the internet and sees every incoming message. A foothold there is worth more than one on almost any other device on the network. What stands out is how often Fortinet keeps showing up in this catalog. That's not a statistical coincidence. It's the price of a product family that sits at the edge everywhere, and whose web interfaces apparently never stopped being attack surface.

Dell CSM: Six Critical Flaws in the Kubernetes Storage Layer

Dell published an advisory for its Container Storage Modules (CSM) — the components Kubernetes clusters use to talk to Dell storage systems. According to BleepingComputer, it covers six flaws, two of them at the maximum score. With CVE-2026-63688, unauthenticated remote attackers get the administrator credentials for all registered storage arrays. CVE-2026-63692 sits in the authorization proxy and the tenant service, and grants admin rights by bypassing authentication.

The remaining four flaws sound barely less serious. CVE-2026-67269 leads to root on cluster nodes. CVE-2026-54472 grants admin access to the authorization proxy. CVE-2026-61421 allows forging auth tokens, and CVE-2026-67273 bypasses Kubernetes access control, up to cluster-wide access to secrets. All versions before 1.18.0 are affected; the fix is in 1.18.0. The report doesn't mention any active exploitation.

CSI/storage driver: Kubernetes itself has no idea how to talk to a specific storage system. A driver handles that, acting as a translator between the cluster and the storage box, and it carries that box's admin keys in its pocket to do so. Whoever takes over the translator also gets the keys to the warehouse.

That's the lesson worth taking from this advisory, even without Dell storage in the building: the most dangerous components in a cluster are rarely your own applications. The danger lies in operators and drivers with far-reaching permissions. For every vendor's Helm chart, it's worth asking which secrets it's allowed to see.

bleepingcomputer.com

Apple EU: The Per-Install Fee Becomes a Commission

As of 2026-10-01, Apple's new terms for apps in the EU are in effect, announced on 2026-08-18 "after close cooperation with the European Commission." The core change: the controversial Core Technology Fee, a per-install charge, is gone. In its place comes a Core Technology Commission of 5% on digital revenue for apps distributed outside the App Store. The Initial Acquisition Fee and Store Services Fee are also gone. According to trade press, there's now a single rate card instead, with tiers of 26, 20, 15, and 5%.

Why the shift matters: A per-install fee punishes success that generates no revenue. A free app that suddenly goes viral would have paid per download without earning a single euro. A commission on revenue only costs money when money actually comes in. For small teams and free apps, that's the difference between "risk" and "a bill."

Also included: alternative payment methods alongside Apple's in-app purchase, child-safety requirements for those alternative payments, and expanded approvals for alternative marketplaces and web distribution. Contractually, all of this lives in Attachment 14 of the Developer Program License Agreement.

My take: this is the first DMA compromise that looks like actual negotiation rather than defiance. I only have the exact percentages per payment method from secondary sources — anyone doing the math should check the rate card in Apple's own document.

Also: Developer ID Sub-CA Is Expiring

Also on 2026-10-01, Apple announced that the original Developer ID Certification Authority expires on 2027-02-01. After that, .pkg installers signed with an affected certificate will no longer install. Mac apps already notarized with a secure timestamp will keep working. New certificates come from the G2 sub-CA, and when creating one, you have to explicitly select "G2 Sub-CA" — otherwise, per Apple, you might end up with another certificate that expires in 2027. For CI pipelines that build Mac installers, this belongs on next quarter's ticket list.

Microsoft's October: Three End-of-Support Dates in Two Weeks

According to t3n, Windows 11 SE, the stripped-down education edition, has been out of support since 2026-10-01. On 2026-10-13, two bigger items follow: Windows 11 version 24H2 and Office 2021, including LTSC 2021. Anyone still on 24H2 after that will stop getting security updates and will need to move to a newer Windows 11 version.

On that same day, per two secondary sources in agreement, the first year of Extended Security Updates for Windows 10 ends. A Microsoft primary source with a specific date wasn't accessible, so this comes with a caveat. Anyone keeping Windows 10 machines alive via ESU should now check whether and how the second year has been booked.

Heads up Office 2021 is the trap in this bundle. A purchased version feels "done," because it doesn't send subscription reminders. Starting 2026-10-13, it stops getting patches, and Office documents are among the most common entry points for attacks. Small offices running purchased licenses will be the last to notice.

Fitting that pattern, a heise report from today: in Windows 11 26H2, backing up Windows settings is set to become the default option. According to heise, Microsoft wants this to make Windows "more resilient."

Java 27: Operations Over Language Features

Catching up OpenJDK 27 shipped on 2026-09-15, with nine JEPs and no LTS status; heise reported on it on 2026-09-22. heise sums it up this way: Java 27 doesn't stand out with one big language feature, but with changes that matter for operations. Two of them are relevant for Spring shops.

JEP 523 makes G1 the default garbage collector in all environments — including small containers. JEP 527 brings a hybrid post-quantum key exchange for TLS 1.3, combining the classic ECDHE exchange with the quantum-resistant ML-KEM. On top of that, there's the third preview of the PEM API for certificates and keys.

Hybrid key exchange: You put two locks on the door — one proven, one new, built to resist future quantum computers. The attacker has to crack both. If the new lock turns out to be flawed, the old one still holds. If the old one is someday broken by quantum computers, the new one holds.

My take: the G1 change is the one you'll actually feel. Anyone running Spring Boot services in containers with limited CPU and RAM will get a different memory profile when jumping to 27, without changing a single line. Worth measuring before moving to the next LTS.

CRA: The Reporting Duty Is Running, the Standards Aren't

Since 2026-09-11, manufacturers have been required under the Cyber Resilience Act to report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents. The OpenSSF newsletter from 2026-09-30 clarifies that this duty applies exclusively to manufacturers. For open-source stewards, the obligations don't begin until 2027-12-11. At the same time, the deadline for harmonized standards, originally 2026-10-30, slipped back two months under a draft amendment from July.

Harmonized standards: The law says "secure." The standards say what "secure" means in concrete terms — which tests, which documentation. Meet a harmonized standard, and you're automatically presumed compliant. Without one, you have to justify yourself why your own approach is good enough.

It's a lopsided situation: the duty to report already applies, but the yardstick for "built well enough" is still missing. For small manufacturers, that means setting up a working reporting process now and waiting on the rest until the standards are in place.

OpenSSF: npm Is Thinking About Post-Quantum Signatures

The same newsletter notes that the package-repository working group discussed npm's post-quantum plans for package signatures with Sigstore. The OpenSSF Governing Board has also committed to sustainable funding for public package registries. Neither is a release yet, but both point in the same direction, and it fits with Java 27: post-quantum crypto is moving from research into the tooling chain.

openssf.org · cyberresilienceact.eu

Browsers: Firefox Goes Purple, and the CVE Numbers Get Bigger

Firefox 157 shipped on 2026-09-29. Mozilla calls the new design "Nova" — per LWN, the biggest visual overhaul in years. The Register mainly sees a "very faint violet-purple tint" and rounder tabs. The smaller details are more interesting: compact display density is back, and WebRTC calls now use hardware compression.

The bundled advisory MFSA 2026-97 is rated "High" and lists well over 50 CVEs, including several sandbox escapes. Mozilla notes that internally found memory bugs are no longer rolled up into one bundled CVE, but instead each individual bug now gets its own number. So part of why the number is this high is simply that the counting changed. Anyone using CVE counts per release as a quality metric is, since this change, no longer comparing anything to the past.

Chrome: Chrome 154 arrived already on 2026-09-22. Malwarebytes counts 108 security fixes, 11 of them critical. On 2026-09-30, Google began an early stable rollout of Chrome 155 to a small share of users. Details on the fixes weren't available yet.

Cloudflare Basin: Iceberg Without a Cluster

As part of its Birthday Week on 2026-10-01, Cloudflare took its data platform out of beta and renamed it Basin. Pipelines becomes Basin Pipelines, R2 Data Catalog becomes Basin Catalog, and R2 SQL becomes Basin SQL. Underneath sits Apache Iceberg. CTO Dane Knecht promises no clusters to manage, no unnecessary data movement, and open standards that keep customers in control of their data.

On egress, the fine print is worth reading. Direct out of R2, it's free. Attach other billed services to the bucket, and those can generate costs, and for delivering streaming data to "sinks," Cloudflare charges fees once included allowances are used up. So "no egress fees" is accurate — but only for the path Cloudflare has in mind.
