Atlassian: the exploit is out, and so are the attackers

Since yesterday: Public PoC, first attack attempts.

Yesterday's status: critical flaw, fix available, no attacks known. Today there is a proof of concept from watchTowr and a ready-made Nuclei template. The honeypot operator Previdian says, according to The Hacker News: "Within two hours of public exploit details becoming available, we were already seeing exploitation attempts hit our honeypot network." By Oct 7, Previdian had counted a total of 15 attack attempts from three IP addresses in Japan and the US.[1] BleepingComputer confirms the attacks after the PoC.[2]

About the flaw: CVE-2026-21589 allows reading files in the web root without login. The Hacker News gives CVSS 9.3. BleepingComputer gives no score, and I did not check the NVD. Eight Data Center products are affected. The cloud variants are already patched.[1]

Not confirmed None of my sources names the affected version ranges, only the fixed versions. I have not read the Atlassian advisory itself. CVE-2026-21589 is not yet in the CISA KEV catalog: the current catalog version is 2026.10.04, which predates the advisory.[3]

My take: This is the classic Atlassian pattern, and it came as fast as expected. 15 attempts is few, but this is the phase in which scanners comb the internet. It becomes mass exploitation once the Nuclei template sits in the usual toolkits. If you run Jira or Confluence yourself and have not patched: a publicly reachable Data Center does not belong on the open internet today.

Valid certificates for Google, without hacking Google

The story is elegant and uncomfortable. Attackers changed the authoritative DNS records of the country domains .gh (Ghana), .sl (Sierra Leone) and .as (American Samoa). As a result, domains such as Google's country sites there pointed to the attackers' servers. The attackers then had 12 certificates for 7 Google and YouTube domains issued, 11 from Let's Encrypt and one from ZeroSSL.[1]

The certificates showed up in the Certificate Transparency logs on Sept 22 (.gh), Sept 25 (.sl) and Sept 27 (.as). Google revoked them on Sept 26 and Oct 1 and reported on it on Oct 6. According to Google, its own systems were not affected: "Google's own systems were not breached." But Google also writes: "we cannot guarantee that our analysis identified every affected domain".[1] BleepingComputer reports that other brands are affected too and that the registries are run by third-party companies.[2]

DV certificate: A TLS certificate where the certificate authority only checks that you control the domain, usually via DNS. Like the post office verifying your address by sending you a letter. Whoever redirects your mail gets the letter and passes the check.
Certificate Transparency (CT): A public logbook in which every certificate authority must record every certificate. Like a land registry: you cannot secretly register a house in your name without it appearing in the book. That is exactly how this attack was spotted.

Two details matter. First, according to BleepingComputer, revocation via Google's CRLSets protects only Chrome.[2] Other clients depend on their own revocation checking. Second, it is about the reuse of domain validations: Let's Encrypt reuses a passed validation for 30 days, while the CA/Browser rules allow 200 days. That drops to 100 days in March 2027 and to 10 days in March 2029.[1]

My take: No admin at Google could have done anything better here. CAA records do not help, because whoever controls the DNS zone also controls the CAA record. The only thing that worked was CT monitoring. If you run your own domains, watch them with a CT monitor such as Cert Spotter or via crt.sh. It costs nothing. And when choosing a domain, the quality of the registry matters more than you think.

SonicWall SMA1000: CVSS 10, but (so far) no attacks

On Oct 6, SonicWall published advisory SNWLID-2026-0017 for the SMA1000 gateways. The most severe flaw is CVE-2026-102255, an SSRF without login in the WorkPlace interface, with CVSS 10.0. Also included are a command injection (CVE-2026-102256, CVSS 7.8), a Zip Slip (CVE-2026-102257, CVSS 7.2) and an XSS (CVE-2026-102258, CVSS 5.5).[1] BleepingComputer confirms the "max severity" rating.[2]

Fix: version 12.4.3-03670 or newer, or 12.5.0-03082 or newer. There is no workaround. According to SonicWall there is "no evidence that any of the four flaws is being used in attacks".[1] An Anthropic researcher found the flaws. The AI angle is covered in the ai-dev brief.

SSRF (Server-Side Request Forgery): The attacker gets a server to make requests on their behalf, often to internal systems. Like a doorman whom a stranger asks to "just call the boss's office." The doorman is allowed to. The stranger is not.

My take: SMA gateways sit at the edge of the network by definition. The SMA line has had exploited flaws more than once. "No attacks known" for an appliance with CVSS 10 is a state of days, not weeks.

FortiBleed: The FBI says it is not over

According to The Hacker News, the FBI and Secret Service have warned since Oct 6 that the FortiBleed campaign against FortiGate firewalls continues. The figure, per The Hacker News: "more than 86,644 working device credentials spanning 194 countries as of June 19, 2026".[1] There is no CVE. Attackers use reused and leaked credentials, and the old SHA-256 password storage makes cracking easier.

My take: This is the uncomfortable kind of security problem. There is no patch you can apply and then have peace. Rotate admin passwords, enable MFA on the admin interface, and do not expose the management interface to the internet. It is boring and it helps.

npm: eight packages, one remote-access trojan

CloudSEK and Checkmarx found eight malicious npm packages; the campaign is called MALFEX. According to The Hacker News, the packages were "collectively downloaded 40,767 times", 37,419 of them for the package function-flag alone. The remote-access trojan "Overlord", written in Go and controlled via the Solana blockchain, sits in tlxbnhd, tldriver and mxdriver according to Checkmarx, plus an info stealer called "movinlike". The payload of function-flag could not be recovered because its host was offline; the package was still installable on Oct 1.[1]

C2 via a blockchain: A trojan needs a server to fetch commands from (command & control, C2). Normally you can shut that server down. If the commands sit in a public blockchain, there is nothing to shut down. Like a bulletin board that nobody is allowed to take down.

My take: 37,000 downloads for a package with such a generic name show that it landed in real dependency trees. Use lockfiles, npm ci instead of npm install in CI, and --ignore-scripts where possible. Install scripts are almost always the entry point.

Mold 3.0: the fast linker, now in Rust

Mold, the linker that is much faster than GNU ld, has moved completely from C++ to Rust with version 3.0.0. According to heise, 3.0 is a drop-in replacement for 2.42.1 and produces the same output. "Link speed is on par as well." When building all Gentoo packages, the developers found no regressions.[1]

The reason is robustness: "The switch to Rust is meant to make Mold more robust against corrupted input files." The C++ version could read out of bounds on broken object files and crash with a segfault. Now it aborts in a controlled way. The build runs via Cargo instead of CMake and needs Rust 1.95 or newer. The next goal: Mold as the default linker in Linux distributions.[1]

My take: A rewrite with no loss of speed and no regression across all of Gentoo is a strong achievement. For C++ teams with long link times, Mold remains the easiest win in the build. The Rust dependency in the toolchain is the price, and it is the same debate as with Python.

COSMIC bans AI code, GNOME wants AI bug reports

System76's COSMIC desktop no longer accepts AI-generated contributions. According to The Register, anyone opening a pull request confirms: "I have not included any LLM (also known as AI) generated content in this PR, including code, comments, and descriptions." Using AI to learn or to hunt bugs remains allowed.[1] At GNOME, Calendar and the Extensions platform also reject AI-generated submissions.

At the same time, GNOME developer Michael Catanzaro argues in a blog post from Oct 2 that GNOME should accept AI-generated bug reports. GNOME Security already cut its disclosure period from 90 to 30 days as of Aug 1.[1]

My take: This fits Google's OSS bounty pause from yesterday. Projects are splitting into two camps right now: some protect their review time with a ban, others hope for more bugs found. I find the split "AI for finding, not for writing" sensible. It can only be enforced on trust.

CRA: four weeks of reporting duty

The reporting obligations of the Cyber Resilience Act have applied since Sept 11, 2026. Anyone offering a product with digital elements in the EU must report every actively exploited vulnerability in it: an early warning "within 24 hours", a notification "within 72 hours" and a final report within 14 days after a fix is available. Reports go through ENISA's Single Reporting Platform. The duty also applies to products already on the market. Fines: up to €15 million or 2.5% of worldwide annual turnover.[1] The remaining CRA obligations apply from Dec 11, 2027.

My take: For any company with its own software, this means: a flaw like Atlassian's in your own product now starts a clock. If you have no process, no one who takes over the topic on the weekend, you have it too late.

What is worth twenty minutes today

If you run Atlassian Data Center and have not patched yet: put the instance behind a VPN, then patch. If you run domains: register them with a CT monitor. If you have FortiGate or SMA gateways: check admin access. And if you still run PostgreSQL 14: put an upgrade date before Nov 12 in your calendar.

It's Thursday, October eighth. Yesterday the Atlassian flaw was still just a warning. Today it's under attack. And the cleverest story of the day is about certificates issued for Google, without Google ever being hacked.

First, Atlassian. Since an exploit went public, honeypots are seeing attack attempts, according to The Hacker News as soon as two hours later. The self-hosted Data Center versions of Jira, Confluence, Bitbucket and other products are affected. An attacker can read files without signing in. Patches are available. The flaw is not yet in the catalog of the U.S. agency CISA.

Now the certificates. Attackers changed the DNS records of the country domains for Ghana, Sierra Leone and American Samoa. That let them get twelve valid certificates issued for seven Google and YouTube domains, almost all from Let's Encrypt. It was spotted through the public certificate logs. Google has revoked the certificates. The lesson: if you own domains, watch those logs. It costs nothing.

SonicWall has closed a flaw with the top rating of ten in its S-M-A one-thousand gateways. No attacks are known yet. And the FBI warns that the FortiBleed campaign against Fortinet firewalls is still running. According to The Hacker News, there are more than eighty-six thousand working credentials. No patch helps here, only new passwords and a second sign-in step.

In the npm ecosystem, eight malicious packages have turned up, downloaded around forty thousand times in total. They install a trojan that gets its commands through the Solana blockchain.

There's good news too. The fast linker Mold has moved completely to Rust in version three, without losing any speed. Python three-fifteen arrives tomorrow. And PostgreSQL fourteen gets updates only until November twelfth.

In the open-source world, the COSMIC desktop is banning AI-generated code in contributions, while GNOME is debating bug reports found by AI. And in Europe, the Cyber Resilience Act reporting duty has been in force for four weeks: if you have an exploited flaw in your own product, you must send an early warning within twenty-four hours.

Tomorrow we'll see whether Python three-fifteen arrives on time, whether the Atlassian flaw lands in the CISA catalog, and whether the PostgreSQL nineteen release candidate finally appears.
