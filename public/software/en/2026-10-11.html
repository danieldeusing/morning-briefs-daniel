DNS: the new root key signs today

IANA's table gives October 11, 2026 as the date for the switch to KSK-2024: "KSK-2024 is scheduled to sign the zone; KSK-2017 will no longer sign the zone."[1] Neither IANA nor Cloudflare names a time of day. The new key has key tag 38696; the old KSK-2017 has key tag 20326.[1][2]

KSK and trust anchor: DNSSEC signs DNS answers so nobody can forge them. At the very top of that chain sits the root key (KSK). Your resolver has to know this key as its "trust anchor." Think of a notary whose seal certifies every other seal: if the notary changes the seal and your resolver doesn't know the new one, it believes no document anymore.

Cloudflare describes the risk bluntly: "If a resolver does not trust the replacement key, its users may be unable to reach websites under any top-level domain."[2] Resolvers with RFC 5011 learn the new key automatically; according to Cloudflare, KSK-2024 has been in the root's DNSKEY set since January 11, 2025. The problem is resolvers that lost their learned state, for example through software upgrades or moves to new machines.[2]

Who is affected: Not customers of Cloudflare DNS, 1.1.1.1 and Gateway; Cloudflare says it already trusts KSK-2024.[2] Anyone who validates on their own can be affected: an Unbound on a Pi-hole, a BIND in a company network, an old router with its own resolver.

How to check Open the test page dnstest.dev/ksk-2024/. Or query the sentinels: is-ta-38696 should return a valid answer, not-ta-38696 a SERVFAIL. According to Cloudflare, an unclear result does not mean the key is missing.[2] If it really is missing, update the trust anchor following your software vendor's instructions.

My take: for most developers this is a non-event. But if you run your own resolver and rebuilt it since 2025, spend two minutes on it today. When DNS fails, it looks like "the internet is broken," and nobody thinks of a root key first.

Deno joins Cloudflare: what happens to the runtime, Deploy and JSR

On Friday, Oct 9, both sides announced the move. Deno writes: "the entire Deno team is joining Cloudflare."[1] Cloudflare writes: "The Deno team is joining Cloudflare, and we're merging workerd and celld!" and tags the post "Acquisitions."[2] Neither post names a purchase price or contract details.

These points matter for users, all per the Deno blog:[1]

  • Deno runtime: "We will support the Deno runtime for another year with monthly releases containing bug fixes and security updates." After that, development by the team ends.
  • Open source: "Deno will remain open source, and we welcome others who want to continue its development."
  • Deno Deploy: runs "six months before shutting down." Paying customers get help migrating to Cloudflare Workers.
  • JSR: keeps running, the infrastructure moves to Cloudflare.

The post says nothing about Fresh and Deno Sandbox. Cloudflare's goal: "make workerd self-hosting a first-class supported way to build and run apps using the Workers programming model."[2]

workerd: The open-source runtime behind Cloudflare Workers. Your code runs in it as a small JavaScript function. Until now that made sense almost only in Cloudflare's data centers. Self-hosting means the same runtime on your own server, like a Docker container without a Cloudflare account.

My take: for Deno this is an honest ending, not a sale with empty promises. One year of support and a clear statement is more than many acquisitions deliver. For the JavaScript runtime market it means: Node stays the standard, Bun the challenger, and Deno becomes a community project with an open future. If you use Deno Deploy in production, you now have a six-month deadline on your desk. The second part is the interesting one: a self-hosted workerd would be the first serious answer to "what if I want to leave Cloudflare?"

Deadline Deno Deploy: six months from Oct 9, the post gives no exact shutdown date. Deno runtime: updates until about October 2027.

Windows Update: correction to yesterday

Since yesterday: Yesterday we could not load the article and tentatively tied it to Secure Boot. Now we have it, and the picture is more concrete.

According to BleepingComputer, certificates that Windows Update needs are expiring: "A set of these certificates will expire on May 17, 2027 and June 19, 2027." Devices without matching updates lose Windows Update afterward.[1] The deadlines per the article:

  • Windows 11 24H2 and Server 2025: need at least the September 2025 update.
  • Windows 11 25H2 and later: no action needed.
  • Other Windows 11 versions, Server 2022, Windows 10: need the July 2026 update, by June 19, 2027 at the latest.
  • Windows 10 Enterprise 2019 LTSC, Server 2019 and 2016: need the July 2026 update, by May 17, 2027 at the latest.

For devices served through WSUS, the article names an exception.[1]

My take: this is not an emergency, but it is a real inventory item. The dangerous one is the forgotten box: the Windows PC in the workshop, the old VM for a client tool. It quietly loses updates in 2027 and nobody notices.

[1] bleepingcomputer.com (Oct 9)

Atlassian Data Center: weekly tally on a critical flaw

CVE-2026-21589 allows file access without login. Affected are the Data Center versions of Jira, Jira Service Management, Confluence, Bitbucket, Bamboo, Crowd and Crucible/Fisheye. Rating: CVSS 4.0: 9.3.[1] heise reported the first attacks on Oct 8: Previdian counted "156 attack attempts from 25 IP addresses in eight countries."[1]

Patch status: Fixes exist for all products. Help Net Security lists Confluence DC 9.2.26 or 10.2.19, Jira Software DC 9.12.40, 10.3.26 or 11.3.12, Bitbucket DC 9.4.26, 10.2.8 or 10.5.1.[2] For Bamboo and Crowd, sources contradict each other on individual version numbers. The authoritative list is in the Atlassian advisory, which we have not read ourselves.

If you can't patch right away: Reports name a WAF rule or a block via Tomcat RewriteValve as a stopgap.[3] The flaw is still not in CISA's KEV catalog as of today.[4]

SonicWall SMA1000: patch since Tuesday, attack status disputed

CVE-2026-102255 sits in the Appliance WorkPlace interface of models 6210, 7210 and 8200v. BleepingComputer calls it a maximum-severity flaw; the SMA 100 series is not affected.[1] heise writes that the flaw "opens internal functions."[2] We did not check the exact CVSS score and the fix firmware against the SonicWall advisory today.

Exploit status: A Previdian honeypot network saw exploitation attempts on Oct 9. SonicWall "has not yet flagged this vulnerability as actively exploited." Shadowserver counts more than 400 internet-exposed SMA1000 devices.[1]

My take: whether it's "attempt" or "attack" makes no difference to operators. The patch came on Oct 6, and a maximum-severity VPN interface does not stay unpatched on the network over a weekend.

Citrix NetScaler: the week's second SAML patch

CVE-2026-107406 is a memory overflow in NetScaler ADC and Gateway. It allows code execution or a crash, but only on systems configured as a SAML identity provider or service provider. Fix builds: 14.1-73.46 and 13.1-64.29 or newer, FIPS 14.1-73.46, FIPS/NDcPP 13.1.37.283.[1]

Exploit status: Citrix says: "not aware of any unmitigated exploits of this vulnerability." Shadowserver sees over 21,000 NetScalers online, about 20,000 ADC and 1,500 Gateway.[1] The older flaw CVE-2026-88779, by contrast, has been in the KEV catalog since Oct 4.[2]

What this Sunday is worth in twenty minutes

If you run your own DNS resolver: open dnstest.dev/ksk-2024/, done. If you use Deno Deploy: put a date for the migration in your calendar, six months go fast. If you have old Windows boxes: make a list of which ones need the July update by May 2027. The rest can wait for Tuesday's Patch Tuesday.

It's Sunday, October eleventh. A quiet day, but of all days, something is happening today in the foundation of the internet: the DNS root zone is switching its master key.

According to IANA, the new root key, KSK twenty twenty-four, starts signing the root zone today, and the old one from twenty seventeen steps down. If you run your own validating resolver, such as Unbound or BIND, it has to trust the new key. Otherwise, Cloudflare says, your users can't reach websites anymore. Most resolvers learned the key automatically long ago. A Cloudflare test page shows in seconds whether everything checks out.

The second big story came on Friday: the entire Deno team is joining Cloudflare. The Deno runtime gets one more year of monthly updates, after which the team's development ends. Deno stays open source. Deno Deploy shuts down after six months, and the JSR package registry keeps running. Cloudflare wants to make its own runtime, workerd, something you can host yourself.

A correction to yesterday: on the expiring Windows certificates, BleepingComputer says this is about Windows Update itself. The certificates expire in May and June of twenty twenty-seven. Devices without the right update will then lose their updates.

On this week's security flaws, the picture is steady. At Atlassian Data Center, attacks are under way on a flaw rated nine point three. SonicWall has patched its SMA one thousand devices, though whether they're already under attack is disputed. Citrix closed a second SAML flaw in NetScaler, with no known attacks there. And Cisco reports a flaw with the maximum score of ten in its License On-Prem server.

Today the deadline from the US agency CISA runs out for five old flaws in ProFTPD, BIND, Struts, ONLYOFFICE and Strapi. On Tuesday, a deadline follows for an Apple flaw. Also, 7-Zip has closed several flaws, but the maker doesn't give details.
