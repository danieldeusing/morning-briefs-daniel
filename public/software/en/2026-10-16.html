PostgreSQL 19 RC1: Test Now, Move in November [1][2]

Follow-up to yesterday Yesterday morning it wasn't there yet; later in the day it arrived: on Oct 15 the PostgreSQL Global Development Group released the first release candidate of PostgreSQL 19. The final version is planned for November 19, 2026.[1]

Since Beta 4 there have been mostly bug fixes: several fixes for REPACK (CONCURRENTLY), for pg_plan_advice, for parallel autovacuum and for new planner optimizations that depend on whether an expression can be NULL. COPY (query) TO … (FORMAT json) now uses the query's column names, including aliases, as JSON keys.[1] That last point in particular is a small but real behavior change if you already tested the JSON export in beta.

Release Candidate (RC): A version that is meant to be done unless someone finds a serious bug. Like the dress rehearsal before opening night: costumes and script are set, you're just hunting for mishaps.

For the upgrade, as always with major versions: pg_upgrade or pg_dump/pg_restore, no in-place update.[1] Our take: now is the right moment to run your CI against the RC1 image. If you wait until GA, you find your own bugs in the very week you actually want to migrate.

Deadline PostgreSQL 14 gets its last update on Nov 12, 2026; the current release is 14.24.[2] If you are still on 14, that leaves less than four weeks. Jumping straight to 18 is the calmer path; 19 is then a topic for spring.
[1] postgresql.org (“PostgreSQL 19 RC 1 Released!”, Oct 15) · [2] postgresql.org/support/versioning (accessed Oct 16)

Amadeus: A Spring Boot Heap Dump as a Data Leak [1]

We mentioned the Amadeus leak only briefly on Wednesday. Now the BSI is warning, and heise has added details (as of Oct 15, 6:54 pm). According to heise, the heap dump of a Spring Boot application was reachable through an endpoint without authentication. It contained credentials; the admin passwords allowed read and write access to the global system. Passenger data was visible in plain text and searchable by last name. A large share of the records examined concerned Lufthansa and Condor flights.[1] DE

Heap dump: A snapshot of the entire working memory of a running Java application, as a file. It holds everything the app currently has in mind: configuration, passwords, customer data. Like a photo of a clerk's desk with all open files and the note with the safe code lying on it.

According to heise, Amadeus was alerted to a “potential vulnerability” on Sep 21. The BSI says the hole has since been fixed. Amadeus does not say how many people are affected or whether anyone actually accessed the data; the investigation is ongoing. The BSI advises scrutinizing emails about outstanding payments or early check-in and, when in doubt, clarifying via the official contact point.[1]

heise does not name the endpoint. In Spring Boot, however, the Actuator ships exactly such an endpoint (/actuator/heapdump). Our opinion: this is not an exotic attack but a configuration mistake any Spring team can make. The mistake is boring, and that is exactly why it slips through.

To check Search all Spring Boot services for management.endpoints.web.exposure.include. Is it set to * or heapdump, and is the management port reachable from outside? The Actuator belongs on an internal port or behind authentication.
[1] heise.de (as of Oct 15, 6:54 pm)

Atlassian Data Center: Now Officially in the KEV [1][2]

Update since Oct 13 On Tuesday we still wrote: attacks, but no KEV entry. That has changed. CISA added CVE-2026-21589 to its catalog on Oct 15; US federal agencies must act by Sun Oct 18.[1]

KEV catalog: The list from the US cyber agency CISA of flaws that are demonstrably being attacked. Like a police report saying “break-ins are happening on this street right now” instead of “windows can break.”

According to BleepingComputer, the flaw allows access to files in the web root without login. The Data Center products are affected: Bitbucket, Confluence, Jira Software, Jira Service Management, Bamboo, Crowd, Crucible and Fisheye. The cause sits in a shared web-resource library that converts :: into /. According to BleepingComputer, the first attack attempts came within two hours after watchTowr published its analysis. On Jira with Crowd integration, this lets an attacker read crowd.properties with credentials.[2] We know the CVSS score only secondhand: security firms cite 9.3 under CVSS 4.0 (as of Oct 12). We have not checked the Atlassian advisory in the original.

PAN-OS CVE-2026-0322: Root via the Management Interface [1][2]

Yesterday we had only the identifier from the KEV catalog. Now heise has details (Oct 15). The flaw sits in the PAN-OS management interface. An attacker with a login and network access can execute code with root privileges. Palo Alto rates it “high” (CVSS 4.0: 8.6 per Palo Alto's advisory). Affected are PA-series and VM-series firewalls and Panorama; not Cloud NGFW or Prisma Access.[1]

According to Palo Alto there have been only very few attacks so far; CISA has nonetheless listed the flaw as exploited since Oct 14, deadline Sat Oct 17.[1][2] There are no workarounds. The fix list is long, for example 12.2.4, 11.2.16 and 10.2.18-h12; the full list is at heise. Those with Threat Prevention can enable threat IDs 510046 and 510047, but that requires SSL decryption.[1]

Context Because the attacker must already be logged in, this is not a flaw for the open internet but for the step after. The real problem is a management interface reachable from too many networks. heise and Palo Alto advise restricting it to trusted internal IP addresses.

CISA Deadlines: Ivanti Today, the Rest on the Weekend [1][2]

Today, Fri Oct 16, two US deadlines end: Ivanti Connect Secure (CVE-2026-104935) and Windows Shell (CVE-2026-96951, Mark of the Web bypass). Both entered the catalog on Oct 13.[1]

On Ivanti, the key points from the Rapid7 analysis: auth bypass with CVSS 8.6, already exploited before disclosure. Fixes are in 22.8R2.5, 25.1.1.2 and 25.1.3.0 and 25.1.3.1; versions 22.7.x and older are not affected. A third flaw in the privilege-escalation chain has no patch yet, and according to Rapid7 there are no reliable indicators of compromise.[2] Our view: patching a device that has been under attack for two weeks does not prove it is clean. Anyone running Ivanti should plan a rebuild, not just the update.

On Sat Oct 17 follow Cisco Secure Email Gateway (CVE-2026-76519) and two Ahsay AhsayCBS flaws (CVE-2026-105133, CVE-2026-105134). For these three we have only the KEV entry itself; we have not checked vendor advisories.[1]

Linux: Seven Stable Kernels, One Important Network Fix [1]

On Oct 15 seven stable kernels were released: 7.2.11, 6.18.57, 6.12.113, 6.6.159, 6.1.190, 5.15.223 and 5.10.272. They contain the fix for CVE-2026-99999 in skb_checksum_help(). An IPv4 packet with wrong checksum metadata could corrupt the network header, with “a bunch of mischief” as the result, according to patch author Michael S. Tsirkin.[1] For servers and container hosts that means: don't let the next distribution kernel update sit.

Stable kernel: The Linux developers keep maintaining several older versions in parallel and push only bug fixes, no new features. Like a carmaker still supplying recall parts for older models.
[1] lwn.net (Oct 15)

Ubuntu 26.10 and Plasma 6.8: Rust and Wayland Win Out [1][2]

Canonical released Ubuntu 26.10 on Oct 15. It brings kernel 7.3 and completes the switch to the Rust-based uutils coreutils. TPM-backed disk encryption now also works on hardware without a hardware root of trust. Toolchains included are Python 3.14.7, Rust 1.97.1, Zig 0.16 and Go 1.27.[1] Important for Docker images: this is an interim release, not an LTS. For base images, the latest LTS remains the sensible choice.

KDE Plasma 6.8 is Wayland-only. X11 programs keep running via XWayland, but the X11 session is gone. New are, among other things, system audio in Spectacle recordings and a faster remote desktop server. Kubuntu is due to get Plasma 6.8 with 27.04 in April.[2]

Wayland instead of X11: Both are systems that put windows on the screen. X11 dates from the 1980s; Wayland is the modern successor. Like the switch from a tube TV to a flat screen: old broadcasts keep running through an adapter.

Post-Quantum Crypto: Germany Sets 2030 as the Deadline [1]

On Oct 12 the BSI published a “guideline for the transition to PQC,” as part of a roadmap with the digital and interior ministries. High-risk cryptographic applications must be quantum-safe by end of 2030, medium-risk ones by end of 2035. The responsible bodies are to make the deadlines binding before the end of 2026. Every organization should build a crypto inventory; hybrid schemes are recommended.[1] DE

Hybrid schemes: You encrypt with a classical and a new, quantum-safe method at the same time. An attacker has to break both. Like two different locks on the same door.

Our view: for developers, the crypto inventory is the practical part. Where in the code is RSA, where is TLS with which libraries, where are signed tokens? You need this list anyway, whether for the federal government, the CRA or your own customer. According to heise, the guideline names no specific algorithms.[1]

[1] heise.de (Oct 15)

The Leap Second Is History [1]

The General Conference on Weights and Measures decided, per heise, in Versailles near Paris on Thursday: the leap second will be abolished on May 20, 2027. Instead, UTC and the Earth-rotation time UT1 may drift apart by up to an hour. Only at a deviation of 3,600 seconds does a leap hour come. Experts expect that to be needed only in more than 1,000 years.[1]

For software this is good news. Leap seconds have thrown servers, databases and clusters out of sync because a minute suddenly had 61 seconds. A negative leap second, for which hardly any system is prepared, is also off the table.[1]

EU Cloud Law: The Lobby's €115 Billion Bill [1]

The EU Commission is planning the Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA), with sovereignty rules for cloud services. CCIA Europe, a lobby group whose members include Amazon, Google and Meta, commissioned a study from ECIPE. Result: up to €115 billion in costs over ten years, more than six times the Commission's upper estimate of €18 billion. Around €80 billion of that, however, is a model scenario: it assumes Article 31 extends the rules to a quarter of cloud workloads in key private sectors.[1] EU

Our view: whoever sells sovereign cloud offerings themselves likes to inflate the cost of sovereignty. The number is a negotiating argument, not a forecast. What matters for developers is Article 31. If it applies, “where does this run” becomes an architecture question even for mid-sized companies.

AdFection: Spyware via Ads [1]

The Citizen Lab at the University of Toronto describes how ad networks become an entry point for state trojans. Loading a manipulated ad in an app or on a web page is enough; no click is needed. The Israeli company Discover-Tech received the device's advertising ID and IP address and delivered malicious JavaScript to targets. The ads themselves contained no exploit code and so passed the ad platforms' checks. Google and IAB Europe have since removed Discover-Tech.[1]

Advertising ID: An identifier the phone passes to ad networks so they can match ads. Like a customer number you show everywhere without noticing.

Linux Kernel Drops AI Labeling [1]

According to Sasha Levin, the Maintainers Summit agreed to abolish the Assisted-by tag. It was meant to make developers disclose AI tools in patches; the rule had been in the docs since January 2026. Jeff Layton of Red Hat gives three reasons: not everyone follows it, the tag is free advertising for AI companies, and its purpose is unclear. Laurent Pinchart disagrees; he wants a different disclosure mechanism.[1] Our view: that is honest. An obligation nobody can check is just decoration. AI tools themselves are a topic for the ai-dev brief.
