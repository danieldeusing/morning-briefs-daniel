PHP2Shell, day two: the attackers were there before the patch

Since yesterday The fixes have been out since Oct 21: PHP 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35 close CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, both with CVSS 4.0 9.2 and exploitable without a login.[1] What is new is how early the other side was. According to The Hacker News, Amazon's honeypot network MadPot logged an exploitation attempt on Oct 17, four days before the release.[2]

Honeypot: A deliberately exposed decoy server whose only job is to record attacks. Like a fake ATM with a camera: anyone who tinkers with it gives away their method.

heise reports on Oct 22: updates are trickling in, many websites are offline, attacks are under way. heise had not confirmed any successful break-ins by then.[3] The Register reports millions of attempts at Cloudflare and 32 downed UK government services.[4] Both come from that one source only; we have not cross-checked them.

What php.net calls interim hardening does not replace the patch: memory_limit=-1 via php_admin_value, post_max_size at 8M, session.upload_progress.enabled=0, and under 8.5 also fatal_error_backtraces=0.[1] According to php.net, mod_php is the most exposed. Watch out with old branches: 8.2 only gets security fixes until Dec 31, 2026, and 8.1 and older get none at all from upstream.[1]

Our take An attempt four days before the fix means someone knew details before the hosts had their packages ready. According to LWN, the delay was "a structural failure, caused by both the security team's decision process and its lack of resources."[5] That is honest, but it is exactly the problem: a project that a large part of the web runs on has a volunteer-sized security team.

GitLab: the next maximum-severity flaw [1][2][3][4]

On Wednesday, Oct 21, GitLab fixed CVE-2026-110264, an integer overflow in the regex compiler. BleepingComputer quotes GitLab: the flaw "could have allowed an unauthenticated user to execute arbitrary code on the GitLab server".[1] GitLab itself rates it Critical, with a CVSS score of 10.0.[4] According to The Hacker News, self-managed instances going back to version 7.0 are affected.[2]

Fixed in 19.5.1, 19.4.3, 19.3.6, 19.2.9, 19.1.9, 19.0.10 and 18.11.13. GitLab.com is already patched, and Dedicated customers on these builds need to do nothing. No exploitation has been reported.[1] According to heise, the update closes twelve flaws in total.[3]

Integer overflow: A number grows larger than its storage space can hold and then wraps around to a small value. Like an old odometer that starts again at 0 after 99,999. If a program then computes memory from the wrong value, that can become an entry point.

It is already the second regex-compiler flaw in eleven days. CVE-2026-97229 (CVSS 10.0, Oct 12) still needed a manipulated CI/CD configuration; according to BleepingComputer, both come from the same researcher.[1] No source says how the two are technically related.

eMudhra: retreat from the TLS business [1][2][3][4][5][6]

Since yesterday The interim tally: the private keys of eight eMudhra intermediate certificates are compromised. Mozilla blocks them via OneCRL and NSS 3.131.1.[1] Chrome blocks the six TLS intermediates.[5] Since Oct 19, Go rejects the chains from 1.27.3 and 1.26.10.[6] Node.js cleaned up its CA store in the Oct 21 release.[2]

New, per The Hacker News: eMudhra is exiting the global SSL/TLS business. Chrome plans to stop trusting the root emSign ECC Root CA - G3 around Oct 27; about 30,800 still-valid certificates are affected.[3] The date and the number come from this one source only.

Intermediate CA: A subordinate certificate authority that the root CA allows to issue certificates. Like a branch manager with the company stamp: if his stamp is stolen, you do not just void his letters, eventually you stop trusting the whole company.

The Register writes that one of the keys was the number "4". On oss-security, by contrast, the original post is said not to prove the key was easy to guess.[4] That remains a claim. In practice: if you have customers with emSign certificates, switch the CA before Tuesday.

KEV: Plex2Shell deadline expires today [1][2]

US federal agencies must fix CVE-2026-96656 and -96654 in Plex Media Server and CVE-2026-106931 in EG SiteGuard Server Edition by today. For -96655, -96652 and Siemens Software Center (CVE-2026-110936) the deadline is Nov 3.[1] According to BleepingComputer, the Plex chain has a score of 9.8. The fix has been in 1.43.3.10861 since August 4, and exploitation has been under way since Oct 17.[2]

KEV catalog: The list from the US agency CISA of flaws that are demonstrably being attacked. Like the police list of car models that are actually being broken into right now, not just in theory.

October in the catalog is full: numerous entries, including BeyondTrust Remote Support, Fortinet, Atlassian, PAN-OS, Cisco Secure Email Gateway, Ivanti Connect Secure, Windows and Citrix NetScaler.[1] Our state is the catalog version from Oct 21, 14:04 UTC; later entries are missing here.

If you run Plex in a homelab: check the version and do not expose the server directly to the internet. According to BleepingComputer, the chain starts without a login and obtains the admin token on its own.[2]

Arista VeloCloud: the fourth zero-day of the year [1][2]

Arista fixed CVE-2026-114625 in the VeloCloud Orchestrator, an SQL injection that gives attackers operator rights. It is already being actively exploited. According to BleepingComputer, Arista has already patched its hosted VCO deployments to 5.2.3.18, 6.1.3.10 and 6.4.2.12. BleepingComputer gives no fixed On-Prem builds; On-Prem admins should check Arista's advisory.[1] On the score the sources contradict each other: The Hacker News says 9.9, BleepingComputer writes only "critical".[1][2]

According to BleepingComputer, this is the fourth exploited Arista zero-day of 2026. For network management systems that is a pattern, not a coincidence: whoever has the orchestrator has all the branch offices.

LiteLLM: how the pipeline was hijacked [1][2]

Short, because the product is an AI gateway (details in the ai-dev brief). The software lesson is general: litellm 1.107.0 sat on PyPI for just under two hours on Oct 21, and longer on Docker Hub and GHCR. The malicious version dropped .pth files and a sitecustomize.py that run on every Python start.[1] According to The Hacker News, the attackers used stolen GitHub CLI OAuth tokens from three developers.[2] 1.106.2 is safe; if you had 1.107.0, rotate all secrets and rebuild.[1]

.pth file: A small file in the Python package folder that the interpreter reads on every start, even if the package is never imported. Like a note on the fridge that everyone reads in the morning, no matter what they have for breakfast.

Linux KSMBD: two flaws without a mainline fix [1]

The Hacker News reports two flaws with CVSS 9.0 in the kernel SMB server KSMBD. One is reachable without a login via port 445, the other needs SMB Direct. Both were described on public lists before there was a kernel fix.[1] Only one source. If you do not deliberately use KSMBD, you do not load the module; if you do, do not expose port 445 to the internet.

HTTP/3: QPACK as a memory hog [1]

A denial-of-service weakness in the HTTP/3 header format QPACK allows up to 4,000x memory amplification, according to The Hacker News. Fixes exist in quic-go, quiche, lsquic and Wireshark; Bun, h3 (Rust) and aioquic were still open at publication. There is no CVE.[1] Relevant for Caddy users because Caddy speaks HTTP/3 via quic-go: keep an eye on the next Caddy release.

QPACK: The compression scheme for HTTP/3 headers. Instead of sending every header again, the client points to a shared table. Like speed dial on a phone: handy, until someone stuffs the table with nonsense.

Cargo learns to reject too-fresh packages [1]

According to heise, Cargo is getting an optional block against packages that were published only recently.[1] That is the right answer to cases like LiteLLM: most poisoned versions are caught within hours. If you wait a few days, you are never part of those cases.

Minimum package age: The package manager installs a version only after it has been public for a while. Like yogurt from the back of the shelf: if it is bad, others have already noticed.

curl: small flaws public from Monday [1]

From Oct 26, low and medium reports for curl land publicly on GitHub. High and critical stay private on HackerOne. According to Daniel Stenberg, over 90 percent of the recent reports were below High.[1] Our view: the right call. An embargo for small stuff costs maintainer time and protects nobody.

CRA: this week is the real test [1]

Since Sep 11, 2026, the reporting duties of the Cyber Resilience Act have applied. If you sell products with digital elements in the EU, you report an actively exploited flaw within 24 hours as an early warning, followed after 72 hours by the notification, via ENISA's Single Reporting Platform. Fines reach up to €15 million or 2.5 percent of turnover. The main obligations arrive on Dec 11, 2027.[1]

PHP2Shell and GitLab are the practical test now: if you ship a product with PHP or GitLab and learn of exploitation, a clock is running.

Cyber Resilience Act (CRA): An EU law that makes manufacturers of software and devices responsible for their security. Like a recall duty for cars: whoever knows about a defect must report and fix it.

The rest can wait until Monday.

It's Friday, October twenty-third. Two days after the PHP patches, one thing is clear: the attackers got there before the fixes did. On top of that, there's a severe GitLab flaw, and an Indian certificate authority is pulling out of the business.

First, PHP. Versions eight point five point twelve, eight point four point twenty-seven, eight point three point thirty-six and eight point two point thirty-five close two flaws that let attackers run code without logging in. According to The Hacker News, Amazon's honeypot network saw an attempt as early as October seventeenth, four days before the fix. heise reports many websites offline, but no confirmed break-ins yet. The hardening tips from php.net are no substitute for the update.

Then GitLab. The flaw, tracked as CVE-2026-110264, sits in the regex compiler and allows code execution without a login. GitLab rates it critical, with the top score of ten out of ten. Since Wednesday, it's been fixed in version nineteen point five point one and six older branches. No exploitation is known so far, and GitLab.com is already safe.

At eMudhra, the keys for eight intermediate certificates are compromised. According to The Hacker News, the company is leaving the TLS business altogether, and Chrome plans to stop trusting its root around October twenty-seventh. About thirty thousand eight hundred certificates would be affected. If you use any of them, switch before Tuesday.

Today is the deadline set by the U.S. agency CISA for two Plex flaws from the Plex2Shell chain. The fix has been available since August. Separately, Arista closed an actively exploited flaw in its VeloCloud Orchestrator, the fourth one this year.

On LiteLLM, we now know more about how the pipeline was hijacked: according to The Hacker News, with stolen GitHub tokens from three developers. The lesson for everyone: Python startup hooks are a way to persist. Fittingly, Cargo can now reject packages that are too fresh.

There's good news too. Linux seven point three has been out since October eighteenth and is likely to be the next long-term kernel. Python has three bugfix releases, and curl will make small flaws public starting Monday. And for Angular, version twenty loses long-term support at the end of November.

Between now and Monday, we'll be watching whether the PHP attempts turn into real break-ins, and whether Chrome holds to the emSign date.
