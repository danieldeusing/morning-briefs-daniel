Collabora: the Nextcloud flaw nobody saw coming [1][2]

CISA added CVE-2026-110447 to the KEV catalog on Oct 23, and the deadline for US agencies is today.[1] The issue is not Collabora in general but the built-in CODE server that Nextcloud ships through the richdocumentscode app. According to BleepingComputer, the fix was committed on Sep 9, version 26.4.303 came out on Sep 11, and the first attacks happened as early as Sep 10. The advisory and CVE followed only on Oct 14.[2]

The attack runs without login via proxy.php and drops webshells. An awkward detail: per BleepingComputer, the precondition is that the built-in CODE server is stopped, for example after a restart, update or crash. And per the article "disabling the app is insufficient". Standalone Collabora installations, Docker, RPM and DEB packages, and Nextcloud AIO are not affected.[2] Only The Hacker News gives the CVSS score of 9.2; we have not checked it against NVD.

Heads up If you run a Nextcloud with the "Nextcloud Office (built-in CODE server)" app, check that the app version is 26.4.303 or newer. Because the attacks began a month before the advisory, it is worth looking for unknown PHP files in the webroot even if the update went on long ago.
Our take This is the self-hoster's nightmare: a month between fix and advisory in which nobody knew they had to act. Auto-updates for Nextcloud apps are the only thing that would really have helped here.

PHP2Shell: one claim fewer, the problem just as big [1][2]

New since Saturday According to The Hacker News on Oct 25, Palo Alto's Unit 42 has withdrawn its earlier statement that the flaws were used in an attack on a European government agency. In the same breath, the outlet reports a suspicion of worldwide scanning by North Korean actors.[2]

As a reminder: CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619 allow code execution without login. They have been fixed since Oct 21 in 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35, with minimal backports for 7.4, 8.0 and 8.1. Calif, OpenAI and Wiz found them. Per the PHP security team, the risk is highest with mod_php. Cloudflare saw attacks early and enabled temporary WAF rules, and the UK's NCSC advised: update or take it offline.[1] For the often-cited CVSS score of 9.2, we have only search hits in this round; BleepingComputer gives none.

Our take A retraction on attribution changes nothing about the technology. Anyone who hoped over the weekend that "it's not that bad after all" is reading the report wrong. The question for today remains: is any PHP below the fixed release of its branch (8.2.35, 8.3.36, 8.4.27, 8.5.12) still running somewhere, even in an old client project? There is also a second, still unpatched PHP flaw (CVE-2026-115560, known since Oct 23, needs an unusual session serializer configuration). Per BleepingComputer the mitigation is session.upload_progress.enabled=0.

Ruby: three releases against the GitLab regex bug [1][2][3]

Ruby 4.0.9, 3.4.12 and 3.3.13 were released on Oct 23. They come with an announcement titled "Regexp compilation fixes related to GitLab's CVE-2026-89078, CVE-2026-93577, CVE-2026-97229 and CVE-2026-110264". The releases fix several bugs in Ruby's regex engine Onigmo that GitLab reported.[1] CVE-2026-110264 is the maximum-severity GitLab flaw from Oct 21 that we covered on Sunday: code execution without login.[3]

The Hacker News sums up the consequence this way: any application that builds a regular expression from text an attacker controls is affected.[2] Ruby 4.0.8, incidentally, only came out on Oct 22, one day earlier.[1]

Regex compiler: A regular expression like ^a+b$ is a tiny program. Before Ruby searches with it, Onigmo translates it into an internal form. If the translator has a calculation error, a maliciously crafted expression can overwrite memory. This only becomes dangerous when outsiders get to define the expression themselves, for example through a search box with a regex option.
Our take This is the real lesson of the GitLab case: the bug was not in GitLab but one layer deeper, in the language. If you have Ruby services with user-supplied regex, upgrade the runtime, not just the gem.

Plex2Shell: the homelab as crypto miner [1][2]

Four Plex flaws are chained together: SSRF via /player/timeline (CVE-2026-96652), URL injection (-96654), authenticated SSRF (-96655) and a file-write bug (-96656). Researcher Zach Main reported them on Jul 27, published details and a PoC on Oct 16. Since Oct 17 there have been reports of real attacks with crypto miners. Main rates the chain at 9.8.[1]

CISA has listed all four in the KEV catalog since Oct 20; the deadline for -96654 and -96656 ran out on Oct 23, for -96652 it runs until Nov 3.[2] 1.43.3.10861 and newer is safe, released as early as early August.[1] So the fix was ready a good ten weeks before the first attacks. Anyone who does not update Plex automatically wasted that time.

Node.js: Brotli: public PoC, no known attacks [1][2]

The security release of Oct 21 closes ten CVEs, four of them high, in 22.23.4, 24.22.1 and 26.12.1. The most severe is CVE-2026-111497, an out-of-bounds write in the Brotli decoder that, per Node.js, allows code execution. In addition there are two bypasses of the TLS hostname check via unusually written IP addresses and a NUL-byte bug in dns.resolve.[1]

On Oct 24, The Hacker News writes that Bun, Python and other software are also affected by the Brotli bug; no attacks have been reported. A public RCE proof of concept (Node2Shell) exists, per The Hacker News on Oct 23.[2] We have not independently confirmed this extension to other runtimes. Node.js itself gives neither a CVSS score nor an exploit status.

eMudhra: keys from a faulty script [1][2]

Eight intermediate certificates of the Indian CA eMudhra are revoked, six for TLS and two for code signing. Cause according to BleepingComputer: a script error in key generation in April 2025 produced a second set of weaker P-384 keys that were accidentally selected for signing. A researcher found this on Oct 17; Mozilla (OneCRL) and Chrome revoked them the same day. There is no indication, per the article, of end-user certificates issued with these keys.[1]

Apple and Microsoft followed within a few days; the exact dates conflict between sources. Node.js removes the eMudhra CA from its built-in CA store with the security release of Oct 21.[2] According to The Register, eMudhra is subsequently exiting the global TLS business entirely (see Sunday).

Intermediate certificate: Browsers trust a small list of root certificates. The root signs intermediate certificates, and those sign the certificates of websites. If the key of an intermediate certificate is weak, someone who guesses it can issue a valid-looking certificate for any domain. That is why it gets revoked before there is abuse.

Supply chain: LiteLLM, short and with a pointer [1][2]

The poisoned LiteLLM 1.107.0 was on PyPI (11:39 to 13:23 UTC), Docker Hub and GHCR on Oct 21. According to LiteLLM's own post, the attackers got in through stolen GitHub CLI OAuth tokens, switched off branch protection and started the release workflow themselves. The malware embeds itself through .pth files and sitecustomize.py; the recommended version is litellm==1.106.2.[1] The Hacker News, by contrast, writes of a leaked master key from a repo leak in May 2026.[2] We follow the primary source. The AI side of the story is in the ai-dev brief.

Our take For non-AI teams, the lesson is universal anyway: a CLI token on a developer laptop is enough to switch off branch protection. Fine-grained tokens and protected release environments in GitHub Actions are no longer optional extras.

BlackX: Kaspersky sees APT41 behind it [1]

There is an addition to Sunday's bootkit: on Oct 23, The Hacker News reports that BlackX bypasses Secure Boot on Windows systems that lack the revocations. Kaspersky attributes it to APT41 and considers it an evolved version of BlackLotus.[1] The mitigation stays the same: enable the Secure Boot revocations deliberately, and have a current boot medium ready first.

CRA: the first real reporting week [1][2]

Since Sep 11, manufacturers under the Cyber Resilience Act must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents through ENISA's Single Reporting Platform: early warning in 24 hours, notification in 72 hours, final report 14 days after the fix. Fines go up to EUR 15 million or 2.5% of worldwide turnover; the CRA applies in full from Dec 11, 2027.[1][2]

Our take Collabora, Plex and PHP2Shell are exactly the cases the deadline was designed for. If you ship a Nextcloud or a PHP backend as a commercial product into the EU, you are a manufacturer. There are no new figures from ENISA so far.

Good morning, it's Monday, October twenty-sixth. The weekend was quiet, but last week's open problems are still open.

Today the deadline from the US agency CISA runs out for two actively exploited flaws. The first sits in Nextcloud's built-in office server, the Collabora app. Attackers have used it since September tenth to plant webshells, a month before an advisory even appeared. Just turning the app off isn't enough; you need version twenty-six point four point three-oh-three. The second flaw affects the Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator.

On PHP2Shell, Palo Alto's Unit forty-two has withdrawn its claim that the flaws were used against a European agency. That changes nothing about the technology. If you still run PHP older than version eight point two point thirty-five, you should change that.

Ruby shipped three releases on October twenty-third. They fix bugs in the Onigmo regex engine that are behind the serious GitLab flaw. Any application that builds regular expressions from outside text is affected.

Also: Plex servers have been hijacked for crypto miners since October seventeenth, and you're safe from version one point forty-three point three. There's a report about unpatched flaws in Tailscale SSH, but so far from just one source and without confirmation from Tailscale. The certificate authority eMudhra has lost eight intermediate certificates over weak keys.

There's good news too: Python three point fifteen point two has been out since Sunday, and Linux seven point three since October eighteenth. For PostgreSQL fourteen, support ends on November twelfth.

My advice for today: read less new stuff, check off more old stuff. Check Nextcloud, PHP, Ruby and Plex, and your week is off to a good start.
