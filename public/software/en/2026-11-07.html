SonicWall: VPN Login Without a Second Factor Is Being Exploited

SAML: A standard that lets a central login service (for example Entra ID or Okta) tell another application: "This person is signed in." Picture a hotel key card issued by the front desk that every door accepts. If one door doesn't check the card properly, half a card gets you in.

SonicWall warns about CVE-2026-19868, an authentication bypass in the SSL VPN of SonicOS when login runs through SAML. According to BleepingComputer, the CVSS score is 7.1. Gen7 up to 7.3.3-7015 and Gen8 up to 8.2.2-8015 are affected; the fixes are 7.3.4-7013 and 8.2.3-8015. There is no workaround. SonicWall writes: "SonicWall PSIRT has investigated a report indicating potential exploitation of this vulnerability."[1]

CISA added the flaw to the KEV catalog on Nov 6, under the title "Use of Single-factor Authentication." The deadline for US federal agencies is Nov 20.[2] The title says the most important thing: the second factor you once ran a whole MFA project for does not protect you here.

Heads up The CVSS score of 7.1 and the version numbers come only from BleepingComputer. The advisory SNWLID-2026-0014 and the NVD page did not load when we checked. Anyone patching should cross-check the versions in the advisory itself.
Our take 7.1 sounds like "medium," but the number hardly matters here. An exploited flaw in the VPN login is the front door to the company network. SonicWall devices have appeared in KEV again and again in recent years. Anyone running them needs a fixed patch rhythm for the firewall, not just for the servers behind it.

SonicWall GMS: Vulnerable and Without a Future

Update since yesterday Just one sentence yesterday, the details today. The Global Management System (GMS) up to and including 9.5.2 has two flaws. CVE-2026-109593 is critical and allows reading files without logging in. CVE-2026-109594 is a medium-severity SQL injection that requires the attacker to be logged in.[1]

Patches are not coming, because GMS has reached end of support. In advisory SNWLID-2026-0019, SonicWall recommends moving to NSM. According to heise, no exploitation is known so far.[1]

Our take A management system manages all the firewalls. Whoever takes it over holds the keys to all of them. If the move can't happen right away: take GMS off the internet and make it reachable only from an admin network.
[1] heise.de (Nov 5)

Plex2Shell: QNAP Advises a Reinstall

Not to be confused with PHP2Shell from October. This is about four flaws in Plex Media Server: CVE-2026-96652, -96654, -96655 and -96656. According to BleepingComputer, exploitation began "shortly after the full technical details became public on October 16". CISA has listed them in the KEV catalog since Oct 20.[1]

Now QNAP reports break-ins on NAS devices through this route. The fix is Plex 1.43.3.10861 or newer. QNAP advises treating an affected device as compromised and rebuilding the system cleanly. The source gives no CVSS score.[1]

Our take Plex on a NAS is the classic home-network and small-office setup: configured once, reachable from outside through a port forward, never touched again. An update is not enough if the device was already open. An attacker with a shell on the NAS sits right next to all your backups.
[1] bleepingcomputer.com (Nov 6)

D-Link DGS-3130: Zero-Days Without a Patch

In advisory SAP10523, D-Link warns of several zero-days in the DGS-3130 series of switches; the model named is the DGS-3130-30TS. The flaws affect the DDP protocol and the security architecture of the firmware. According to BleepingComputer: "D-Link didn't share details on these flaws and has yet to publicly disclose the CVE IDs."[1]

Until a patch arrives, D-Link recommends: turn off DDP, remove the default SNMP community strings, and restrict management access.[1]

SNMP community string: A kind of password that monitoring tools use to query values from a network device. Many devices ship with the default "public." It's like a mailbox whose key is the same for all the neighbors.
[1] bleepingcomputer.com (Nov 6)

OpenSSH 10.6p2: The 256-Character Detail

Update since yesterday The release notes speak of avoiding a "password authentication bypass that is exploitable through sshd(8)" "that affects some Linux systems when built and run with PAM support disabled." The cause lies outside sshd. The bug was reported by Özgün Kültekin of Wakefield.[1]

heise adds the mechanics: affected systems with musl-libc and without PAM don't check the password properly. "Instead, they accept any password longer than 256 characters." Alpine is affected. According to heise, other distributions were still shipping 10.6p1. The workaround remains PasswordAuthentication no.[2] We still have not found a CVE number or a CVSS score.

Our take No human types 256 characters, but every script does. That makes the bug easy for mass scans. Yesterday's rule applies twice over: SSH with keys only.
[1] openssh.org (Nov 5) · [2] heise.de (Nov 5, updated 22:44)

Xen: Three Use-After-Free Flaws in the Linux Backend

Use-after-free: A program frees memory and then keeps using it anyway. Like a hotel room that has already been given to the next guest, while the old guest still has the key.

The Xen team published three advisories on Nov 6. XSA-526 and XSA-527 affect xen-netback, XSA-528 affects xen-blkback. In all three cases a guest system can trigger a use-after-free in the Linux host (CVE-2026-100080, -100081, -100082). Two more advisories, XSA-529 and XSA-530, are announced for Nov 10 at 18:00 UTC, still under embargo.[1]

Seven stable kernels carry the fixes: 7.3.3, 6.18.62, 6.12.116, 6.6.162, 6.1.192, 5.15.225 and 5.10.274. According to LWN, each "only contains fixes for three use-after-free vulnerabilities." Shortly before, 7.3.2 and 6.18.61 came out.[2]

Our take A kernel release for just three fixes is a signal. It matters for anyone running Linux as a Xen host (dom0). Anyone who only runs guests in a cloud waits for the provider.
[1] xenbits.xen.org/xsa (Nov 6) · [2] lwn.net (Nov 6)

BleepingComputer reports on Nov 6 about fake security tools on GitHub that install backdoors.[1] We have not checked the details.

Our take The target group is cleverly chosen: whoever is looking for an exploit or scan tool often runs it with admin rights. A repo with lots of stars is no proof. Third-party tools belong in a throwaway VM, not on your work machine.

Linux: User Namespaces and File Capabilities

User namespace: A kernel feature that lets a process be "root" in its own small world without having real root on the system. Rootless Docker and Podman build on it. Like a child who is the boss in the nursery but not in the whole house.

On Nov 5, LWN describes a patch series by Josef Bacik. It aims to stop privileges from leaking out of a user namespace through file capabilities. LWN sums up the underlying problem this way: "In practice, keeping privilege contained within a user namespace has often turned out to be difficult."[1]

DockTail: Containers Straight into the Tailscale Network

Port mapping: Docker forwards a port on the host to a container, for example -p 8080:80. Like a doorman who takes mail for room 80 at main entrance 8080. Every forward is one more door you have to keep an eye on.

According to iX, the open-source tool DockTail automatically registers Docker containers on the Tailscale mesh VPN. Every container thereby becomes reachable on the private network without opening any ports on the host.[1] We have not checked versions or maturity.

Our take For homelabs and small servers this is exactly the right direction, and it fits the week. The Plex and NAS break-ins run through ports that someone opened for remote access. A service that only lives in the tailnet shows up in no mass scan.
[1] heise.de/developer (Nov 6)

What Saturday Is Worth

Three things for the weekend: bring SonicWall firewalls with SAML VPN to 7.3.4-7013 or 8.2.3-8015. Check whether any Plex server is exposed to the internet through a port forward, and rebuild it if in doubt. Retire PostgreSQL 14 by Thursday. The common thread remains: firewalls, NAS devices, switches and management consoles are the target. Fewer open ports, more private network.

It's Saturday, November seventh. The week ends with network hardware: firewalls, NAS devices and switches are front and center.

The biggest story comes from SonicWall. There's a flaw in its SSL VPN that lets attackers get around the login when it runs through SAML. In that case the second factor doesn't protect you. SonicWall has indications of attacks, and the US agency CISA added the flaw on Friday to its catalog of actively exploited vulnerabilities. The fixed versions are seven point three point four and eight point two point three. The severity score of seven point one has so far been reported only by BleepingComputer.

Also from SonicWall: its management system, GMS, has a critical flaw but won't get any more updates, because support has ended. The advice is to move to the successor product and, until then, take GMS off the internet.

On QNAP NAS devices, attackers are exploiting four flaws in Plex Media Server, known as Plex2Shell. QNAP says to treat affected devices as compromised and rebuild them. Separately, D-Link is warning about several zero-days in switches of the DGS thirty-one thirty series, with no patch available yet.

On yesterday's OpenSSH bug, heise has one more detail: on Alpine and other systems running musl without PAM, any password longer than two hundred fifty-six characters got through. The advice stands: turn off password login and use keys only.

In the Linux kernel, there were seven stable releases for three flaws in the Xen backend. Two more Xen advisories are expected Tuesday. Cisco has released patches for UCS Central and Unified Communications Manager, and no attacks are known there.

Some good news for your homelab: the open-source tool DockTail automatically hooks Docker containers into your Tailscale network, with no port forwarding at all. That fits the week, because the break-ins came through exactly those kinds of open ports.

On Thursday, support ends for PostgreSQL fourteen. Next week also brings Microsoft Patch Tuesday and the two new Xen advisories. We'll cover those in the next brief.
