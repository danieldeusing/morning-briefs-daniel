Patch week: what arrives on Tuesday

On Tuesday, Nov 10, the second Tuesday of November, Microsoft's Patch Tuesday lands.[1] Nobody knows in advance how big the round will be. For context: according to The Register, Microsoft set a record of 974 fixes in September, after 622 in July and 206 in June.[2] Depending on how they count, others report 964 (Tenable) to 966 (BleepingComputer).[4][5]

The same day at 18:00 UTC (3:00 p.m. in Brasília), the Xen advisories XSA-529 and XSA-530 go public. They appear on the calendar as “assigned, but embargoed”; there are no titles yet.[3]

Embargo: The flaw is known and the fix is ready, but the details stay secret until a set date. That gives vendors and distributions time to prepare their updates. Like a newspaper that already has the story but may not print it until midnight.
Our take Monday is the right day for an inventory: which Windows servers, which Xen hosts, which Postgres instances are running? Whoever knows that today only has to work through the list on Tuesday. Microsoft's record numbers come partly from AI-assisted bug hunting; that is no longer the exception but the new normal.

PostgreSQL 14: the end comes Thursday

The PostgreSQL versioning table lists the “final release” for version 14 on Nov 12, 2026. The current minor version is 14.24. According to the roadmap, Nov 12 is also the date of the next minor round; the one after that comes on Feb 11, 2027.[1][2] The rule: “a final minor version will be released and the software will then be unsupported (end-of-life).”[1]

End-of-support dates for the other lines, per the table: 15 until Nov 11, 2027; 16 until Nov 9, 2028; 17 until Nov 8, 2029; 18 until Nov 14, 2030. PostgreSQL 19 has been out as RC 1 since Oct 15.[1]

Our take If you are still on 14, install the last 14 release on Thursday and then plan the jump to 18. A jump to 15 alone buys just one year. The database keeps running after Thursday, but every future flaw stays open.

Torvalds: the AI bug wave may have peaked

With Linux 7.4-rc2, Linus Torvalds writes that the release is big. One reason: he had held back code from rc1, and rc1 was already the biggest RC in kernel history. The other reason, he says, is “various AI fixes.” He quotes Alexei Starovoitov, one of the BPF developers: “the fixes are now coming in faster than the new reports.” Starovoitov adds a caveat: “I think most other areas haven't reached that point yet.”[1]

eBPF: A mechanism that lets small, verified programs run directly inside the Linux kernel, for example for network filtering or monitoring. Like a power outlet in the engine room where you may plug in a vetted measuring device without rebuilding the machine.

The background is the wave of bug reports that AI tools have been producing since spring. The Register cites the Microsoft record and 1,449 fixes at Oracle in a single month.[1] The models themselves are covered in the ai-dev brief.

Our take One subsystem is not the kernel, and BPF is small and well maintained. Still, this is the first good news from this wave: if one team can work down the bug list, the problem is a matter of capacity, not a permanent condition. For operators that means the big patch rounds will stay for a while, but they will not keep growing forever.

Xen: seven stable kernels for three flaws

On Nov 6, Greg Kroah-Hartman released kernels 7.3.3, 6.18.62, 6.12.116, 6.6.162, 6.1.192, 5.15.225 and 5.10.274. According to LWN, each contains only the fixes for three use-after-free flaws in xen-netback and xen-blkback (XSA-526 through -528). Two allow a denial of service against the host; CVE-2026-100081 may allow privilege escalation.[1][2]

If you run Linux as a Xen host or in Xen guests, pick up these kernels. With XSA-529 and -530 on Tuesday, the next round is already in sight.

OpenSSH 10.6p2: how the bug works

The Nov 5 release notes describe a “password authentication bypass that is exploitable” in sshd, on systems built and run without PAM. It was reported by Özgün Kültekin.[1] The Register describes the mechanics: on Linux with musl libc, without PAM and with password login, sshd accepts any password longer than 256 characters for an account with “*” in the password field. According to The Register, the flaw triggers with passwords of 257 to 1,024 characters. The cause traces back to a change from 2016.[2]

musl libc: A slim standard library for C programs that Alpine Linux uses instead of the usual glibc. It is the reason Alpine containers are so small. Like a toolbox with fewer, lighter tools that sometimes behave a little differently.

heise confirms: “Instead, they accept any password longer than 256 characters.” Packages are available for Alpine.[3] OpenSSH developer Damien Miller advises, per LWN, “set PasswordAuthentication=no or disable SSH for these systems IMMEDIATELY.”[4] The flaw is tracked as CVE-2026-123158 (published Nov 5), CVSS 8.1. MITRE set the score as the CNA; NVD has not scored it itself. It is not in the CISA KEV catalog.[5]

Heads up Mainly affected are those who use Alpine images with sshd and password login, for example as a jump host or in old Docker images. Check: sshd -T | grep passwordauthentication. If it says yes, upgrade to 10.6p2 or turn off password login.

Edge devices: where things stand on Monday

There has been nothing new on the boxes at the network edge since Friday. Here is the status, with deadlines.

SonicWall SSL-VPN

CVE-2026-19868 allows bypassing the SAML checks in the SSL-VPN, including MFA enforced via SAML. The attacker needs valid credentials for an alternate authentication method. CISA has listed it in the KEV catalog since Nov 6; the deadline for US federal agencies is Nov 20.[1] According to BleepingComputer: CVSS 7.1; fixes in Gen7 7.3.4-7013 and Gen8 8.2.3-8015; advisory SNWLID-2026-0014; no workarounds. The update closes seven flaws in total, including two in the management interface rated 8.8.[2]

KEV catalog: CISA's list of flaws that are demonstrably being attacked. US agencies must close them by a deadline. Like a list of the locks burglars are actually picking right now, not just in theory.

SonicWall GMS

GMS up to 9.5.2 has a critical unauthenticated file-read flaw (CVE-2026-109593) and an SQL injection (CVE-2026-109594, medium). No update is coming; SonicWall recommends moving to NSM.[3]

Zyxel GS1900

Zyxel is observing attacks on GS1900-series switches. The flaw is now tracked as CVE-2026-107441, CVSS 8.8. An attacker on the LAN needs no login, only a crafted HTTP request. Fixes exist only for models still in support, such as the GS1900-24 with 2.90(AAHL.3)C0.[4]

Plex2Shell

Four flaws in Plex Media Server (CVE-2026-96652, -96654, -96655, -96656), in the KEV catalog since Oct 20. QNAP reports break-ins on NAS devices via port 32400. The fix is 1.43.3.10861 or newer.[5]

Heads up We have not checked any of these CVSS figures against the NVD; the NVD pages returned no data. The values come from BleepingComputer and heise. The KEV entries and deadlines come directly from the CISA feed.

Attacks on refrigeration systems: now intelligence services too

FBI and CISA warn of attacks on refrigeration and cold-chain controllers. According to BleepingComputer (Nov 5), attackers exploit unpatched or internet-exposed devices, login flaws and weak configurations. In October an estimated 250 to 350 French supermarkets were affected; in November, more than 115 stores in six European countries, with some systems heating up to 50 °C. The French blood service EFS reported altered readings in three storage sites.[1]

heise reports on Nov 7 that German intelligence services have been brought in after the sabotage cases.[2] The recommendations are the familiar ones: take controllers off the internet, change default passwords, separate OT from the office network, set safe temperature limits in fixed form.[1]

OT (Operational Technology): The computers that control physical things, such as refrigeration units, pumps or conveyor belts. As opposed to IT, which processes data. Like the difference between the checkout system and the thermostat in the cold room.
Our take This is the same pattern as with firewalls, only with consequences you can smell. A thermostat on the internet is an edge device. If you build or connect controllers for customers, set the limits in the hardware, not only in software.

Certificates: eMudhra and DigiCert

eMudhra: macOS needs until December

According to heise, Apple will close the last eMudhra leak only with macOS 27.3, which so far exists only as a developer beta. 27.2 had no fix, and the release comes “in December at the earliest.” The keys of the affected CA certificates “in part consisted of just a single number.” Important for developers: command-line tools such as git, curl, python3 and openssl use the outdated file /etc/ssl/cert.pem.[1] JetBrains has fixed it in 2025.3.3, 2025.2.5 and 2025.1.5; on the two older lines you must also turn off JCEF via ide.browser.jcef.enabled. The current lines 2026.1.2 and 2026.2 RC2 close the flaw too.[2]

CA certificate: A digital ID of an authority that browsers and tools trust. Whoever holds its key can issue forged website IDs. Like a registry-office stamp that has fallen into the wrong hands.

DigiCert: 16 months exposed

DigiCert admits that an internal system stood open for over 16 months, since February 2025. It was discovered on Jun 18, 2026; access to archived records between Jun 27 and Jul 2 has been confirmed since Oct 19. A “small proportion” of the archive is affected; DigiCert does not say how many customers. CA operations, CertCentral and DigiCert ONE are said to be unaffected.[3]

Our take On the Mac, do not wait for Apple: if you use curl or git against internal services, check your own CA file or use the one from Homebrew. With DigiCert, the time span is the problem, not the scale: four months between discovery and disclosure is too long for a CA.
[1] heise.de (Nov 6) · [2] heise.de (Nov 7) · [3] theregister.com (Nov 6)

Truffle Security wants leaked keys revoked

Truffle Security is launching a free “Secrets Revocation Program.” It uses TruffleHog to check whether leaked keys are still valid and passes them to the issuer. The issuer revokes them via an endpoint or within 14 days of a webhook. The target is around 1.4 million publicly findable, valid secrets. 16 issuers are taking part, including Hugging Face, Notion, Vercel, RubyGems and Twilio.[1]

The number that sticks: “81% of the keys we reported last year still worked six months later.” The researchers also found 2,655 valid keys in configuration files of AI coding tools since January 2025.[1]

Our take Good that someone is tackling the problem from the issuer's side: notifying the owner clearly was not enough. For your own repos this means: a key that was ever public gets rotated, not just deleted from the Git history.
[1] bleepingcomputer.com (Nov 5)

More news

Fake security tools on GitHub

According to Dr.Web, over 2,000 Windows systems have been compromised through repos posing as security tools, such as Fscan. They deliver AsyncRAT, a clipper and infostealers.[1] Foreign tools belong in a throwaway VM.

Data leaks: Iris Ohyama, Chainalysis, Arkéa

At the Japanese manufacturer Iris Ohyama, data of around 7.7 million customers may be exposed via a service provider's cloud (Nov 8).[2] At Chainalysis, a break-in exposed credentials of all customers.[3] The Crédit Mutuel Arkéa case (up to $133 million via a service provider) was in yesterday's issue.[4] Three cases, one pattern: the route through the partner.

Apple: iCloud links and Lockdown Mode

Apple plans to scan public iCloud sharing links for “harmful content,” The Register reports.[5] And according to heise, Calif.io has bypassed iOS Lockdown Mode; the exploit survives an update.[6]

Veeam on AMD EPYC

Veeam warns that Software Appliance VMs fail to boot on hosts with AMD EPYC.[7] If you run backups this way, check the vendor's notes before the next update.

ITU: internet governance under stress test

At the ITU Plenipotentiary Conference, which starts today, Monday Nov 9, 278 proposals are on the table, according to The Register.[8] The topic is slow, but it helps decide who will regulate protocols and addresses in future.

It's Monday, November ninth. The weekend was quiet; almost nothing new has reached the trade press since Saturday. So today's issue looks at the week ahead.

The good news of the day comes from Linus Torvalds. Other areas aren't there yet. We read that as a first sign the wave is manageable.

On edge devices, Friday's picture holds: the SonicWall flaw in the VPN login, with a deadline for US agencies on November twentieth; the SonicWall management system with no patch; attacked Zyxel switches; and the Plex attacks on QNAP devices.
