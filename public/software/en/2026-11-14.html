Check Point: from the PHP flaw to root and to the wiper

On Thursday, Check Point released emergency hotfixes for CVE-2026-123039. The flaw sits in the IPC interface DynamicCLI and allows local privilege escalation on the firewall, Security Gateway, Scalable Platforms and the management servers. "Check Point observed an active exploit of this vulnerability against a couple of dozen customers", writes BleepingComputer; it has been exploited since late October.[1] The article gives no CVSS score, only "high-severity".

The full chain: through PHP2Shell, two critical PHP flaws (CVE-2026-109618, CVE-2026-109619), attackers get into the gateway's Mobile Access portal without authentication. With CVE-2026-123039 they become root there. Then they deploy a wiper called "destroy_v6" on VMware ESXi hosts, which deletes data recursively.[2] The attacks began on Oct 30 against Israeli organizations; a second wave on Nov 2 hit companies in several countries. The PHP patches have been available since Oct 21; according to Amazon, the attack attempts began four days earlier.[2]

Local privilege escalation: The attacker is already on the device, but only as an ordinary user. The flaw turns them into the administrator (root). Like a visitor who is already standing in the lobby and then finds a master key.
Wiper: Malware that does not encrypt data and does not want ransom, but only destroys. Here with a note that explicitly rejects negotiations.[2] Like a burglar who takes nothing but sets everything on fire.
Heads up Check Point describes the hotfixes in SK1000362; the November Jumbo Hotfix covers PHP2Shell and CVE-2026-123039. Anyone who cannot patch immediately should, according to the vendor, stop the dynamic_server process and prevent it from restarting.[1][2] CISA added the flaw to the KEV on Nov 13, deadline Nov 16.[3]
Our take The pattern is the same as with Barracuda and Citrix: the security appliance at the edge is the way in, and from there it goes on to virtualization. Anyone running a Check Point box should also check the ESXi behind it and whether there are backups the wiper cannot reach.

GitHub "wfx-loot": swapping secrets is not enough

Update since Fri The key figures from yesterday stand: according to Aikido, the attacker used 4,837 stolen GitHub accounts to test 61,764 repositories for write access, and stole secrets from "at least 1,835 targets".[1] heise adds on Nov 13: "Workflows are said to have already executed the malicious code in more than 1,800 cases." The firm SafeDep found malicious-code commits "in over 8,000 public repositories".[2] We found the SafeDep figure only at heise.

What is new is mainly the teaser: "Through hijacked GitHub accounts, attackers set up backdoors at AWS and Azure."[2] According to heise, this is not a flaw in GitHub Actions but abuse of the write permissions of stolen tokens. Fly.io says tokens of its GitHub app leaked through a publicly reachable .git/config. There is still no statement from GitHub.[2]

Heads up Traces according to heise: branch wfx-docs, PR "docs: dependency audit notes", commit "ci: refresh build cache". "Affected users should revoke the tokens, OAuth grants and SSH keys of their account." In the cloud, remove AWS roles that trust all accounts and Azure federated credentials with the prefix wfx-ci-.[2]
Federated credential: A rule in the cloud that says: "Whoever shows up with this outside ID, e.g. a GitHub workflow, may come in." If an attacker creates such a rule, they get back in even after a password change. Like a spare key the burglar left with the caretaker.
Our take This is the most important point of the week for us: anyone who only rotates the GitHub secrets leaves the door open in AWS or Azure. Ten minutes this weekend belong to the list of trust rules in the cloud, not just the repo settings.

Quantum Systems: the update was the weapon

The German drone maker Quantum Systems reports an attack on its ground and mission-planning software QBase. According to Spiegel and the Financial Times, cited by BleepingComputer, the attack came "via a malicious QBase update file" through the update server.[1] It was discovered in late October. According to reports, the Bundeswehr kept the drones on the ground as a precaution and pulled the systems from an exercise. How many customers installed the update is unclear; the company names no culprit.[1]

Attack through the update channel: Customers trust updates from their vendor and install them automatically. Whoever takes over the update server delivers malicious code under the vendor's name. Like a forged letter inside the bank's genuine envelope.
Our take For software vendors this is the real lesson, even without the military angle: sign updates, keep the signing key separate from the update server, and verify the signature on the client. Then a compromised server alone is not enough.

Apache httpd 2.4.71: Apache confirms the range

Update since Fri The project's security page now provides the facts on CVE-2026-119618: severity "critical", a faulty synchronization in assign_headers() of mod_http2 that can lead to code execution. "This issue affects Apache HTTP Server: from 2.4.55 through 2.4.70", plus mod_h2 from GitHub in 2.0.8 through 2.0.42.[1] The flaw was reported by Anthropic (Oct 26) and Google (Nov 3); 2.4.71 came out on Nov 11. There is no CVSS score.

2.4.71 fixes two more flaws: a crash in mod_md (CVE-2026-106023, "important", from 2.4.30) and a one-byte overflow in mod_vhost_alias (CVE-2026-109163, "low").[1] heise rates an exploit as "possible, but difficult". Only servers with HTTP/2 enabled are affected; Protocols http/1.1 helps as a stopgap.[2]

Race condition: Two parts of a program access the same data at the same time, and the result depends on who gets there first. Like two people filling out the same form at once: in the end it contains parts of both.
Our take Behind Caddy this does not affect us. But anyone who has Apache as a reverse proxy or in a Docker image should check the version in the image, not on the host. Distribution packages often lag a few days behind.

Citrix NetScaler: 28 flaws, no attacks

Update since Fri Only a brief note yesterday, now with numbers: Citrix is closing 28 flaws, 9 of them critical, in NetScaler ADC and Gateway. The most severe, CVE-2026-107412, has CVSS 9.5 and affects devices configured as a gateway or AAA server.[1] Fixed in 14.1-73.57, 13.1-64.39, 14.1-FIPS 14.1-73.58 and 13.1-FIPS/NDcPP 13.1-37.293. "So far Citrix has no reports that attackers are already exploiting the flaws."[1]

Our take NetScaler flaws of class 9+ have often been exploited within a few days in recent years. "No attacks known" is a time window for this product, not a state.

KEV: what was added this week

On Nov 13, CISA added two flaws, both with deadline Nov 16: Check Point CVE-2026-123039 (see above) and UnrealIRCd CVE-2026-114713, a faulty handling of delimiters.[1] The entries from Nov 10 have deadline Nov 24: the Outlook flaw CVE-2026-124194, Chromium CVE-2026-125350 (use-after-free), Cisco BroadWorks CVE-2026-20320 (XXE) and Openwave Webtop CVE-2026-111793. The deadline for Barracuda ESG expired yesterday.[1]

XXE (XML External Entity): An XML parser follows references in the document to external files or addresses. An attacker sends XML that points to /etc/passwd and gets the file back. Like a form with the field "please attach the document from the filing cabinet" that someone obediently fills in.

Further KEV entries from Nov 9 (each due Nov 12, so the deadline has already passed), per the CISA catalog: GPUStack CVE-2026-102895 (improper authentication), OpenBSD OpenSSH CVE-2026-123158 (unexpected status code or return value), SignalWire FreeSwitch CVE-2026-116779 (code injection) and IBM Aspera Faspex CVE-2026-123218 (incorrect authorization). If you run OpenSSH, check that entry first.

FaceStealer: North Korea wants the repos

The US Department of Justice has presented and disrupted an attack scheme it calls "FaceStealer" and attributes to North Korea. According to heise, the perpetrators are said to have stolen over $400 million in crypto; over 900 records of victims were seized. The new part is the target: open-source developers. "The aim is said to be to gain access to their repositories through them."[1]

Our take Together with "wfx-loot", this gives a clear picture: a maintainer's account is worth more today than a server. Hardware key instead of SMS, and no long-lived tokens in the home directory.
[1] heise.de (Nov 13)

Trezor: implant between circuit board and display

Trezor confirms "malicious hardware implants designed to steal wallet recovery phrases" in three Safe-5 hardware wallets. An extra circuit board reads the recovery words off the display and sends them encrypted over cellular to a server.[1] The devices came from the reseller CryptoBilis in Southeast Asia, bought between Aug 20 and Sep 23, 2026. Firmware 2.13.1 from Nov 11 blocks the implants found, but does not protect words that have already been siphoned off.[1]

.NET 11: short support, and .NET 8 is out

Microsoft released .NET 11.0 on Nov 10, with C# 15. It is an STS release with 24 months of support until November 2028.[1] Important for operations: "Support for .NET 9.0 and 8.0 ended at the same time as the release of .NET 11.0." .NET 10 also runs until November 2028.[1] Technically, .NET MAUI apps for Android, iOS and Mac Catalyst now run on CoreCLR instead of Mono, and several SDK tools start faster thanks to Native AOT. The SDK also ships a project template for MCP servers; details on that belong in the ai-dev brief.

LTS and STS: LTS (Long-Term Support) gets long security updates, STS (Standard-Term Support) shorter ones. Since the switch to 24 months of STS, .NET 10 (LTS) and .NET 11 (STS) end in the same month. Like two leases with different start dates that expire on the same day.
Our take Anyone who still has services on .NET 8 gets no more security fixes from now on. The jump goes to .NET 10, not 11: same end of support, but a year more maturity.
[1] heise.de (Nov 11)

libghostty 1.0: one terminal core for everyone

The emulation core of the Ghostty terminal has been released as libghostty-vt 1.0 with a stable C ABI, under the MIT license. It handles control sequences, cursor and scrollback; drawing is left to the embedding application.[1] JetBrains IDEs use it as the default terminal from 2026.3, the new KDE terminal Kwi builds on it, and Neovim has accepted a PR to move away from libvterm. Bindings exist for Rust, Go, Python and JavaScript.[1]

Stable C ABI: A binary interface that does not change between versions. Programs can swap the library without being recompiled, and distributions can ship it as its own package. Like a power-outlet standard: every device fits, whichever the manufacturer.

What Saturday is worth

Ten minutes of cloud instead of GitHub: in AWS, look for roles that trust all accounts, and in Azure for federated credentials with wfx-ci-. If you have Apache in an image, rebuild it with 2.4.71. And if you still run .NET 8, plan the jump to 10.

It's Saturday, November fourteenth. Two stories today show where attackers are focusing: the firewall itself, and the update channel.

Check Point is confirming an exploited flaw in its gateways. According to BleepingComputer, attackers get into the portal through two PHP flaws, become root with the new flaw, and then wipe VMware servers with a wiper. A couple of dozen customers are affected. The U.S. agency CISA has set a deadline of Monday.

In the GitHub campaign involving nearly five thousand stolen accounts, heise reports more than eighteen hundred workflow runs with malicious code. More important is a second finding: the attackers are setting up backdoors in AWS and Azure. If you only swap the secrets, that door stays open.

At the German drone maker Quantum Systems, an attack came through a tampered update to its planning software. According to reports, Bundeswehr drones were kept on the ground as a precaution.

Apache confirms the critical flaw in the HTTP/2 module for versions two point four point fifty-five through seventy. Citrix is closing twenty-eight flaws in NetScaler, nine of them critical, and no attacks are known yet.

On the release front, dot-NET eleven arrived, with twenty-four months of support. At the same time, support ended for dot-NET eight and nine. Ghostty's terminal core has been released as version one and will soon be in JetBrains, KDE and Neovim.

Europol and the BKA took down two hundred thirty-five servers from malware networks. And according to the FBI, a North Korean scheme is now going after open-source developers, to get to their repositories.

Our advice for the weekend: spend ten minutes checking the trust rules in your cloud. Next week we'll look at Monday's Check Point deadline and at PostgreSQL nineteen on Thursday.
