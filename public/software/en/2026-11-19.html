Codeberg: domain gone, platform gone

On Wednesday at 09:13, Codeberg reported on Mastodon that its website and repositories were unreachable. The cause was not its own servers but the domain codeberg.org. According to Codeberg, it was "neither expired nor transferred" but had vanished from the account at the US registrar NameSilo and been set to the status clientHold.[1] NameSilo later said it had locked the domain and that "the case is now with the abuse team".[1] At 12:23, Codeberg reported that the domain was slowly reachable again.[1]

clientHold: A status that the registrar (the vendor you rent a domain from) can set. The domain still belongs to the customer but disappears from DNS, the phone book of the internet. It is like a house whose number was unscrewed overnight: the house is still there, but no delivery driver can find it.

According to heise, there is no official reason. Two theories are circulating: a complaint by a company about a repository on Codeberg, or a political motive, because Codeberg had classified DDoS attacks and spam in 2025 as a campaign by far-right actors.[1] At the same time, Reddit banned the subreddit /r/codeberg. Codeberg has given up a fallback domain codeberg.eu; a second domain cannot be activated within a few hours.[1] According to users, the servers remained reachable by IP address. One side effect: a Forgejo security update planned for Wednesday was delayed.[1]

Our take This is the lesson of the day, and it applies beyond Codeberg. A German nonprofit, founded as an alternative to US platforms, depends on a US registrar that pulls the plug without warning. For your own setup, that means: if you have critical domains, know which registrar holds them, whether the registrar lock and contact details are current, and how long a move would take. And anyone who clones straight from codeberg.org in CI had red builds yesterday. A mirror costs little.
[1] heise.de (Nov 18, 11:48, updated 12:23)

Java4Shell: eleven flaws, not one

Update since Wed Yesterday the CPU condition was in the foreground. heise classifies the Oracle update of Nov 17 differently: "There are eleven updates at once against remote code execution in Java."[1] Besides Java4Shell (CVE-2026-114938), Oracle closes nine more OpenJDK flaws (CVE-2026-114939 through -114947) and one in OpenJFX (CVE-2026-86144).[1] Affected are Java 17, 21, 25 and 27 "up to and including 17.0.21.0.3, 21.0.13.0.3, 25.0.5.0.3 and 27.0.1.0.3 respectively".[1] According to heise, no attacks are known so far.

According to heise, Java4Shell itself affects only certain CPUs: Intel Xeon from Ice Lake on and AMD EPYC and Ryzen from Zen 4 on.[2] Calif clarifies that Java clients are "also affected only if they run on one of the processors mentioned above".[2] Calif also describes a second exploit in which a malicious server takes over a Java program that connects to it via TLS. According to Calif, code execution without login works in Keycloak, Jira, Confluence, JFrog Artifactory and Jenkins; there is no public PoC.[2]

Our take That ends the discussion "our CPUs have no AVX-512 VBMI, so we wait". heise says it plainly: the updates should be "applied immediately, even on systems with unaffected CPUs".[2] Yesterday's CPU check remains useful for setting the order, but not for skipping systems.

KEV: four new entries, three deadlines on Saturday

On Nov 18, CISA added four flaws to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog.[1] For three of them, US federal agencies already face a deadline of Saturday, Nov 21. That is an unusually short window of three days.

Apache Axis 1: no patch in sight

CVE-2026-90436 affects Apache Axis.[1] According to Mend, OpenCVE and Debian, the flaw allows unauthenticated class instantiation (CWE-470) in all Axis 1 versions up to 1.4. NVD has no score yet; Mend lists CVSS 4.0 at 9.2. Published on Nov 6. Axis 1 is end of life and no fix is planned. We could not load the NVD page itself; score and details are therefore not checked against the primary source.

REDCap, Synology, LiteLLM

CVE-2026-90817 affects REDCap, the Vanderbilt University platform for research data that many clinics and universities run; deadline also Nov 21.[1] CVE-2024-10441 in Synology DiskStation Manager (DSM) is a flaw from 2024 that is only now considered exploited; we found no details on the exploitation.[1] And CVE-2026-40217 affects the AI proxy LiteLLM, deadline Dec 2.[1] Some secondary sources give a different CVE number; we follow the catalog. More on LiteLLM belongs in the ai-dev brief.

Our take Axis 1 is the interesting case for Java people. Nobody builds with Axis 1 today, but in old enterprise applications it often sits as a transitive dependency for a single SOAP partner. A mvn dependency:tree | grep axis across the old repos takes five minutes. Since there is no patch, the fix is: isolate the endpoint or rebuild on Axis2/CXF. And if you run a Synology NAS: check the DSM version and keep the web interface off the internet.

Android: Play demands justifications [1]

Google is tightening the rules in the Play Console in four stages. "The start is January 27, 2027": apps targeting Android 17 (API 37+) are to use the new location button for one-time location requests. Anyone who wants precise location (ACCESS_FINE_LOCATION) must explain in the Play Console why approximate location is not enough. From the same date, apps without a real need are to use the protected contact picker; from Android 18, intents replace the WRITE_CONTACTS permission. On Apr 14, 2027, rules for the AppFunctions API (functions for AI agents) arrive, and on Aug 25, 2027, a duty to explain ACCESS_LOCAL_NETWORK.

Our take For web people with a Capacitor or React Native app, this is the most important item of the day. The explanation in the Play Console is quickly written. The rework to the location button and the contact picker is real work and belongs in the Q1 backlog, not in the last week of January.

PostgreSQL 19: due today

According to postgresql.org: "The planned date for the general availability of PostgreSQL 19 is November 19, 2026."[1] That holds only if no further release candidate is needed. This morning the news archive still showed no GA announcement, only RC 2 from Nov 12.[2]

The minor releases of Nov 12 (18.7, 17.12, 16.16, 15.20, 14.25) "fix 52 security vulnerabilities and over 140 bugs".[1] The most severe is CVE-2026-6460: a type confusion after ALTER ... SET DATA TYPE executes arbitrary code, CVSS 8.8.[1] Anyone using pg_trgm should rebuild GiST indexes with gist_trgm_ops. And 14.25 is the last release of PostgreSQL 14, which gets no more fixes from now on.[1]

Our take Minor update first, then think about 19. A major release on GA day is not a good idea for production data; waiting for 19.1 is the usual, boring and right choice. If you are still on 14, you now have a real problem: the jump to 18 goes via pg_upgrade, and the note in the release notes about indexes with full-text search, pg_trgm and fuzzystrmatch belongs in the plan.

What Thursday is worth

Three tasks: schedule the Java update on all machines, regardless of CPU. Search the old Java repos for Axis 1. And half an hour for your own domains: which registrar holds them, is the lock active, and which builds depend on third-party Git hosts without a mirror?

It's Thursday, November nineteenth. Today's lead isn't a code bug, it's a domain.

Codeberg, the Berlin-based nonprofit GitHub alternative, was down for several hours on Wednesday morning. According to heise, the American registrar NameSilo had locked the domain. It hadn't expired and hadn't been transferred, it simply disappeared from DNS. There's no official reason. Around midday the domain slowly came back. Our take: a domain is a single point of failure. If you have critical domains, know who holds them, and if your builds clone straight from Codeberg, you need a mirror.

There's an important detail on Java4Shell. According to heise, Oracle's update from Tuesday closes eleven flaws that allow code execution, not just the one. So it belongs on machines whose processors aren't affected by Java4Shell itself, too. No attacks are known yet.

The U.S. agency CISA added four new entries to its catalog of actively exploited vulnerabilities. One is Apache Axis, an old SOAP library for Java. According to search results, there's no patch for it anymore because the project has been discontinued. The others are the research platform REDCap, Synology's NAS software DSM, and the AI proxy LiteLLM. For the first three, the deadline for U.S. agencies runs out on Saturday.

For Android developers, Google is announcing new Play rules. Starting January twenty-seventh, twenty twenty-seven, apps have to justify why they need precise location. Rules for contacts and for access to the local network follow later.

PostgreSQL nineteen is due today, as long as no further release candidate is needed. More important for operations are last week's updates: they close fifty-two security flaws, the worst scoring eight point eight out of ten. PostgreSQL fourteen gets no more fixes from now on. Spring Boot four point two, by contrast, hadn't been announced as a final release as of this morning.

There's nothing new from the EU today on the CRA or the AI Act.
